# 05 — Departments & Crew

*Every job on a film, who they report to, and what they actually decide.* This
is the reference doc for "who does that?" — the roster, the org chart, the
hiring chain, and the on-screen consequence of each role.

> **Domain reference.** Real industry, not this game. See `README.md`.

**How to read this document.** Each department gets a **roster table** (the
deep bench: who reports to whom, what they own) followed by **profiles** of the
department heads. Every profile answers the same four questions in the same
order, matching the library convention in `README.md`:

> **Who** they answer to and command · **When** they join and leave · **What
> separates good from mediocre** (the skill axis) · **How failure shows** on
> screen or in the schedule.

The skill axis is the part worth reading twice. Most crew documentation stops
at job descriptions, which tells you what the job *is* but not what makes one
person worth three times another in the same chair.

## 1. The hiring chain

```
Studio / financiers
   └── Producer(s)
        ├── Director  ──────────────► picks the creative HODs with the producer
        ├── Line Producer / UPM ────► builds budget & schedule, hires the
        │                              logistical departments, approves all crew
        └── 1st AD ─────────────────► runs the shooting day
                                       (hired jointly by director & UPM)

Each Head of Department then hires their own crew, within the budget the
UPM approves.
```

Two chains of authority run in parallel on set and must stay aligned:

- **Creative**: Director → HODs → their crews.
- **Logistical**: Producer → UPM → 1st AD → the floor.

The 1st AD sits at the junction and is the person who converts creative intent
into a timetable. When those two chains disagree, the film loses days.

### 1.1 The order of hire

Crew is hired in a strict dependency order, because each hire's first job is
to tell the production what the *next* hires need to be. A rough studio-feature
timeline, counted backwards from day one of photography:

| When | Who is hired | Why then |
|---|---|---|
| Development / financing | Director, then producer's key producing staff | The package (`03-financing-and-dealmaking.md` §2) |
| −16 to −20 weeks | Line producer / UPM | Nothing can be budgeted or scheduled without them |
| −12 to −16 weeks | Production designer, 1st AD, location manager | The three longest-lead functions: build, schedule, and where |
| −10 to −14 weeks | DP, costume designer, VFX supervisor | Look decisions gate camera tests, fittings, and methodology |
| −8 to −12 weeks | Art director, construction coordinator, set decorator, POC, accountant, stunt coordinator | Build and office start; money starts flowing daily |
| −6 to −8 weeks | Gaffer, key grip, script supervisor, SFX supervisor, makeup and hair heads, transport coordinator | The tech scout and the production meeting need them in the room |
| −4 to −6 weeks | Camera crew, costume supervisor, prop master's team, editor | Kit is ordered, fittings run, cutting room built |
| −2 to −3 weeks | Sound department, 2nd AD, most of the floor crew, on-set dressers | The cheapest people to start late; nothing depends on them earlier |
| −1 week | Set PAs, additional grips/electrics, background crew | Volume hires, replaceable, dailies-rate |

Two rules fall out of this ordering:

1. **A late HOD hire compresses everything downstream.** A production designer
   hired four weeks late does not lose four weeks of design — it loses the
   construction weeks at the *other* end, because day one of photography does
   not move.
2. **Departments with lead time hire first and leave last.** Art and
   construction bracket the shoot; sound and the floor AD team are almost
   exactly coincident with it.

### 1.2 How hiring actually works, and the politics of it

The org chart says the director picks creative HODs and the UPM approves the
budget. What actually happens is a negotiation between four lists:

- **The director's list.** Directors work with the same people repeatedly —
  a DP, an editor, a PD, a 1st AD they trust. The list is short (often one
  name deep per role) and emotionally non-negotiable early on.
- **The producer's list.** Producers know who *delivers*: who comes in on
  budget, who is calm at 2am on night eleven, who has never blown a day.
  Producers care less about the reel and more about the reference call.
- **The studio's list.** On studio films, a handful of positions — DP,
  production designer, editor, VFX supervisor, and always the UPM and 1st AD —
  are subject to **studio approval**. This is rarely used to impose someone,
  and frequently used to veto: a first-time HOD on a $150M film is a bond and
  insurance conversation as much as a creative one.
- **The available list.** The binding constraint. Good HODs are booked six to
  twelve months ahead. A film whose start date slips six weeks can lose its DP,
  its PD, and its 1st AD in a single afternoon, and each of those departures
  takes the crew that would have followed them.

The recurring dynamics:

- **HODs bring teams.** Hiring a gaffer effectively hires their best boy and
  usually two or three sparks; hiring a DP often carries an operator, a 1st AC,
  and a preference for a particular gaffer and key grip. This is why a single
  HOD change reorganises a whole department, and why producers treat an HOD's
  team as part of what they are buying — a crew that has worked together for
  ten films is measurably faster than an assembled one.
- **The UPM's veto is budgetary, not creative.** A UPM cannot tell a director
  which DP to hire; they can say the film cannot afford that DP's package (rate
  plus the operator plus the camera order that DP always shoots). In practice
  this is the same thing, and both parties know it.
- **Incentives choose the crew.** A production shooting for a rebate must hire
  a specified proportion locally (`03-financing-and-dealmaking.md` §7). That
  makes "local hire" a job qualification independent of skill, creates
  fly-in/fly-out arrangements where the HOD travels and the crew is local, and
  is the single biggest reason a director's regular team may not be available
  to them on a given film.
- **Union jurisdiction constrains who can do what.** In the US, IATSE covers
  most below-the-line crafts, the Teamsters cover transport and (in most
  locals) location managers, and the DGA covers the director, UPM, and ADs. A
  signatory production hires from those rosters, at those rates, under those
  hours rules. Non-union and lower-budget agreements loosen this and are the
  route by which most crew enter the business.
- **Nepotism, in the technical sense, is structural.** Crew hiring runs almost
  entirely on personal referral. This produces reliable crews and a closed
  business simultaneously, which is why formal trainee and roster schemes exist
  and why they matter more than they look (`12-talent-labor-and-careers.md` §8).

### 1.3 The deal memo

Below-the-line hiring is papered with a **deal memo** — one or two pages,
often issued by the POC and countersigned by the UPM. What it fixes:

| Term | What it means | Why it is fought over |
|---|---|---|
| **Rate** | Daily or weekly, and on what hour basis | Sets everything else, including precedent for the next job |
| **Guaranteed hours** | E.g. "10 hours, then 1.5x; 12 then 2x" | Determines the real cost of a long day |
| **Guarantee / show length** | Weeks guaranteed regardless of schedule | Protects against a shoot collapsing under them |
| **Prep and wrap days** | Days before and after photography, often at a lower rate | Departments live or die on whether prep is paid |
| **Kit / box rental** | Rental of the crew member's own equipment | Effectively untaxed additional income; a real part of the offer |
| **Travel, per diem, housing** | For non-local hires | Can be worth more than a rate bump |
| **Credit** | Screen credit, and on what card | See §18; matters for the next job, not this one |
| **Exclusivity** | Whether they can take other work in gaps | Rarely enforced below HOD level |

### 1.4 Firing and replacement

Crew are replaced far more often than the credits suggest, and the mechanics
matter:

- **Below HOD level**, replacement is quick and quiet: the HOD tells the best
  boy or supervisor, the POC issues a new deal memo, and the call sheet changes
  overnight.
- **At HOD level**, replacement mid-shoot is a crisis. It costs a day at
  minimum (the new HOD must be walked through the film), it usually costs part
  of the department, which is loyal to the departed head, and it signals to the
  crew and to the completion bond that something is wrong.
- **Replacing a director** is the extreme case: DGA rules, credit consequences,
  and reshoot costs make it rare and expensive, and it usually means the studio
  has decided the film is unreleasable as being made.
- **The bond company can force changes.** If a completion-bonded film goes far
  enough over, the bond can take over the production and replace the UPM, the
  1st AD, or the director (`03-…` §8).

## 2. Crew size

| Production | Approx. total crew on the floor | Total on the crew list | Shooting days |
|---|---|---|---|
| Micro-budget indie | 10–25 | 20–40 | 12–20 |
| Independent feature | 40–80 | 80–150 | 20–30 |
| Studio mid-budget | 100–200 | 250–400 | 35–55 |
| Tentpole (main unit) | 250–500+ | 800–2,000+, counting VFX vendors and second units | 70–120+, plus units |

"Crew" in the credits (hundreds to thousands on a VFX film) is not the same as
crew on the floor on a given day.

### 2.1 Where the crew actually is

On any shooting day a production's people are in four places at once, and
confusing them is the most common error in estimating a crew:

```
  AHEAD              ON SET              AT BASE            OFF-SITE
  ─────────────────  ──────────────────  ─────────────────  ────────────────
  Rigging electric   Camera, grip,       2nd AD, cast in    Cutting room
  & grip pre-rig on  electric, sound,    makeup/hair/       (editor + AEs)
  tomorrow's         standby art/props/  costume, catering,
  location           costume/makeup,     accounts trailer,  Construction
                     script supervisor,  transport, extras  (next set)
  Construction on    1st AD + set PAs,   holding, medic,
  the set that       director, producer, unit publicist     Production office
  shoots in 3 weeks  video village                          (POC, APOC,
                                                            accountant)
  Set dec dressing
  the next set                                              VFX vendors
```

Rough proportions on a studio mid-budget day with a 160-name crew list: about
**80–100 on the floor**, **30–40 at unit base**, **20–30 prepping ahead or
striking behind**, and **10–20 off-site** in the office and cutting room.

### 2.2 The shape of a call sheet's crew list

A studio mid-budget call sheet lists the crew department by department, in a
conventional order. Directional headcounts for a single-camera day:

| Department | On the call sheet | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Production / AD | 10–16 | Producers, UPM, 1st/2nd/2nd-2nd AD, POC, accountant, 4–6 PAs |
| Camera | 6–12 | Doubles per additional camera; +DIT, +loader, +stills |
| Grip | 5–9 | Key, best boy, dolly grip, 2–5 company grips |
| Electric | 6–12 | Gaffer, best boy, board op, 3–8 sparks, genny op |
| Rigging (grip + electric) | 6–15 | Never on set — always on tomorrow's location |
| Art department | 8–15 on the day | Plus 20–60 in construction and paint off-site |
| Set decoration | 5–12 | Decorator, lead man, swing gang, on-set dresser |
| Props | 2–5 | Master, standby, assistants; more if action props |
| Costume | 5–12 | Supervisor, 2–4 set costumers, background costumers |
| Hair & makeup | 5–12 | Scales with cast count and with any prosthetics |
| Sound | 3–4 | Mixer, boom, utility — and rarely more (see §11) |
| Locations | 3–8 | LM, assistants, unit assistants |
| Transport | 8–30 | Drivers scale with the number of trucks and trailers |
| Catering / craft service | 4–8 | Contractors |
| Stunts / SFX / VFX | 0–40 | Entirely day-dependent; zero on a dialogue day |
| Editorial | 2–4 | Listed but not present |
| Support (medic, security, teacher, intimacy coordinator) | 2–8 | Some legally mandated |

The list has a property producers watch: it is **almost entirely fixed cost per
day**. Whether the unit shoots seven pages or half a page, the same 150 people
are paid, which is why the day — not the page — is the atomic unit of a film
budget (`06-principal-photography.md` §1).

### 2.3 What actually drives crew size

Crew grows for specific reasons, not in proportion to budget:

1. **Number of cameras.** Each additional camera adds an operator, 1st, 2nd,
   and usually a grip and a lamp op — and slows lighting, because every camera
   needs an acceptable angle.
2. **Set complexity and turnover.** A film that shoots on a new stage set every
   three days needs a rigging crew, a swing gang, and a construction department
   running weeks ahead in parallel.
3. **Practical effects, stunts, and creatures.** These arrive as whole
   sub-crews for specific days and vanish afterwards.
4. **Cast and background count.** Every 100 extras adds background costumers,
   makeup artists, ADs, holding-area staff, and catering.
5. **Night and weather work.** More lighting, more generators, more grip
   rigging, more transport.
6. **Location vs stage.** A location day adds locations staff, transport,
   security, and unit assistants that a stage day does not need.

## 3. Production department

The producing and logistics spine. Hired first, released last.

| Role | Reports to | What they own |
|---|---|---|
| **Executive Producer** | — | Usually financing, packaging, or a rights holder; on studio films often the exec who championed it. Credit is loosely defined. |
| **Producer** | Studio/financiers | The film. Develops it, hires the director, controls the budget, is answerable for delivery. The only credit the Academy awards for Best Picture (capped, and PGA-arbitrated). |
| **Co-Producer / Associate Producer** | Producer | Delegated slices; often a negotiated credit rather than a defined job. |
| **Line Producer** | Producer | The physical film: budget authorship, schedule feasibility, spending. Frequently the same person as, or senior to, the UPM. |
| **Unit Production Manager (UPM)** | Line producer/Producer | Day-to-day operations: crew deals, rentals, locations spend, cost report. DGA-covered. |
| **Production Supervisor** | UPM | Executes the UPM's function without the DGA credit; common on non-DGA or smaller films. |
| **Production Coordinator (POC)** | UPM | The production office: travel, contracts, distribution of scripts and call sheets, equipment orders. The information hub. |
| **Assistant Production Coordinator (APOC) / Production Secretary** | POC | The volume of the above. |
| **Travel Coordinator** | POC | Flights, hotels, visas, work permits, and the constant re-booking as the schedule moves. |
| **Production Accountant** (+ 1st/2nd assistants, payroll clerk) | Line producer | Payroll, purchase orders, petty cash, weekly **cost report**, incentive audit compliance. |
| **Script Supervisor** | Director (on the floor) | **Continuity** and the editorial record: what was shot, which takes were circled, screen direction, eyelines, wardrobe/prop continuity, running time estimate, lined script and daily editor's log. The editor's most important upstream collaborator. |
| **Production Assistants (PAs)** | Everywhere | Entry-level; office PAs, set PAs, and the AD department's runners. |

### 3.1 Line Producer / Unit Production Manager

**Reports to** the producer and, through them, the studio and any completion
bond. **Commands** the production office, the accountant, transport, locations,
and — budgetarily — every department. **Coordinates with** the 1st AD hourly
(schedule vs money), the POC continuously, and every HOD weekly.

**Joins** first among the physical-production crew, 16–20 weeks out. **Leaves**
last: after picture wrap they run wrap-out, asset disposal, final cost report,
and the incentive audit, which can run months past the shoot.

**The week.** They author and defend the budget in a package like Movie Magic:
top sheet, detail, fringes, and a contingency (usually 10%). They negotiate the
big vendor deals — camera, lighting, transport, stages, insurance. They read
the schedule the 1st AD produces and answer whether it can be paid for. Every
week they publish a **cost report**: what has been spent, what is committed,
and the **estimate to complete** (ETC) — the number the studio and bond
actually watch. Mid-shoot, most of their day is spent trading: the director
wants a crane day, the schedule wants a company move deleted, the art
department wants two more build days, and only one of those can be funded.

**What separates a good line producer/UPM from a mediocre one**

1. **Budgeting the film that is actually going to be shot**, not the one on the
   page. A good UPM reads a script and knows that "they drive across the
   desert" is eight days and "they argue in the kitchen" is one, before anyone
   has scheduled it.
2. **Knowing where the contingency really is.** Excellent UPMs pad in the right
   places — the sequences with unknowable difficulty — instead of trimming
   uniformly, which starves the whole film equally.
3. **Saying yes cheaply.** The best ones find the third option: not "no crane",
   but "no crane, and here is a rig that gets 80% of the move for a tenth of
   the cost, and I have already asked the key grip if it works."
4. **Protecting the schedule from the budget.** A mediocre UPM saves money on
   prep, rigging crew, and second-unit days — precisely the spend that buys
   shooting-day speed — and then loses the savings back in overtime.
5. **Reading the cost report early.** A film that is going over is detectable
   in week two by anyone who looks at pattern rather than total. Good UPMs
   raise it while it is still a conversation; mediocre ones raise it when it is
   a crisis.
6. **Crew relations.** They set hours, meal penalties, and how respectfully
   people are treated. A UPM the crew trusts can ask for a hard week and get
   it; one they don't gets worked to rule.
7. **Honesty upward.** The bond and the studio forgive overage they were warned
   about far more readily than overage they discover.

**How failure shows**

| Failure | Where it shows |
|---|---|
| Under-budgeted schedule | Days cut mid-shoot; scenes shot in half the coverage they need |
| Prep starved | Departments arrive unready; the first shooting week runs half a day behind daily |
| Cost report lagging reality | Studio finds out in week five; the last act of the film gets cut down to pay for the first |
| Over-tight crew deals | Turnover mid-shoot, and the replacements are worse |
| Poor vendor negotiation | 5–15% of the equipment budget lost invisibly |
| Overage concealed | Loss of studio confidence; bond intervention; UPM replaced |

### 3.2 Production Coordinator (POC)

**Reports to** the UPM. **Commands** the APOC, production secretary, travel
coordinator, office PAs. **Coordinates with** literally everyone — the POC is
the switchboard through which departments learn what the others are doing.

**Joins** 8–12 weeks out, when the office opens. **Leaves** several weeks after
wrap, closing accounts, returning kit, and archiving paperwork.

**The day.** Distribute the call sheet and script revisions (coloured pages, in
the standard revision order) to a list of 200 people who must all have the same
version. Raise purchase orders. Book flights, hotels, work permits, and
insurance certificates. Order and chase equipment. Field the phone calls that
would otherwise reach the UPM. Maintain the crew list, the contact list, and
the movement orders. Run the office as a 14-hour information relay while the
shoot is running.

**What separates a good POC from a mediocre one**

1. **Nobody is ever working from the wrong version.** This is the whole job:
   revision discipline, distribution lists, and the confidence that the pink
   pages reached the standby props and the 2nd AD, not just the HODs.
2. **Anticipation.** Knowing that the week-six night exterior means the unit
   needs 40 extra hotel rooms, and booking them in week two rather than
   discovering the town is full.
3. **Filtering.** Protecting the UPM's attention from the 80% of traffic that
   the office can resolve itself, without hiding the 20% that cannot.
4. **Vendor memory.** Knowing which rental house will deliver on a Sunday and
   which airline will re-book a cast member without a fee.
5. **Calm under a schedule change.** When tomorrow's location falls out at 6pm,
   the POC re-books cast travel, notifies 200 people, and reissues paperwork in
   two hours without a single person turning up in the wrong place.

**How failure shows.** Wrong-version pages on set (a scene shot as written
three drafts ago), kit that did not arrive, an actor at the wrong hotel, a
missing insurance certificate that stops a location day, and a UPM buried in
traffic instead of managing the film's money.

### 3.3 Production Accountant

**Reports to** the line producer. **Commands** first and second assistant
accountants, a payroll clerk, and on larger films a dedicated incentive
accountant. **Coordinates with** every HOD (their purchase orders and petty
cash), payroll companies, and the studio's finance department.

**Joins** 8–12 weeks out. **Leaves** among the very last, often 8–20 weeks
after wrap, because the audit and the incentive claim outlive the film's
production.

**The week.** Process timecards and cut payroll — the single largest recurring
outflow, weekly, with union rules, overtime tiers, meal penalties, and fringes
all computed per person. Approve and code purchase orders against the budget's
account structure. Manage petty cash floats for departments that buy in the
field. Produce the weekly **cost report** with the UPM, reconciling actuals,
commitments, and estimate-to-complete. Maintain the paperwork trail the tax
incentive audit will demand — which, in a rebate jurisdiction, is worth 20–40%
of qualifying spend and is lost entirely on a documentation failure.

**What separates a good production accountant from a mediocre one**

1. **The cost report tells the truth early.** A good accountant surfaces
   commitments — money spent but not yet invoiced — so the report reflects the
   film's real position rather than its cheque book.
2. **Incentive literacy.** Knowing exactly what qualifies in this jurisdiction,
   coding it correctly at the moment of spend, and never having to reconstruct
   it later. Reconstructed claims lose money.
3. **Speed of payment.** Vendors and crew who are paid on time give better
   service and better rates; a production with a reputation for slow payment
   pays a premium forever.
4. **Making departments legible to themselves.** Good accountants tell an HOD
   where their department actually stands weekly, in terms they can act on.
5. **Audit-proof paperwork** without turning the office into a bureaucracy that
   stops the film moving.

**How failure shows.** An incentive claim reduced or rejected (the largest
single avoidable loss on many independent films), payroll errors that poison
crew morale within a week, a cost report that understates by 8% until week six,
and a wrap that drags for months because nothing was reconciled as it went.

### 3.4 Script Supervisor

Organisationally in the production department; functionally the director's and
editor's person on the floor. On a small crew they are one of a handful of
people whose absence stops the day.

**Reports to** the director on the floor, and effectively serves the editor.
**Commands** nobody (it is a department of one, occasionally two on multi-unit
films). **Coordinates with** camera (lens, filter, frame), the 1st AD (timings
and what is still owed), costume/hair/makeup/props (continuity states), and
editorial (the daily paperwork).

**Joins** 2–4 weeks before photography, timing the script and attending the
production meeting and tech scout. **Leaves** at picture wrap, though they are
often called during post to answer "was there a take where…".

**The day.** Sit beside the director, watching the take rather than the
monitor's frame. Track continuity of everything visible and audible: which hand
held the glass, how full it was, which way the actor exited, what was said that
differed from the page. Note lens, filters, and camera position per setup. Time
each take. Record which takes the director circles and why. Produce, daily:
the **lined script** (a vertical line per setup showing what coverage exists
for every line of dialogue), the **editor's log** with take-by-take comments,
the **daily continuity report**, and a running estimate of the film's length.

**What separates a good script supervisor from a mediocre one**

1. **Catching the mismatch in the moment.** Not on the report — in the take,
   loudly enough that the shot is redone while the set is still lit, which
   costs 90 seconds instead of a reshoot day.
2. **Coverage awareness.** Knowing, at 6pm, that the scene has no clean single
   on the actor who carries the scene's turn, and telling the 1st AD before
   the unit moves off.
3. **Editorial thinking.** Understanding how the scene will be cut and
   protecting the cut points: eyeline consistency, screen direction, matching
   action across the cut, the overlaps an editor needs.
4. **Judgement about which continuity actually matters.** Obsessing over a
   background extra's hat while missing a reversed screen direction is the
   classic mediocre failure. Audiences never see the hat; they always feel the
   geography being wrong.
5. **Notes an editor can use.** "T4 best for her, T6 best for him, T7 has the
   good door" is worth more than a transcription of every take.
6. **Diplomacy.** They contradict the director, the DP, and the star, in front
   of the crew, several times a day, and must remain someone people listen to.

**How failure shows**

| Failure | Where it shows |
|---|---|
| Missed continuity error | Visible jump in the finished film, or a usable take rendered unusable in the cut |
| Missed screen-direction reversal | The geography of a scene becomes incoherent; sometimes only fixable by flipping or cutting the shot |
| Coverage gap not flagged | The editor has no way to make the scene work; pickups or a reshoot day |
| Poor notes | The editor burns days re-screening every take to find what the circled take was supposed to be |
| Bad timings | Running-length estimate wrong by 15 minutes; the studio discovers a 2h40 assembly it did not budget for |

## 4. Directing department (the AD team)

| Role | Reports to | What they own |
|---|---|---|
| **Director** | Producer/Studio | Performance, staging, coverage, tone. Chooses shots with the DP, directs actors, approves every department's work, and (per DGA) has a right to the **director's cut**. |
| **1st Assistant Director (1st AD)** | Director & UPM | The shooting day. Builds the schedule, issues call sheets, runs the floor, calls the roll, manages background, and is the **safety officer** on set. The single biggest determinant of how many pages get shot. |
| **2nd AD** | 1st AD | Cast wrangling: call times, actor movement, the **production report**, tomorrow's call sheet, background paperwork. Usually based at unit base rather than on the floor. |
| **2nd 2nd AD / 3rd AD** | 2nd AD | Set-side support, background action, additional units. |
| **Set PA / Trainee AD** | AD team | Locking up the set, escorting cast, holding the public. |
| **Additional / Second Unit AD** | 2nd unit director | The parallel unit's floor. |

**Note on nomenclature**: UK/Australian usage differs — the UK "3rd AD" is on
the floor and the "2nd AD" is at base; the US "2nd 2nd" fills the UK 3rd's job.

### 4.1 Director

**Reports to** the producer and studio, with contractual protections (final
cut is rare; the DGA-guaranteed **director's cut** is universal). **Commands**
every department creatively. **Coordinates with** the DP and 1st AD constantly,
the editor daily during the shoot, and the producer about everything.

**Joins** at development or attachment, often years ahead. **Leaves** at
delivery, after the director's cut, studio cuts, test screenings, the mix, and
the DI (`07-postproduction.md`).

**The week, in prep.** Location scouts, design approvals, casting sessions,
storyboarding or shot-listing with the DP, rehearsals with cast, camera and
wardrobe tests, and the production meeting where every department asks them a
question they must answer immediately. Prep is where a director's film is
actually determined; the shoot mostly executes it.

**The day, in production.** Block the scene with the actors, hand it to the DP
and 1st AD, work with the actors while the crew lights, shoot the coverage,
watch playback, decide when a take is good, and move on. The recurring
decision is not "what is the best shot" but "is this good enough, given that
there are four more scenes today" — a judgement made 30–60 times a day.

**What separates a good director from a mediocre one**

1. **Knowing what the scene is about**, so that when time runs out the shot
   that carries the meaning has been captured and the ones that don't have been
   sacrificed on purpose.
2. **Directing actors specifically.** A mediocre director gives adjectives
   ("more menacing"); a good one gives an action, a target, or a change of
   circumstance the actor can play. Actors work harder for directors who make
   them better.
3. **Deciding fast, in public.** The unit stands still while the director
   thinks. Indecision is the most expensive personal trait on a set
   (`06-…` §5) and it is visible to a crew within two days.
4. **Shooting for the cut.** Getting the pieces the edit needs, in the right
   sizes, with usable overlaps — rather than a beautiful master with no way in
   or out of it.
5. **Delegation and trust.** Good directors give HODs a clear brief and then
   let them work; mediocre ones either abdicate (departments freelance and the
   film has no unity) or micromanage (departments stop bringing ideas).
6. **Holding tone across ten weeks.** Consistency of performance pitch and
   visual grammar across scenes shot months apart and out of order is a real,
   trainable skill and the most common thing missing from first features.
7. **Preserving political capital.** Every argument won with the studio costs
   something; good directors spend it on the two things that matter and concede
   the rest.

**How failure shows.** A film that is well made shot by shot and inert as a
whole; performances that vary in register between scenes; an editor with no
way to cut a sequence; a schedule that collapses in the last week because the
first week was over-covered; or a director's cut so far from the film the
studio thought it was financing that the marketing has nothing to sell
(`09-marketing-and-distribution.md`).

### 4.2 1st Assistant Director

The most operationally consequential job on a film, and the one whose skill
axis is easiest to measure: pages per day.

**Reports to** the director (creatively) and the UPM (logistically) —
genuinely two masters. **Commands** the entire AD department and, on the floor,
every crew member's *time*. **Coordinates with** every HOD before each setup,
the script supervisor on what is owed, transport and locations on moves, and
the 2nd AD on cast.

**Joins** 8–16 weeks out — early enough to build the schedule from the
breakdown (`04-preproduction.md` §2). **Leaves** at picture wrap, occasionally
staying for reshoots.

**The week, in prep.** Break down the script, build the strip board and the
DOOD (day-out-of-days), and iterate the schedule against location availability,
cast availability, build completion, weather windows, and money. Attend every
scout and the production meeting. Walk each location and pre-plan the day's
shape: where the unit parks, what gets shot first, where the sun will be.

**The day, in production.** Set the day's order and defend it. Call the roll
("picture's up… turn over"). Give every department its next instruction before
it needs it, so that lighting, makeup, background, and props are all converging
on the same moment. Watch the clock against the page count continuously and
know by 11am whether the day is achievable. Run safety: the briefings, the
hazard controls, the authority to stop work. Manage background action. Protect
the director from interruption while still telling them the truth about time.

**What separates a good 1st AD from a mediocre one**

1. **Scheduling that survives contact.** A good schedule groups by location and
   by lighting condition, front-loads what is riskiest, and leaves recoverable
   material late — so a lost hour costs a shot, not a day.
2. **Running the day one setup ahead.** The mediocre 1st AD manages the current
   shot; the good one has already sent the rigging crew, put the next actor in
   makeup, and pre-lit the reverse. Everything the crew experiences as "fast"
   is this.
3. **Reading the director.** Knowing when the director is genuinely finding
   something and needs another take, and when they are stalling — and pushing
   only in the second case.
4. **Honest time reporting.** "We are 40 minutes down and the last scene is at
   risk" at 2pm gives the director a choice; the same information at 6pm gives
   them a loss.
5. **Authority without volume.** A crew obeys a 1st AD they respect. Shouting
   is the visible symptom of having lost the room.
6. **Safety judgement that holds under pressure.** The good ones stop the shot.
   The industry's serious incidents overwhelmingly follow a day where somebody
   decided the schedule mattered more (`06-…` §8).
7. **Background direction.** Crowd that behaves like people rather than like
   extras is a 1st AD's craft and is invisible when done well.

**How failure shows**

| Failure | Where it shows |
|---|---|
| Bad schedule | Company moves and re-lights that need not have existed; days lost across the whole shoot |
| Not working ahead | The unit waits; 45–90 minutes a day evaporates in turnarounds |
| Losing the day quietly | Scenes dropped or shot in one wide at 7pm — visible in the finished film as a flat, uncovered scene |
| Over-deference to the director | Schedule overruns become the producer's problem in week three |
| Over-assertion | Director stops trusting them; the two chains of §1 split and the film slows |
| Safety failure | Injury, shutdown, investigation, insurance and legal exposure |
| Poor background handling | Crowd that looks staged; the scene's world reads as false |

### 4.3 2nd AD

**Reports to** the 1st AD. **Commands** the 2nd 2nd/3rd ADs and set PAs.
**Coordinates with** cast, makeup/hair/costume (the morning pipeline), transport
(pickups), and the production office.

**Joins** 2–4 weeks out. **Leaves** at wrap, after the last production report.

**The day.** Runs unit base, not the floor. Wakes the cast pipeline: pickups,
makeup, hair, costume, and delivery of each actor to set at the exact moment
they are needed and not an hour early (an actor sitting in costume for two
hours is a morale and overtime problem). Books and briefs background. Produces
the **daily production report** after wrap and drafts **tomorrow's call sheet**
for the 1st AD and UPM to approve — the last document of a 14-hour day, made
while everyone else goes home.

**Good vs mediocre**: a good 2nd AD is never the reason the floor waits. They
build a call sheet that reflects what will actually happen, get cast to set
prepared and on time, and keep background numbers correct so the production is
neither short of people nor paying 60 extras to sit in a tent. The mediocre
version produces a call sheet that is optimistic, delivers actors late or too
early, and generates a stream of small delays that the 1st AD absorbs all day.

## 5. Camera department

| Role | Reports to | What they own |
|---|---|---|
| **Director of Photography / Cinematographer (DP/DoP)** | Director | The image: lighting, lens and format choice, exposure, camera movement, and the film's **look** (with the director and colourist). Commands both camera and lighting/grip departments. |
| **Camera Operator** | DP | Frames and executes the shot. On many films the DP operates themselves (especially in the US indie and UK traditions). |
| **1st Assistant Camera (Focus Puller)** | Operator/DP | Focus, lens changes, camera build and maintenance. A blown focus is an unusable take, so this is a high-pressure, high-skill job. |
| **2nd Assistant Camera (Clapper Loader)** | 1st AC | Slate, camera reports, media/magazine handling, marks. |
| **Digital Imaging Technician (DIT)** | DP | On-set colour pipeline, exposure monitoring, LUTs, look management, and liaison with post. |
| **Data Wrangler / Digital Loader** | DIT | Offload, checksum, backup, and delivery of camera media. The single point of catastrophic data loss risk. |
| **Steadicam Operator** | DP | Stabilised handheld work; often a specialist day-hire. |
| **Drone / Aerial Operator, Underwater Operator, Techno-crane Operator** | DP | Specialist rigs, with their own licensing and safety regimes. |
| **Video Assist / Playback Operator** | DP/Director | The monitors ("video village"), recording and instant replay of takes. |
| **Camera Trainee / Loader** | 2nd AC | Entry point into the department. |
| **Unit Stills Photographer** | Publicity/DP | The marketing stills. Contractually important — see `09-…`. |

Multi-camera work multiplies the whole department: an "A camera" and "B camera"
each need an operator, 1st, and 2nd.

### 5.1 Director of Photography

The largest single creative delegation a director makes, and the head of the
biggest cluster of crew on the floor: camera, grip, and electric together are
20–35 people on a studio film.

**Reports to** the director. **Commands** the camera department directly and
the grip and electrical departments through the key grip and gaffer.
**Coordinates with** the production designer (the sets must be lightable and
the palette must survive the format), the 1st AD (how long a setup takes), the
costume designer and makeup head (how fabrics and skin read on this sensor),
VFX (plate requirements), and in post the colourist.

**Joins** 10–14 weeks out on a studio film, 4–6 on a low-budget one. **Leaves**
at picture wrap, returning for the DI grade — the DP's contractual right to
supervise the grade is a real and sometimes contested term.

**The week, in prep.** Agree the look with the director in references, then
test it: camera and lens tests, filtration, LUT creation with the DIT and the
post facility, makeup and wardrobe tests shot and graded. Walk every location
and stage, decide where the light comes from and what has to be rigged, and
issue that to the gaffer and key grip as a plan the rigging crew can execute
ahead of the unit. Order the camera package. Shot-list or board the film with
the director.

**The day.** Watch the block, then say — usually within two minutes — where the
camera goes, what lens, and how the scene is lit. Hand the *how* to the gaffer
and key grip and then work the details: exposure, the actor's key, the
background separation, the fill. Approve the frame with the operator, watch
the take, and decide whether it is exposed and composed as intended.

**What separates a good DP from a mediocre one**

1. **The look serves the story.** Anyone can make an image pretty. A good DP
   makes a specific film look like itself — and can articulate *why* this scene
   is soft and warm and that one is hard and cold, in terms the director
   recognises as their own intention.
2. **Speed.** The single most commercially valued attribute below the top tier.
   A DP who lights a scene in 40 minutes rather than 90 gives the film an extra
   two setups a day, which across 40 days is worth several days of schedule.
   Speed comes from pre-planning and from lighting *broadly* — building an
   environment that works from several angles — rather than per-shot.
3. **Consistency across the shoot.** Scenes shot eleven weeks apart must cut
   together. Mediocre DPs drift; good ones keep references and a disciplined
   pipeline.
4. **Knowing what the grade can and cannot fix.** Underexposed shadows,
   clipped highlights, and mixed colour temperature are the three things that
   cost real money in the DI.
5. **Leading two departments.** The DP does not touch a lamp or a flag. Their
   output arrives entirely through the gaffer and key grip, which makes
   communication precision — not visual taste — the binding skill.
6. **Working with the director's process.** Some directors want the DP to
   propose; some want them to execute. Misreading this permanently damages the
   working relationship.
7. **Protecting the actors' time and the light.** Good DPs light with
   stand-ins, are ready when the actors arrive, and never make a performer wait
   in the cold for a flag adjustment.

**How failure shows**

| Failure | Where it shows |
|---|---|
| Slow lighting | Setups lost daily; scenes shot with less coverage; overtime |
| Inconsistent look | Scenes that cannot be cut against each other; expensive DI work |
| Under/over-exposure | Noise, clipping, and grade limitations that survive into the release print |
| Coverage sacrificed to beauty | An editor who cannot cut around a performance problem |
| Poor VFX plate discipline | Missing reference, no clean plate, unusable tracking markers — cost lands in `08-…` |
| Weak department leadership | Grip and electric working at cross-purposes; lighting time doubles |

### 5.2 Camera Operator

**Reports to** the DP. **Commands** the camera crew on the floor when the DP is
at the monitor. **Coordinates with** the dolly grip (a move is one performance
by two people), the 1st AC, and the actors.

**Joins and leaves** with the shoot, plus rehearsal days for complex moves.

**Good vs mediocre.** A good operator composes *inside* a moving frame in real
time, keeping headroom, lead room, and the significant part of the actor's
performance in the right part of the screen while the actor improvises
something different from rehearsal. They protect the DP's frame while
absorbing the unexpected — an actor who steps two feet left, a hand that comes
up into frame. They also give the director honest, immediate feedback ("I lost
her eyes on the turn") because they are the only person who saw the shot as it
truly was. Mediocre operating produces frames that are technically correct and
slightly dead, or takes ruined by a reframe half a beat late.

### 5.3 1st Assistant Camera (Focus Puller)

**Reports to** the operator and DP. **Commands** the 2nd AC and trainee.

**The day.** Build and maintain the camera. Measure and mark actor distances
before the take, then pull focus by hand or wheel through the take — often
blind, judging by the actor's movement rather than the monitor. Change lenses
between setups in under two minutes. Keep the camera clean, the batteries
charged, and the timecode synced.

**Good vs mediocre.** Wide-open, large-format, long-lens shooting has made
usable depth of field vanishingly thin — inches on a close-up. A great 1st AC
holds focus on a moving actor at T1.4 and does it on take one, which is worth
more schedule than most departments' entire efficiency. A mediocre one is
"soft on the good take", the single most demoralising sentence on a film set,
because it means the performance existed and cannot be used.

## 6. Grip department

Grips handle everything the camera sits on or that shapes light without being
electrical.

| Role | Reports to | What they own |
|---|---|---|
| **Key Grip** | DP | Camera support (dolly, crane, car rigs), and rigging of flags, nets, silks, and overheads that shape light. |
| **Best Boy Grip** | Key grip | Crew, kit, truck, and ordering. |
| **Dolly Grip** | Key grip | Executing dolly and crane moves — a performance skill in its own right, since a move happens once, live. |
| **Grips / Rigging Grips** | Key/best boy grip | The physical work; rigging grips pre-rig locations ahead of the shooting crew. |
| **Crane / Technocrane Operator** | Key grip | Specialist arm and remote-head work. |

In the UK the equivalent structure is a smaller grip team, with more of the
rigging work sitting with **stagehands** and the lighting department.

### 6.1 Key Grip

**Reports to** the DP. **Commands** the grip crew, including a separate rigging
grip crew working ahead on other locations. **Coordinates with** the gaffer
constantly (they share the lighting problem and the same physical space), the
1st AD (how long a rig takes), construction (what a wall or ceiling can carry),
stunts and SFX (any rig that moves or is dangerous), and safety.

**Joins** 4–8 weeks out on a studio film. **Leaves** at wrap, with rigging crew
continuing a few days into strike.

**The day.** Get the camera where the DP wants it — laying dolly track level on
uneven ground, building a platform, mounting a rig on a moving vehicle, flying
a camera from a crane. Simultaneously, shape the light the gaffer creates:
flags to cut it, nets to reduce it, silks and bounce to soften and redirect it,
overheads to control an entire exterior. Rig safely: everything grips do
involves heavy objects at height above people.

**What separates a good key grip from a mediocre one**

1. **Speed with heavy things.** Almost all grip work sits directly in the
   critical path between "the DP has decided" and "the camera can roll". A key
   grip who rigs a 20×20 overhead in 15 minutes instead of 40 buys shots.
2. **Anticipating the shot.** Good keys watch the block and start building
   before they are asked, so that track is down as the DP finishes the sentence.
3. **Solving the impossible mount.** The camera has to be in a place there is
   no floor for — the key grip's real reputation is built on inventing a safe
   rig for that, quickly, from standard hardware.
4. **Owning light control as craft, not labour.** The difference between hard
   sun and a usable exterior is entirely the grip department's overheads and
   negative fill; a key grip who understands what the DP is trying to achieve
   proposes the shape rather than waiting for the instruction.
5. **Absolute safety discipline.** Grips hang, clamp, and counterweight things
   above the cast and crew all day. The good ones are visibly rigorous about
   safety cables, sandbags, and load limits — and are willing to refuse.
6. **Partnership with the dolly grip and the operator.** A complex move is a
   choreographed act between three people and is rehearsed as such.

**How failure shows.** Camera waits for the rig; moves that judder or arrive
late on the mark, ruining otherwise good takes; exteriors that go unusable
because the sun was never controlled; and — the serious case — a dropped or
failed rig, which is one of the most common causes of set injury.

### 6.2 Dolly Grip

A small, unusually skilled job. The dolly grip physically executes the camera
move — accelerating, decelerating, and stopping on a mark, in sync with an
actor's performance, once, live, with no undo. On a move where the camera must
arrive as the actor turns, the dolly grip is performing to the same timing as
the actor. Mediocre dolly work is the reason a take is "no good for camera"
when the performance was fine, and it costs a re-take at full crew cost every
time.

## 7. Electrical / lighting department

| Role | Reports to | What they own |
|---|---|---|
| **Gaffer (Chief Lighting Technician)** | DP | Realising the DP's lighting: fixtures, power, rigging plan, crew. The DP says what it should look like; the gaffer decides how to make that happen. |
| **Best Boy Electric** | Gaffer | Crew, orders, truck, and the power plan. |
| **Electricians / Lamp Operators ("sparks")** | Gaffer | Setting and running lights. |
| **Board Operator / Console Programmer** | Gaffer | LED and console programming, increasingly a specialist as fixtures became addressable. |
| **Generator Operator (Genny Op)** | Gaffer | Power generation and distribution. |
| **Rigging Gaffer & Rigging Crew** | Gaffer | Pre-rigging locations and stages before the unit arrives — the reason a big film can shoot on a new stage every few days. |

### 7.1 Gaffer

**Reports to** the DP — and only the DP; a gaffer taking lighting instructions
from anyone else is a symptom of a broken department. **Commands** the
electrical department, including a separate rigging crew that is never on set.
**Coordinates with** the key grip (they solve the same problem from two sides),
the production designer and set decorator (practical fixtures in shot, and
where a lamp can be hidden), construction (rigging points, wild walls, ceiling
access), locations and the UPM (power supply, generators, and their cost), and
the 1st AD (how long).

**Joins** 4–8 weeks out; the rigging gaffer sometimes earlier, because
pre-rigging a stage is a multi-week job. **Leaves** at wrap.

**The week, in prep.** Walk every set and location with the DP, take the
lighting intent, and convert it into a fixture list, a power plan, a rigging
schedule, and a crew size. Order the lighting package — a five- to six-figure
weekly rental on a studio film, and one of the largest single equipment lines
in the budget. Plan the rigging sequence so that the unit never arrives at a
location that has not been pre-rigged.

**The day.** Take the DP's description ("she should feel lit by the window,
falling off hard to camera left") and produce it: which fixture, where, how
high, through what diffusion, at what intensity and colour temperature. Direct
the sparks. Watch the monitor for what the DP has not yet said they want.
Manage power, heat, cable runs, and the safety of everything electrical in a
building full of water, metal, and people.

**What separates a good gaffer from a mediocre one**

1. **Translating description into fixtures instantly.** The good gaffer hears a
   feeling and names a lamp. That translation is the entire job and it is why
   DPs carry gaffers between films.
2. **Speed, and knowing where speed comes from.** Most lighting time is spent
   not on the key light but on fixing the background and killing spill. Good
   gaffers pre-light the environment so that new angles need adjustment rather
   than rebuilding.
3. **Rigging strategy.** Deciding what the rigging crew does overnight versus
   what the shooting crew does live is the biggest lever a gaffer has on the
   schedule; a well-run rigging crew can save an hour every morning.
4. **Anticipating the reverse.** A mediocre gaffer lights the shot. A good one
   lights the shot in a way that survives turning around — so the reverse takes
   15 minutes instead of an hour. This is the largest hidden difference in
   shooting-day throughput on any dialogue film.
5. **Power and load literacy.** Knowing what a location can carry, what needs a
   generator, and what will trip a building's supply at the worst moment.
6. **Crew management.** Sparks work at height, with heat, current, and heavy
   fixtures. A gaffer who runs a calm crew produces a safe one.
7. **Saying "yes, and here is the cheaper way".** Gaffers who propose the
   practical-in-shot solution rather than the truck of fixtures make friends of
   both the DP and the UPM.

**How failure shows**

| Failure | Where it shows |
|---|---|
| Slow relights on the turnaround | 30–60 minutes lost per scene; setups dropped by mid-afternoon |
| Under-ordered package | Compromised looks, or emergency rentals at premium rates |
| Over-ordered package | Tens of thousands of dollars a week of unused lighting sitting on a truck |
| Rigging behind schedule | The unit arrives at an unrigged location — the most expensive single failure in the department |
| Poor spill control | Lens flare, contaminated background, matte-line trouble in VFX shots |
| Electrical safety failure | Injury, shutdown, and an insurance investigation |

## 8. Art department

The largest department on most films, and the one with the longest lead time.

| Role | Reports to | What they own |
|---|---|---|
| **Production Designer (PD)** | Director | The physical world: sets, locations' treatment, colour, texture, period accuracy, and (with the DP) the film's visual identity. |
| **Supervising Art Director / Art Director** | PD | Executing the design: drawings, budgets, construction liaison, crew management. On big films there are several ADs by unit or set. |
| **Set Designer / Draughtsperson** | Art director | Technical drawings from which sets are built. |
| **Concept Artist / Illustrator** | PD | Look development, key frames, sellable images for approvals. |
| **Graphic Designer** | Art director | Every piece of printed matter, signage, screen graphics, labels, newspapers. |
| **Art Department Coordinator** | PD/Art director | Scheduling, budgets, and drawing distribution for the department. |
| **Set Decorator** | PD | Everything inside the set that isn't built: furniture, drapes, rugs, dressing. A senior, separately-credited role (Oscar-nominated alongside the PD). |
| **Lead Man / Swing Gang** | Set decorator | Sourcing, transporting, installing and striking the dressing. |
| **On-Set Dresser** | Set decorator | Maintaining and adjusting dressing during shooting, and its continuity. |
| **Property Master** | PD (and director) | Every object an actor touches: sourcing, multiples, action props, weapons paperwork. |
| **Props Assistants / Standby Props** | Prop master | On-floor props handling and continuity. |
| **Armourer / Weapons Master** | Prop master/SFX | Firearms and blanks: storage, licensing, safety briefings, and on-set control. A legally regulated, safety-critical role. |
| **Construction Coordinator** | PD/Art director | Building the sets: schedule, labour, materials, budget. |
| **Construction Foreman, Carpenters, Sculptors, Plasterers, Modelmakers** | Construction coordinator | The build. |
| **Scenic Artist / Charge Scenic, Painters** | Construction/PD | Finish, texture, ageing — where a build stops looking like plywood. |
| **Greensman** | Set decorator | Plants, trees, landscaping, both real and artificial. |
| **Standby Carpenter / Standby Painter / Standby Rigger** | Construction | On-set repairs and wild-wall moves during the shooting day. |

### 8.1 Production Designer

**Reports to** the director. **Commands** art direction, set decoration, props,
construction, paint, and greens — 30 to 150 people on a studio film, more than
any other head. **Coordinates with** the DP (the two of them jointly determine
what the film looks like), the costume designer (palette must agree), VFX (what
is built versus extended), the UPM (the art department is usually the second
largest cost after cast), and the 1st AD (build completion gates the schedule).

**Joins** 12–20 weeks out — earlier than anyone except the producer, director,
and UPM, because construction takes months. **Leaves** at wrap, though the PD's
active period ends when the last set is finished; many PDs are already on their
next film by the final weeks.

**The week, in prep.** Read the script for the world, not the rooms. Produce
references, concepts, and a colour and materials logic. Decide, per scene,
build versus location versus a location modified — the decision with the
largest budget consequence in the entire department. Present to the director
and studio for approval. Hand approved designs to art directors for drawings,
then supervise construction, paint, and dressing as sets come up.

**The week, in production.** Attend the shoot far less than people assume:
a PD's shooting-day presence is usually limited to the first day on a new set.
Most of their time is on the sets that shoot in three weeks.

**What separates a good production designer from a mediocre one**

1. **Designing the story, not the place.** A good PD builds a room that tells
   you who lives there and what has happened to them — and that gets *worse or
   better* as the film progresses.
2. **Designing for camera.** Sight lines, wild walls, ceiling access, depth
   behind the actor, and somewhere for the lights to go. A beautiful set that
   only works from one angle costs the DP an hour per turnaround for its whole
   shooting block.
3. **Budget realism at the concept stage.** Mediocre designers present a design
   the film cannot build and then cut it badly. Good ones know the number
   before the presentation and put the money where the camera will be.
4. **Specificity of research.** Period, class, and place are believable through
   details a general audience cannot name but immediately senses when wrong.
5. **Sequencing the build.** Which set is needed first, which can be built on
   the same stage after the first strikes, and what the schedule can survive.
   This is co-authored with the 1st AD and is where art and schedule most often
   collide.
6. **Delegation.** The PD who tries to draw everything themselves stalls a
   department of 80 people.
7. **Getting the same film as the DP and costume designer.** Three departments
   independently making tasteful choices produces an incoherent image.

**How failure shows**

| Failure | Where it shows |
|---|---|
| Set not finished on schedule | The shoot reorders around it, or shoots on a set with painters still working |
| Set unlightable / no wild walls | Lighting and camera time doubles for every day on that set |
| Over-designed for the coverage | Money spent on what the lens never sees |
| Wrong period or class detail | The world reads as false; reviewers name it (`16-…`) |
| Palette fights costume and photography | A muddy, unintentional-looking image no grade repairs |
| Build over budget | Money taken out of shooting days — the department that overspends steals from the schedule |

### 8.2 Art Director / Supervising Art Director

The PD designs; the art director makes the design real, on time, for the money.
On a large film a **supervising art director** runs several art directors, each
owning sets or units.

**Reports to** the PD. **Commands** set designers/draughtspeople, graphic
designers, and the art department coordinator; instructs construction through
the construction coordinator. **Coordinates with** construction, the UPM
(purchase orders), locations, and the 1st AD.

**Joins** 8–14 weeks out. **Leaves** at or shortly after wrap.

**The week.** Turn concepts into dimensioned drawings a carpenter can build
from. Cost each set and hold the department's budget. Solve the practical
questions the design ignores: how the wall breaks away, where the ceiling piece
flies, whether the doorway takes a dolly, how the set is transported. Chase
construction against a schedule that is measured in stages and shooting dates.

**Good vs mediocre.** A good art director is a project manager who can also
draw: they know the build cost of a decision at the moment it is made, they
catch the drawing error before 40 carpenters build it, and they never let a set
be delivered on the day it shoots rather than two days ahead. Mediocre art
direction shows up as sets that arrive late and cost more than the estimate,
and as a PD who is dragged into logistics instead of designing.

### 8.3 Set Decorator

A separately credited, separately Oscar-nominated head, and a genuine
department in its own right — on a period or ensemble film, set decoration
frequently outspends construction.

**Reports to** the PD. **Commands** the lead man (UK: head buyer/dressing
prop master), buyers, the swing gang, on-set dressers, and greens.
**Coordinates with** the prop master (the boundary between "dressing" and
"props" is drawn at whether an actor handles it), the DP (practical lamps in
shot are theirs), costume (palette), the script supervisor (dressing
continuity), and the 1st AD (dressing and striking around the schedule).

**Joins** 8–12 weeks out. **Leaves** at wrap, plus a strike and return period —
much of what a set decorator uses is hired and must go back undamaged.

**The week.** Source everything inside the set that is not built: furniture,
rugs, drapes, lamps, art on the walls, books on the shelves, food in the
fridge. Buy, hire, or have made. Dress the set before the unit arrives, and
have the swing gang strike and re-dress overnight so the same stage can be a
different room tomorrow.

**Good vs mediocre.** Good decoration is *specific and inhabited* — the objects
belong to the character rather than to the period, they are arranged as someone
living there would arrange them, and the depth behind the actor has something
in it worth seeing. Mediocre decoration is generically correct: nothing is
wrong, and nothing tells you anything. The other axis is logistical: a set
decorator who can turn a stage overnight is worth days of schedule; one who
cannot forces the 1st AD to schedule around dressing time.

### 8.4 Property Master

**Reports to** the PD, and in practice directly to the director for hero props.
**Commands** standby props and props assistants; supervises the armourer where
weapons are used. **Coordinates with** the actors (a prop is an acting
instrument), the script supervisor (prop continuity), stunts and SFX (anything
that breaks, fires, or is dangerous), costume (anything carried or worn), and
VFX (props that will be replaced or augmented).

**Joins** 6–10 weeks out. **Leaves** at wrap plus a return period.

**The day.** Have every object the scene needs on the floor before it is asked
for, in the right period, right condition, and — critically — in **multiples**:
a hero version for close-ups, stunt/rubber versions, breakaways, and enough
duplicates that the scene can be shot repeatedly. Manage consumables (food,
cigarettes, paperwork) so take twelve looks like take one. Manage the legal
regime around weapons: licences, storage, transport, blanks, briefings, and
the chain of custody on set.

**What separates a good prop master from a mediocre one**

1. **Multiples and consumables planned before the day.** The commonest and most
   avoidable lost hour on a set is a scene halted because there is only one of
   something that has now been broken, eaten, or bloodied.
2. **Props that are right in the actor's hand.** Weight, wear, and how it opens.
   Actors build behaviour out of props; a wrong object flattens a performance.
3. **Anticipating what the scene will actually need.** The script says "he
   signs the form"; a good prop master brings twelve forms, three pens that
   work, and a blotter, and knows which the director will want.
4. **Safety and legal rigour on weapons.** This is a life-safety function with
   statutory obligations. Good prop masters and armourers are inflexible about
   procedure and will stop the shot.
5. **Continuity discipline** with the script supervisor across weeks.
6. **Sourcing under pressure.** A director invents a prop at 4pm for tomorrow
   morning; the good prop master has it.

**How failure shows.** The unit waits while something is found; a scene shot
without the object it is about; visible continuity breaks in the cut; period
errors that reviewers catch; and, in the weapons case, the most serious
category of on-set incident there is.

### 8.5 Construction Coordinator

**Reports to** the PD and supervising art director. **Commands** foremen,
carpenters, plasterers, sculptors, welders, and — in coordination with the
charge scenic — the paint crew, often 30–120 people. **Coordinates with**
studio/stage management, the gaffer and key grip (rigging points and load),
special effects (anything that must break, move, or flood), and the 1st AD
(delivery dates against shooting dates).

**Joins** 10–16 weeks out. **Leaves** after strike, weeks past wrap.

**Good vs mediocre.** The output measure is brutal and simple: is the set
finished, safe, and paintable two days before it shoots? A good coordinator
sequences trades so that carpentry, plaster, paint, and dressing do not queue
on each other, keeps a floating crew for changes, and tells the PD *early* when
a design cannot be built for the money. Mediocre coordination delivers on the
shooting morning with painters still working, which costs lighting time on
every day of that block, or delivers a set that cannot take a lighting rig.

## 9. Costume department

| Role | Reports to | What they own |
|---|---|---|
| **Costume Designer** | Director (with PD) | The characters' clothing as storytelling: silhouette, palette, period, wear. Designs, sources, and supervises manufacture. |
| **Assistant Costume Designer** | Costume designer | Research, sourcing, fittings, breakdown by character. |
| **Costume Supervisor** | Costume designer | Budget, crew, workroom, hires, continuity paperwork, multiples. |
| **Key Costumer / Set Costumer(s)** | Supervisor | On-set dressing, continuity, and repairs during shooting. |
| **Cutter / Fitter / Tailor / Seamstress** | Supervisor | Manufacture and alteration. |
| **Ager / Dyer / Breakdown Artist** | Supervisor | Distressing, dyeing, blood, wear — the difference between "costume" and "clothes someone lives in". |
| **Buyer** | Supervisor | Purchasing and hire from costume houses. |
| **Background Costumer** | Supervisor | Dressing crowds, often hundreds a day. |
| **Costume Truck / Trailer Costumer** | Supervisor | Running the mobile workroom at unit base. |

### 9.1 Costume Designer

**Reports to** the director, working closely with the PD. **Commands** the
costume supervisor and through them a workroom, set costumers, agers, and
buyers — 5 to 60 people. **Coordinates with** the actors (fittings are a
performance conversation as much as a technical one), the DP (how a fabric
behaves under this light and on this sensor), the PD (palette), hair and
makeup (the whole character silhouette), and stunts (doubles must match, and
costume must be safe to fall in).

**Joins** 8–14 weeks out. **Leaves** at wrap; the supervisor stays for returns.

**The week, in prep.** Break the script down by character, scene, and day, so
that every appearance of every character has a defined costume and a change
number. Research. Design and present to the director. Then source: buy, hire
from costume houses, or make. Run fittings — the point at which the actor first
sees the character physically, and where many performance decisions are
actually made. Age and break down everything so it looks lived in. Ensure
**multiples** exist for stunts, doubles, and anything that gets wet, torn, or
bloodied.

**What separates a good costume designer from a mediocre one**

1. **Character, not period.** A good designer dresses a specific person with a
   history and an economic reality; a mediocre one dresses the decade.
2. **Silhouette and palette discipline** across the ensemble, so characters
   read instantly and separately in a wide shot, and so the film's colour logic
   holds.
3. **Designing for the arc.** Clothing that changes as the character changes,
   deliberately, and that the audience registers without noticing.
4. **Making actors comfortable and confident.** Fittings that build the
   character's physicality; costumes an actor can actually move, fight, and sit
   in for a 12-hour day.
5. **Multiples planning.** The single biggest logistical failure mode: one
   shirt for a scene that involves blood, rain, or a fall.
6. **How it photographs.** Fabrics that moiré on a digital sensor, colours that
   fluoresce under LED, whites that clip, greens near a green screen.
7. **Budget shape.** Knowing when to spend on the hero coat and when to hire
   the background.

**How failure shows**

| Failure | Where it shows |
|---|---|
| Insufficient multiples | Scene stops; a day can be lost drying or replacing a costume |
| Costume unusable for action | The stunt is redesigned around the clothes, or the double is visibly different |
| Wrong texture/colour for the format | Moiré, clipping, or a colour that fights the grade in every shot |
| Ensemble reads flat | Characters indistinguishable in wides; the film looks cheap |
| Fittings late or rushed | The actor meets the character on the shooting day |
| Aging/breakdown skipped | Everything looks like it came off a rack, which reads as fake instantly |

### 9.2 Costume Supervisor and set costumers

The supervisor is the department's UPM: budget, crew, workroom, hires, and the
continuity paperwork. **Set costumers** are on the floor all day, holding the
continuity of what is worn, how it is fastened, whether the tie is loosened
yet, and repairing damage between takes. Their failure appears directly in the
cut as a collar that changes across a cut, and it is one of the continuity
errors audiences most reliably notice.

| Role | Reports to | What they own |
|---|---|---|
| **Makeup Department Head** | Director/Producer | Makeup design for all characters, crew, schedule, continuity. |
| **Key Makeup Artist / Makeup Artists** | Dept head | Application and on-set touch-ups. |
| **Hair Department Head / Key Hair Stylist / Hair Stylists** | Director/Producer | Hair design, wigs, facial hair, continuity. |
| **Personal Makeup/Hair Artist** | Contracted to a star | Approval-driven; a star's own artist, paid by production. |
| **Prosthetic / Special Makeup Effects Designer & Technicians** | Director/Producer | Life-casts, sculpts, appliances, creature makeup, injuries, ageing. A separate craft, often an outside shop (see `08-vfx-and-specialty.md`). |

Application time drives call times: a full prosthetic makeup can require the
actor on set 4–6 hours before the crew call.

### 10.1 Makeup Department Head / Hair Department Head

Two parallel heads (sometimes combined in the UK as a **hair and makeup
designer**), with the same shape of job.

**Report to** the director, with producer approval and, on star-driven films,
the actor's approval. **Command** keys, artists, and stylists — 4 to 30 people,
more with prosthetics. **Coordinate with** costume (the character is one
silhouette), the DP (skin under this light and this sensor), the 2nd AD (the
morning pipeline is entirely theirs to hold), the script supervisor
(continuity), and stunts (doubles must match).

**Join** 4–8 weeks out; earlier if prosthetics or wigs must be made — a
bespoke lace-front wig or a set of appliances is a 4–10 week lead item.
**Leave** at wrap.

**The day.** Start before everyone: on a 6am crew call the makeup trailer is
running at 4am, and on a prosthetic day at 1am. Get every actor camera-ready by
their set time. Then hold it — on set all day, watching the monitor, touching
up sweat, shine, blood, and dirt between takes, and maintaining continuity
across scenes shot weeks apart.

**What separates a good makeup/hair head from a mediocre one**

1. **Speed without visible haste.** The entire day's start time depends on the
   trailer's throughput. A head who can turn six actors in 90 minutes rather
   than 150 gives the unit an earlier first shot every single day.
2. **Designing to the camera and format.** Modern sensors and large formats
   are unforgiving; makeup that reads correctly at 4K in close-up is a
   different discipline from stage or beauty work.
3. **Continuity across a scrambled schedule.** Blood, dirt, injuries, sweat,
   and hair length must match across takes shot 40 days apart. Photographic
   continuity references, taken religiously, are the tell of a good department.
4. **Handling actors.** They are the first person an actor sees each day and
   the person closest to them physically. Good heads manage nerves, exhaustion,
   and star temperament and quietly tell the 1st AD what is coming.
5. **Prosthetic and effects judgement.** Knowing what to build practically and
   what to leave to VFX — a bad appliance is worse than nothing and cannot be
   removed in post.
6. **Managing the personal-artist politics.** Stars bring their own artists;
   the head must integrate them without losing control of the design or the
   department's continuity.

**How failure shows.** The day starts 40 minutes late, every day. Continuity
breaks that show in the cut (a cut that changes a wound, a hairline, or the
amount of sweat). Makeup that reads as makeup in close-up. Or a prosthetic that
takes an hour longer than promised, which pushes the actor's call to a time
that breaches turnaround (`06-…` §7) and costs the production a penalty and a
late start.

## 11. Sound department (production sound)

| Role | Reports to | What they own |
|---|---|---|
| **Production Sound Mixer** | Director/Producer | Recording usable dialogue: mic choice, levels, multitrack routing, sound reports. Their failure becomes ADR (`07-postproduction.md`). |
| **Boom Operator** | Mixer | Mic placement — a craft of getting close without entering frame or shadow. |
| **Sound Utility / 2nd Assistant Sound** | Mixer | Radio mics, cabling, playback, timecode sync. |

Note the whole production sound department is often three people even on a
$200M film, which is why "we'll fix it in post" is so frequently the outcome.

### 11.1 Production Sound Mixer

The most structurally under-resourced head of department on a film: a
three-person crew responsible for half of the finished film's sensory
information, working in an environment optimised entirely for the picture.

**Reports to** the director and producer — notably *not* to the DP, which
means sound has no advocate inside the camera/lighting bloc that creates most
of its problems. **Commands** the boom operator and utility. **Coordinates
with** the 1st AD (asking for quiet, and for a wild track), costume (where a
radio mic can be hidden in that fabric), the script supervisor (take numbering
and circled takes), locations (noise assessment before a location is booked),
and post sound (`07-…` §3), who inherit everything.

**Joins** 1–3 weeks before photography — one of the last HODs hired, and one of
the ones most damaged by a late start, because location sound problems are
cheapest to solve in prep. **Leaves** at wrap; sometimes returns for ADR
reference or wild lines.

**The day.** Choose and place microphones for each setup: a boom overhead or
underneath, radio mics on the actors, and plants hidden in the set. Route and
record a multitrack — every mic on its own track plus a mix — with timecode
synced to camera. Ride levels through takes with unpredictable actor volume.
Log every take on a **sound report**. Fight, continuously and politely, for
conditions: a generator moved, an air conditioner switched off, a road held, a
costume rustle solved, a lamp that hums replaced. Record **wild tracks** and
room tone the moment the 1st AD will give them 60 seconds.

**What separates a good production sound mixer from a mediocre one**

1. **Getting clean dialogue in conditions that do not permit it.** That is the
   whole job. The measure is what percentage of production dialogue survives to
   the final mix without ADR — a good mixer keeps ADR to the handful of lines
   that were always going to need it; a mediocre one hands post a film where a
   quarter of the dialogue must be re-recorded.
2. **Micing invisibly and reliably.** Radio mics that do not rustle, do not
   drop out, and cannot be seen; booms that get within a foot of the actor
   without entering frame or throwing a shadow.
3. **Anticipating post's needs.** Isolated tracks, clean room tone for every
   location, wild lines while the actor is still in character and available,
   consistent track layout and honest reports.
4. **Political skill on a set that does not prioritise them.** The mixer must
   ask 150 people to stop what they are doing. The good ones get quiet by being
   right, brief, and never crying wolf; the mediocre ones either give up (and
   record unusable audio without saying so) or become the person the 1st AD
   stops listening to.
5. **Location literacy in prep.** Attending the tech scout and saying, before
   the location is signed, that the flight path overhead will make every take
   ADR (`04-preproduction.md` §5).
6. **Honest reporting.** A mixer who says "that take is unusable" in the moment
   saves post ten times the cost of doing it again now.

**How failure shows**

| Failure | Where it shows |
|---|---|
| Dialogue unusable | ADR: expensive, schedules a star months later, and almost always a worse performance |
| Mic in frame / boom shadow | The take is thrown away, or a VFX paint-out is paid for |
| Radio mic rustle or dropout | The take survives only on the boom, if there is one |
| No room tone / wild tracks | The sound editors build the scene from nothing; audible seams in the mix |
| Poor reports and track layout | Days lost in post sound conforming and searching |
| Failing to flag a bad take | Discovered in the cutting room weeks later, when the set is gone |

### 11.2 Boom Operator

A physical craft: holding a microphone at arm's length, overhead, for twelve
hours, inches outside a frame they must know precisely, following dialogue
between moving actors without casting a shadow into a lighting setup they had
no say in. A good boom operator knows the lens and the frame line better than
most of the crew, memorises the dialogue so the mic leads the speaker rather
than chasing them, and moves in the same choreography as the dolly. Almost
every "we'll fix it in post" that turns out fine was fixed by a boom operator
who got close enough.

## 12. Locations department

| Role | Reports to | What they own |
|---|---|---|
| **Location Manager** | UPM/Producer | Finding, securing, permitting, and paying for locations; neighbour relations; the unit's footprint. |
| **Assistant Location Manager / Key Assistant** | Location manager | Day-of running of the location. |
| **Location Scout** | Location manager | Finding options. |
| **Unit Manager / Location Assistants** | Location manager | Parking, basecamp layout, facilities, cleanliness, security. |

### 12.1 Location Manager

The process of finding and securing locations is covered in depth in
`04-preproduction.md` §5, including the sequence, the tech scout, and the
skill axis. What follows is the crew-side view: the location manager as a
department head during the shoot.

**Reports to** the UPM on money and permissions, and to the director and DP on
suitability. In the US, location managers are usually Teamsters. **Commands**
assistant location managers, scouts, and unit assistants — 3 to 12 people, more
on a film with many locations. **Coordinates with** transport (where 40 trucks
go), the UPM (fees and overages), the 1st AD (hours, curfews, and access
windows), sound (noise), the gaffer (power), and the local community.

**Joins** 12–16 weeks out, among the first three or four crew. **Leaves** after
the unit does: restoration, final payments, and complaint resolution can run
weeks past wrap.

**During the shoot**, the department's job shifts from finding to *holding*:
being on the location before the unit arrives and after it leaves, managing the
owner and the neighbours in real time, keeping the permit conditions, and
having the answer when the director asks at 3pm whether they can also shoot in
the building across the road. Simultaneously, the scouting half of the
department is three weeks ahead on locations that have not been secured yet,
because scripts change and locations fall through.

**Good vs mediocre, on the day** (see `04-…` §5.3 for the full axis): a good
location manager makes the location feel like a stage — the unit arrives,
parks, and shoots, and nobody spends the morning negotiating. The failure mode
that actually loses days is not a badly chosen location but a **relationship
that collapses mid-block**: an owner or a neighbour who withdraws cooperation
on day two of a four-day location, which is unrecoverable and expensive.

| **SFX Foreman / Technicians / Pyrotechnician** | SFX supervisor | Building and firing the effects. |

| **VFX Producer** | Producer | Bidding, awarding, scheduling and costing the vendors. |

### 13.1 Stunt Coordinator

**Reports to** the director and producer; on safety matters they have a
parallel authority that no one, including the director, can override.
**Commands** stunt performers, doubles, riggers, and specialist coordinators;
frequently also directs second unit (§16). **Coordinates with** the 1st AD
(action days are scheduled around resets), the DP and camera (how the action
will be photographed determines how it is performed), SFX (fire, cars, wire
rigs), costume (doubles must match and the costume must be survivable),
makeup/hair (matching doubles), and the insurer.

**Joins** 6–12 weeks out on an action film, earlier if previs is involved.
**Leaves** at wrap of the action days, though on tentpoles they may be present
throughout.

**The week, in prep.** Break the script's action down into gags. Design each
one and previs or videotape it. Cast doubles matched to principals for build,
height, and colouring. Rehearse — action is the only part of a film that gets
serious rehearsal time. Write risk assessments and safety plans and walk the
insurer and the production through them.

**The day.** Own the action sequence's tempo. Brief everyone, including crew
who are not involved, on the hazard and the stop procedure. Rehearse at low
speed, then at speed, then shoot — often with several cameras, because a gag
that takes three hours to reset must be captured comprehensively on each pass.
Stop the shot if any condition changes.

**What separates a good stunt coordinator from a mediocre one**

1. **Designing action that cuts.** Good coordinators design a sequence in
   shots — geography, direction, and impacts that will assemble — rather than
   a performance that is impressive live and incoherent in the edit.
2. **Making the actor look capable.** The best stunt work is invisible: the
   audience believes the star did it. That means doubles matched precisely,
   handoffs hidden, and the star's own performable portion designed to their
   real ability.
3. **Safety as a system, not a temperament.** Rehearsal, rigging redundancy,
   medical cover, and an explicit stop authority everyone understands. The
   industry's fatalities cluster around schedule pressure and skipped rehearsal.
4. **Resets and camera strategy.** Knowing how many cameras it takes to get a
   one-shot gag, and what a second take actually costs in hours.
5. **Honest scoping.** Telling the production in prep that the sequence as
   written is six days, not two — before the schedule is locked.
6. **Working with the performers.** Stunt performers take the risk; a
   coordinator who protects them, pays them properly, and never pressures them
   is one the best people work for.

**How failure shows.** Action that is incoherent in the cut, doubles that are
visibly not the actor, gags that take twice the scheduled days, and — the
category that defines the job — injuries and deaths, which stop the film,
trigger investigations, and end careers.

### 13.2 Special Effects (SFX) Supervisor

Practical, physical, on-set effects: weather, fire, explosions, breakaways,
mechanical rigs, atmospherics. Distinct from VFX, and increasingly valued
because a practical element photographs correctly and interacts with real
light for free.

**Reports to** the director and producer. **Commands** SFX foremen,
technicians, and licensed pyrotechnicians. **Coordinates with** stunts (most
big gags are joint), construction (breakaway sets are built to break), the DP
(an effect must be photographable and repeatable), VFX (what is practical
versus augmented), the 1st AD (reset times drive the schedule), and the fire
authority and safety officers.

**Joins** 6–12 weeks out — much of the department's work is fabrication in a
workshop weeks ahead. **Leaves** after their last effects day.

**Good vs mediocre.** The axis is **repeatability and reset time**. Anyone can
produce fire once; the value is in producing the *same* fire on take four, at
the moment the 1st AD calls for it, and being able to reset in 20 minutes
rather than three hours. Beyond that: effects that are scaled correctly for
the frame (rain that reads on camera is much heavier than real rain, and must
be back-lit to exist at all), atmosphere that is consistent across a scene's
coverage, and an absolute, unglamorous safety discipline around pyro and
firearms. Failure shows as days lost to resets, as effects that look like
effects, and as the most dangerous incidents on any set.

### 13.3 Visual Effects Supervisor

The only HOD whose department's real work happens after the shoot, which
makes their on-set job entirely about **not making the post work impossible**.

**Reports to** the director and producer; paired with a **VFX producer** who
owns money and vendor management. **Commands** an on-set VFX crew (data
wrangler, coordinator, sometimes a witness-camera unit) and, in post, the
vendor relationships that may involve a thousand artists. **Coordinates with**
the DP (plates, lenses, lighting reference), the PD (what is built versus
extended), stunts and SFX (practical versus digital), the editor (temps and
turnovers), and the 1st AD (VFX requirements per setup).

**Joins** in prep, sometimes during development on a VFX-driven film; often
12–20 weeks out. **Leaves** at final delivery, months after everyone else
(`07-postproduction.md` §5).

**The day, on set.** Ensure every VFX shot is photographed with what post will
need: correct plate, clean plate, tracking markers where they help and nowhere
they do not, HDRI and lighting reference, chrome and grey balls, lens and
camera metadata, measurements of the set, and witness cameras on interactive
elements. Advise the director on what is achievable and what it costs *while
the decision is still free*. Log every shot.

**What separates a good VFX supervisor from a mediocre one**

1. **Methodology choices in prep.** The largest cost lever in the whole film is
   deciding what is built, what is shot practically, and what is created — and
   it is decided before photography, not after.
2. **Set discipline over a crew that resents them.** The five minutes of data
   capture per setup that saves three weeks of vendor time is time the 1st AD
   does not want to give. Good supervisors take it without becoming the
   department that slows the day.
3. **Honest bidding and scope control.** Shot counts that grow from 600 to
   1,400 in post are the standard failure, and they are usually a supervision
   and turnover failure as much as a directorial one.
4. **Translating between director and vendor.** Directors give notes in
   feelings; vendors need specifications. Whoever does that badly generates
   iterations, and iterations are the unit of VFX cost.
5. **Knowing what looks wrong and why.** Integration — light, edges, motion
   blur, grain, and weight — rather than the asset itself, is what makes an
   effect read as real.

**How failure shows.** Missing plates and reference (a shot that has to be
re-photographed or solved expensively), shot counts and budgets that double,
turnovers that arrive late so vendors compress at premium rates, and — the
visible outcome — effects the audience notices as effects, which is the one
outcome the entire budget existed to prevent.

## 14. Editorial (during the shoot)

| Role | Reports to | What they own |
|---|---|---|
| **Editor** | Director (and studio) | Assembling the film. Present through the shoot, cutting as material arrives, so the director can see whether a scene works while the set still exists. |
| **First Assistant Editor** | Editor | Media management, sync, the cutting room's technical integrity, VFX turnovers, temp effects. |
| **Assistant Editors / Apprentice** | 1st AE | Volume of the above. |
| **Post-Production Supervisor** | Producer | Schedules, budgets, and coordinates all of post; the counterpart to the UPM. |

### 14.1 Editor

**Reports to** the director during the director's cut and to the studio
thereafter — a dual loyalty that is the defining political feature of the job
(`07-postproduction.md` §2).

**Commands** first and additional assistant editors, and in post also VFX
editors, temp sound work, and the music editor's coordination. **Coordinates
with** the script supervisor (the daily log is their instruction manual), the
DP and director via dailies, the VFX supervisor (turnovers), and the
post-production supervisor.

**Joins** at or just before the start of photography — occasionally in prep on
a complex film. **Leaves** at picture lock, months after the shoot; on a studio
film they are on the film longer than almost anyone except the producer.

**During the shoot.** Cut every day's material within a day or two of receiving
it, so that a scene can be checked while the set, cast, and location still
exist. Flag missing coverage to the director and 1st AD immediately — an
editor who reports on day 12 that scene 44 has no reaction shot can still get
it; on day 60 they cannot. Produce an assembly that is ready within days of
wrap (the DGA gives the editor a defined period for the editor's assembly, and
the director a defined period for the director's cut).

**What separates a good editor from a mediocre one**

1. **Performance selection.** The single highest-leverage judgement in post:
   which take, and which fraction of which take. Two editors given the same
   dailies produce films of measurably different quality, and the difference is
   mostly this.
2. **Rhythm.** Knowing how long a shot can hold, when to cut early, and how
   pace should vary across a two-hour structure. It is the least teachable and
   most decisive attribute.
3. **Structural courage.** Being willing to lose a scene — often the most
   expensive or best-shot scene — because the film is better without it, and
   being able to demonstrate that rather than argue it.
4. **Solving with what exists.** Great editors repair unshot material,
   performance problems, and confusing geography using reaction shots, sound,
   and reordering, without a reshoot.
5. **Managing the director.** The director has lived with the intended film for
   two years; the editor sees only what exists. Navigating that gap without
   becoming either a yes-man or an opponent is the job's real difficulty.
6. **Reading an audience.** Interpreting test screenings and previews correctly
   — what the audience felt, not what they said (`07-…` §9).
7. **Cutting room discipline.** Keeping the film's versions, turnovers, and
   temp elements coherent across a year of changes.

**How failure shows**

| Failure | Where it shows |
|---|---|
| Wrong performance selection | A flat film assembled from good material; unfixable by anything downstream |
| Slack pacing | Test scores drop, runtime bloats, reviews cite "overlong" (`16-…`) |
| Coverage gaps found late | Pickups and reshoots at full cost (`06-…` §10) |
| Structural cowardice | The film keeps the scene everyone knows should go |
| Losing the director's trust | The studio and director cut around them; sometimes an editor is replaced mid-post |
| Poor turnover hygiene | VFX vendors work on superseded cuts — pure wasted money |

### 14.2 First Assistant Editor

The cutting room's technical spine: ingest and sync every day's material,
maintain the project's integrity across a year, prepare and track VFX
turnovers, build temp sound and effects so the cut can be screened, and manage
versions so nobody ever screens the wrong one. **Good vs mediocre** is measured
in whether the editor ever waits and whether anything is ever lost: an
excellent 1st AE makes the cutting room invisible; a poor one costs the editor
hours a day and eventually costs the production a corrupted or mis-versioned
delivery.

### 14.3 Post-Production Supervisor

The UPM of post: schedule, budget, facilities, vendors, deliverables, and the
calendar that runs from wrap to release. They join in prep (the post schedule
has to be built before the shoot ends) and leave after final delivery. Good
ones protect the editor's and director's time from logistics, keep the vendor
chain honest, and never let a delivery date arrive with a deliverable
unstarted (`07-…` §7).

## 15. Support and welfare

| Role | What they own |
|---|---|
| **Catering** | Hot meals for the unit; a separate contractor. Crew morale is measurably a catering function. |
| **Craft Service** | Continuous snacks/drinks on set (a distinct job from catering in the US). |
| **Set Medic / Nurse** | On-set medical cover; mandatory on hazardous work. |
| **Health & Safety Advisor** | Risk assessments and compliance (a formal, statutory role in the UK). |
| **Security** | Site, cast, and script/asset security; anti-piracy and paparazzi control. |
| **Transportation Coordinator / Captain, Drivers** | Every vehicle: trucks, trailers, cast cars, picture vehicles. Teamsters in the US. |
| **Picture Car Coordinator** | Sourcing, preparing, and duplicating vehicles that appear on screen. |
| **Studio Teacher / Chaperone** | Legally required schooling and welfare for minors. |
| **Intimacy Coordinator** | Choreographing and consent-managing intimate scenes; now standard on studio productions. |
| **Dialect Coach, Acting Coach, Movement/Dance Choreographer, Music Coach** | Performance specialists attached to particular cast or sequences. |
| **Unit Publicist** | On-set press, EPK, set visits, and the publicity assets (`09-…`). |
| **Casting Director (+ Associate, Extras Casting)** | See `04-preproduction.md` §8. |

### 15.1 Transportation Coordinator

Frequently underestimated: on a location-heavy studio film, transport is one of
the largest crews on the picture (10–40 drivers) and moves 30–60 vehicles
between locations nightly. They report to the UPM, command a transport captain
and drivers, and coordinate with locations (where things park), the 1st AD
(when the unit moves), and every department that has a truck. A good
coordinator makes the company move happen inside the allotted window; a poor
one is the reason a "one hour" move takes two and a half, which is the single
most destructive routine time sink on a film (`06-…` §5).

## 16. Second unit

A **second unit** shoots material that doesn't need the principal cast or the
director: establishing shots, inserts, doubles, driving, large-scale action,
scenics. It has its own director, DP, ADs, camera, grip, and electric teams —
effectively a smaller parallel film.

- On tentpoles, second unit can shoot as many days as main unit, and the
  **second unit director is often the stunt coordinator**.
- The line between "second unit" and "additional photography" is contractual
  (DGA rules govern what the director must direct personally).
- **Splinter units** are a lighter version: two or three people (often the DP
  or an operator, a 1st AC, and a PA) grabbing inserts and pickups on the main
  unit's fringes, at almost no cost.
- The **coordination burden** falls on the main unit's 1st AD and the UPM: two
  units competing for the same locations, doubles, picture vehicles, and
  equipment is a scheduling problem that gets worse the more units there are.
- **Matching** is the quality risk. A second unit whose DP does not match the
  main unit's look produces material that is visibly cheaper, and it lands in
  the middle of the film's best sequences.

| Stunt performer | Scale + adjustments per gag, often multiples of scale |

Rule of thumb: **crew labour plus fringes is roughly a third of a
conventional live-action budget**, and shooting days are the unit in which it
is consumed — which is why every scheduling decision is a money decision.

### 17.1 What a crew member is actually paid

The headline rate is between a half and three-quarters of the real cost. The
components, in the order they appear on a deal memo:

| Component | Typical size | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Base rate** | The number quoted | Daily below the line; weekly for HODs and office staff |
| **Overtime** | 1.5x after 8–10 hrs, 2x after 12–14 | Structure varies by agreement; the reason a 14-hour day costs ~1.6 twelve-hour days |
| **6th/7th day** | 1.5x / 2x | A six-day week is materially more than 6/5 of a five-day one |
| **Fringes** | +20–35% of gross | Pension, health, payroll taxes, workers' comp — invisible to the crew, unavoidable for the production |
| **Kit / equipment rental** | $50–$1,000+/day | For crew who supply their own gear: sound mixers, DITs, makeup, script supervisors, hair, props |
| **Box rental** | $25–$150/week | Consumables and small tools |
| **Car allowance / mileage** | $25–$75/day | Common for departments that run errands |
| **Per diem** | $50–$85/day | Non-local hires; untaxed in most structures |
| **Housing and travel** | Rent + flights + travel days | Travel days are usually paid at a partial rate |
| **Meal penalty** | Escalating per half hour | A production that skips a meal break pays the whole crew, repeatedly |
| **Turnaround / forced call** | Penalty payments | See `06-…` §7 — an entire day's pay can be triggered by a nine-hour turnaround |

A rough conversion: a $700/day grip with fringes, box rental, and overtime on a
typical 12.5-hour day costs a production **$1,050–$1,250**. Multiply by 150.

### 17.2 What actually drives an individual's rate

Rates are not a scale with experience along one axis. Six independent factors
set what a person can charge:

1. **Tier and credit history.** The strongest single factor. A DP whose last
   three films were studio releases charges a multiple of an equally skilled DP
   whose last three were independents — the rate follows the *scale of picture*
   the person has proven they can carry, not their competence.
2. **Union agreement and local.** In the US, IATSE rates are set by the
   relevant local's agreement, which varies by region and by the budget tier
   of the picture: most agreements have **low-budget tiers** with materially
   lower minimums. The same job in Los Angeles, Atlanta, and Albuquerque has
   three different floors.
3. **Market heat.** Rates move with demand. In a busy year with several
   tentpoles shooting in one region, experienced HODs are scarce and rates rise
   20–40% above the nominal going rate; in a slow year they collapse toward
   scale.
4. **Show length and guarantee.** A 16-week guaranteed engagement is worth
   accepting a lower rate for; a two-week job commands a premium because it
   costs the crew member the ability to take a longer one. **Dailies** (day
   hires) are the highest rate per day and the worst job.
5. **Kit.** Some crafts effectively rent themselves a business: a sound mixer's
   package can add $500–$1,000/day, a DIT's cart similar, a makeup head's kit a
   few hundred. Two candidates at the same rate are not the same cost, and a
   crew member with a good kit is genuinely cheaper than renting it separately.
6. **Distance and difficulty.** Distant location, night shoots, hazardous work,
   and long hours are all priced — sometimes as a rate bump, more often as per
   diem, housing, and penalty structure.

Two conventions matter more than they look. **Quotes work like talent quotes**
(`12-talent-labor-and-careers.md` §2): a crew member's last rate sets their
next floor, so taking a low rate has a durable cost and is negotiated with a
**"favoured nations" or rate-protection letter** to avoid setting a precedent.
And **rates compound across a department**: an HOD's rate is a small line, but
the crew size, kit, and equipment package they bring is a large one, which is
why a UPM's real negotiation is over the department's shape, not the head's
day rate.

## 18. Credit order

Main titles (opening) and end crawl order is contractually determined, but the
conventional main-title order is:

> Studio → production company → "A Film By" (if granted) → principal cast →
> casting → music → costume design → editor → production design →
> cinematography → executive producers → producers → writer(s) → director

The director's credit is last in main titles, and the writer's immediately
before it. These positions are guild-protected and are among the most fought
over items in any deal.

### 18.1 Why position matters at all

Screen credit is one of the few durable assets a film career accumulates. It is
the evidence in a quote negotiation, the qualification for guild membership and
for awards eligibility, and the thing agents cite. Because the number of
prominent positions is fixed and the number of people who want them is not,
credit is negotiated as hard as money — and unlike money, it is public and
permanent.

The specific things being negotiated are rarely just order:

| Term | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Placement** | Main titles vs end crawl. Main titles is the prize; most crew are in the crawl |
| **Card** | A single card (only you on screen) vs a shared card with one to three others |
| **Position** | First position on a shared card; last position before the director |
| **Size and duration** | Expressed as a percentage of the title's type size and a minimum on-screen time |
| **Paid ad credit** | Whether the credit appears in the **billing block** on posters and trailers — a separate, harder-won right |
| **Form of credit** | The exact wording: "A Film By", "Directed by", "Produced by", "In Association With" |
| **Separated rights / possessory** | The possessory credit ("A [Director] Film") is a WGA flashpoint and is not automatic |

### 18.2 How it is actually negotiated

- **Business affairs papers it, agents drive it.** Credit terms sit in the deal
  from the start and are usually settled before the person starts work,
  because retro-fitting a credit means re-opening every other credit around it.
- **Everything is relative.** A term like "no less favourable than any other
  producer" or "immediately following the editor" ties one person's credit to
  another's, and a single change late in the process cascades through the whole
  block. This is why a credit list can take weeks of lawyering to close.
- **Guild determination overrides negotiation** for the credits the guilds
  control. The WGA determines writing credits by its own arbitration process,
  not by who was hired (`02-development.md` §7), and the Producers Guild
  arbitrates the "p.g.a." mark and the capped Best Picture eligibility.
- **The MPA and studio rules cap the billing block**, so paid ad credit is
  genuinely scarce: there is a limited number of names on a poster, and every
  one of them was fought for.
- **The end crawl is negotiated too**, but by department: the order is
  conventional, and the fights are within departments — whether the on-set
  dresser precedes the swing gang, whether a personal assistant appears at all.
- **Vanity and consequence are hard to separate.** Some credit fights are pure
  ego, and some are worth real money later: a producer credit determines
  whether a person is eligible for a Best Picture Oscar, and a "co-producer"
  instead of "producer" can end a career-defining claim.

### 18.3 Conventional end-crawl order

Roughly: principal cast (in order of appearance or billing) → the rest of the
cast → stunts → production department → AD department → camera → grip →
electric → art → set decoration → props → construction → costume → hair and
makeup → sound → locations → transport → catering and support → editorial →
post-production → music → VFX vendors (which on a large film may run for
several minutes and thousands of names) → second unit → legal, insurance, and
completion bond → shooting locations and thanks → music cue licences → the
studio and guild logos and the copyright notice.

The crawl's length has grown steadily: a 1970s studio film's credits ran two to
three minutes; a modern VFX tentpole's run eight to twelve, largely because
of vendor crews.

## 20. How departments interact, and where the friction is

Most lost time on a film is not a department failing at its own job. It is two
departments needing the same thing — usually the same 40 minutes, or the same
piece of floor — and the friction is structural, predictable, and the same on
every film.

### 20.1 The standing frictions

| Between | The friction | How it is resolved when it works |
|---|---|---|
| **Camera and grip/electric** | Camera can shoot the moment the block is done; lighting needs 30–90 minutes. Every setup is a negotiation over that gap | The DP pre-lights broadly, the gaffer lights for the reverse in advance, and the 1st AD gives camera something else to do |
| **Art and camera** | The PD builds a world; the DP shoots 8% of it, from angles the design did not anticipate. Sets without wild walls or ceiling access cost lighting time every day | Camera attends the design reviews and says where the lens will be, before the set is built |
| **Set decoration and camera/lighting** | The lamp has to go where the sofa is; the practical fixture in shot is dressing but is also the key light | The set decorator and gaffer agree practicals in prep; the on-set dresser stays on the floor to move things fast |
| **Sound and everyone** | Sound needs quiet, and every other department makes noise: generators, wind machines, dolly track, radios, a hundred people. Sound also needs the boom in a space camera and lighting have filled | The 1st AD enforces quiet, the mixer asks rarely and accurately, and the gaffer/genny op site noisy kit deliberately |
| **Costume/makeup and the AD department** | The trailer needs the actor for 90 minutes; the floor wants them now. Every morning is this argument | The 2nd AD builds an honest pipeline; the department gives honest times rather than optimistic ones |
| **Stunts/SFX and the 1st AD** | Action needs rehearsal and reset time the schedule resents; the schedule needs pages | Action days are scheduled as action days, with realistic resets and multiple cameras |
| **VFX and the floor** | VFX needs five minutes of data capture per setup that nobody wants to give, plus markers and clean plates | Requirements go on the call sheet as an item, not as a request made at the moment of the shot |
| **Construction and the schedule** | Sets are needed before they are ready; the shoot reorders around the build | The 1st AD schedules from the construction sequence, and construction commits to dates two days before shooting |
| **Locations and transport** | Where 40 trucks park determines whether the unit is walking ten minutes to set all day | The tech scout resolves parking and base with both departments present |
| **Editorial and the shoot** | The editor sees what the film actually is before the director does, and must say so without undermining them | Dailies notes go to the director privately and coverage gaps go to the 1st AD immediately |
| **Props and set decoration** | The line between "dressing" and "prop" is whether an actor touches it — and a director changes that during the block | Both departments carry the boundary items; the on-set dresser and standby props stand together |

### 20.2 The chains that must not break

Three handoff chains run through every department and are where cross-
departmental failure is most expensive:

- **The continuity chain.** Script supervisor → costume, hair/makeup, props,
  set dec → editor. A break shows in the finished film and cannot be repaired
  cheaply.
- **The data chain.** Camera → 2nd AC's reports → DIT → data wrangler →
  backup → editorial → VFX. Every link is a point of total loss, and the only
  defence is redundant copies verified by checksum before any card is wiped.
- **The information chain.** Script revision → POC distribution → HODs →
  crew → call sheet. A department working from superseded pages builds the
  wrong thing, and the cost is discovered on the day.

### 20.3 The meetings that hold it together

| Meeting | When | Who | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Concept/design reviews** | Prep, weekly | Director, PD, DP, producer | Agreeing the film's look before money is committed |
| **Tech scout / recce** | Prep, per location | Every HOD | Surfacing disagreement while it is still free (`04-…` §5.2) |
| **Production meeting** | ~1 week before shooting | Every HOD, scene by scene | Discovering that two departments understood the script differently |
| **Safety meetings** | Per hazardous sequence | 1st AD, stunts, SFX, safety, the crew involved | Legal requirement and actual function |
| **Daily HOD huddle** | Each morning on set | 1st AD + department keys | Confirming the day's order and what each needs |
| **Dailies** | Daily, during the shoot | Director, DP, editor, producer, PD | Confirming what exists (`06-…` §9) |
| **Weekly cost meeting** | Weekly | Producer, UPM, accountant | Where the film's money actually is |

## 21. Crew culture and what "a good crew" means

Producers talk about "a good crew" as if it were a mood. It is not: it is a set
of behaviours with measurable output, and it is the reason two productions with
identical budgets and schedules deliver different numbers of pages per day.

### 21.1 What a good crew concretely does

1. **Works one step ahead.** The next setup is being prepared while the current
   one is shooting. This alone is worth 45–90 minutes a day.
2. **Solves at the lowest level.** A good crew fixes things laterally — the
   grip and the spark sort it between themselves in 30 seconds — instead of
   escalating to two HODs and the 1st AD.
3. **Tells the truth about time.** "Twenty minutes" means twenty minutes. A
   crew that habitually under-quotes destroys the 1st AD's ability to plan.
4. **Protects the actors.** Quiet when the actor is working up to something,
   no waiting in costume in the cold, no conversation in eyeline.
5. **Stops unsafe work.** In a healthy crew, anyone can say stop, and nobody is
   punished for it. This is the clearest single indicator of a well-run set.
6. **Has been together before.** Departments that have done five films together
   have a shared shorthand that is worth a measurable percentage of the day.
7. **Doesn't gossip destructively upward.** Crews always know first — whether
   the director is lost, whether the film is in trouble. A crew that keeps
   working anyway is worth its weight; one that has decided the film is dead
   works to rule.

### 21.2 What produces it, and what wrecks it

| Input | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Reasonable hours and honoured turnaround** | The largest single driver. Exhausted crews are slow, error-prone, and unsafe |
| **Food** | Meals on time, good and hot. Cheap catering is the most reliably false economy in production |
| **Being paid correctly and on time** | Payroll errors poison a crew faster than almost anything |
| **Clear leadership from the 1st AD and HODs** | A crew that knows the plan moves; one that doesn't stands around |
| **A director who decides** | Indecision at the top idles 150 people and is felt as disrespect |
| **Respect down the ladder** | How the least senior person is spoken to sets the whole set's temperature |
| **Chronic overtime and 6-day weeks** | Short-term pages, medium-term errors, injuries and turnover |
| **A film everyone can see is bad** | Morale collapse; the crew stops volunteering ideas, which is where much of a film's texture comes from |

### 21.3 Where crew quality actually shows in the film

The output of a good crew is not visible as "good crew work". It is visible as:

- **More coverage per day**, which gives the editor options and therefore a
  better cut.
- **More takes of the scenes that matter**, because time was saved on the
  scenes that didn't.
- **Fewer compromises at 6pm** — the last scene of the day shot properly rather
  than in one wide.
- **Texture**: the detail nobody asked for that a department added because they
  cared — a piece of dressing, a bit of business, a light in the right place.
- **Fewer unusable takes**, from focus, sound, continuity, and camera error.
- **Safety**, which is the only outcome that is measured in something other
  than money.

## 22. Failure signatures: which department a problem points to

A diagnostic index. When a specific problem appears on screen or in the
schedule, it usually has a short list of possible origins.

| What you see | Most likely origin |
|---|---|
| Scene plays flat, good material | Editor's selection (§14.1); or director's coverage (§4.1) |
| Scene has no reaction shot at the key moment | Script supervisor did not flag it (§3.4); 1st AD lost the day (§4.2) |
| Soft focus on the best take | 1st AC (§5.3) |
| Dialogue obviously re-recorded | Production sound (§11.1), or a location signed off despite noise (`04-…` §5) |
| Boom in frame or a boom shadow | Sound and camera not coordinated (§20.1) |
| The world looks like a set | Production design (§8.1), scenic paint, or set decoration (§8.3) |
| The period looks approximate | Costume (§9.1), props (§8.4), or set decoration |
| Costume changes across a cut | Set costumer / script supervisor (§9.2, §3.4) |
| Everyone looks made up | Makeup head (§10.1) |
| Action is impressive but incomprehensible | Stunt coordinator's shot design (§13.1) or the editor |
| Effects read as effects | VFX supervisor's integration and methodology (§13.3) |
| Geography of a scene is confusing | Screen direction — script supervisor and director |
| Last scene of the day looks cheaper | 1st AD, DP speed, or a schedule that was never achievable |
| Film runs 20 minutes long | Editor and director; script supervisor's timings should have warned in week two |
| Film over budget with nothing on screen | UPM and 1st AD: money consumed by days rather than by production value |
| Crew turnover mid-shoot | UPM's deals, hours, or a hostile department head (§21.2) |
| Incentive rebate reduced | Production accountant's coding and documentation (§3.3) |

The general rule the index encodes: **almost nothing visible on screen has a
single cause**, and almost everything visible on screen was decided by someone
whose name is in the crawl, weeks before the camera turned.
