# 16 — Critics, Reviews & Word of Mouth

*How a film's reputation forms, and what it does to the box office.* Reception
is not one thing: critics, aggregate scores, opening-night audiences, and
ordinary word of mouth are four different instruments measuring four different
populations, on four different clocks.

> **Domain reference.** Real industry, not this game. See `README.md`.

---

## 1. The four instruments

| Instrument | Measures | When | Predicts |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Critics / reviews** | Professional critical opinion | 1–14 days before release (or from a festival, months earlier) | Adult and prestige performance; awards viability |
| **Aggregate scores** (Rotten Tomatoes, Metacritic) | Consensus, compressed to a number | With the reviews | Perceived quality; used as a marketing asset |
| **Exit polling** (CinemaScore, PostTrak) | The paying opening-night audience | Opening night | **Legs** — better than anything else |
| **Word of mouth** | Everyone else, talking | Days 2 onward, compounding | The multiple, and the film's life |

The single most useful distinction: **critics predict prestige, audiences
predict money.** They correlate loosely and diverge systematically by genre.

### 1.1 The four clocks

Each instrument runs on its own clock, and confusing them is the most common
analytical error in reception:

```
months out        2 wks      Thu     Fri        Sat/Sun    Wk2      Wk3+   Yrs
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
festival reviews  ▓▓▓▓
tracking          ░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░
fan sentiment     ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓░░░░░░
review embargo               ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓
previews/presales                    ▓▓▓
CinemaScore/PostTrak                        ▓▓▓
word of mouth                               ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓░░░
awards standing                                          ░░░░░░▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓
reappraisal                                                          ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓
```

Practical consequences of the offsets:

- Reviews land **before** anyone has paid, so they act on the decision to buy.
- Exit polling lands **after** the opening audience has already paid, so it
  cannot change opening weekend; it only forecasts what happens next.
- Word of mouth is the only instrument that **compounds** — every day's
  audience becomes the next day's transmitters.
- Reappraisal (§11) operates on a decade scale and is invisible at release,
  but it is what sets long-run library value.

### 1.2 The instruments that are not in the table

Three adjacent measurements are routinely mistaken for reception and are not:

- **Tracking** (NRG and similar) measures *awareness and interest before
  release* — it is a marketing instrument, not a reception one
  (`09-marketing-and-distribution.md` §8). Tracking says who intends to come;
  reception says what they thought.
- **Test screenings** measure a recruited, non-paying audience against a film
  that can still be changed (`07-postproduction.md` §9). Their scores use a
  different scale and are not comparable to exit polling.
- **Fan sentiment** — the pre-release opinion of a self-selected, highly
  engaged population — is a genuinely distinct force with its own dynamics
  (§12), and is a bad proxy for the general audience.

## 2. Who critics are

The population is more stratified than the word "critic" suggests. Five tiers
do materially different jobs, and only some of them move anything.

| Tier | Examples of type | Reads for | Actual influence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Trade critics** | Variety, The Hollywood Reporter, Deadline, Screen International | The industry: buyers, distributors, exhibitors, awards voters | High on acquisitions and awards narrative; near zero on ticket sales |
| **National press** | Major daily papers, weeklies, national magazines | A general readership | High for specialty and prestige; the review of record |
| **Elite critical** | Long-form magazines, film journals, established individual voices | Cinephiles and other critics | Low immediate, high on canon formation (§11) |
| **Online / genre / specialist** | Film sites, genre outlets, established newsletters, Substacks | Enthusiast audiences by niche | Decisive within a niche — horror and animation especially |
| **Broadcast, video, social** | TV segments, YouTube, TikTok reviewers, podcasts | A mass consumer audience | Largest raw reach; consumer advice more than criticism |

- **Trade critics** review from festivals and early screenings and write for
  *the industry*: their reviews assess commercial prospects as much as
  quality, and they are read by buyers and distributors deciding what to
  acquire (`09-…` §10). A trade rave out of Sundance moves an acquisition
  price by millions; it does not sell a ticket.
- **National press critics** are fewer than they used to be; staff film-critic
  positions have been cut heavily over two decades.
- **Online and specialist critics** are the largest population by number,
  ranging from serious criticism to aggregation fodder.
- **Aggregator-approved critics** — Rotten Tomatoes and Metacritic each
  maintain an approved list, and admission to it is what makes a critic count
  toward the score. This is a real gatekeeping power (§2.4).

**Critics' groups** (New York Film Critics Circle, LA Film Critics
Association, National Society of Film Critics) vote awards in December and
shape the awards conversation without predicting the Academy well
(`13-awards-and-critical-reception.md` §4).

### 2.1 What a critic's week actually looks like

The job is far more procedural than the finished prose suggests.

| Stage | What happens | Who is involved |
|---|---|---|
| **Assignment** | An editor allocates the week's releases; a lead critic takes the two or three that matter, freelancers take the rest | Arts/film editor, lead critic, freelance pool |
| **Invitation** | The distributor's publicist (or an outside PR firm) issues screening invitations to a list they control | Studio publicity, outside unit/press agency |
| **RSVP and vetting** | Publicists check the list against outlet, circulation, and past behaviour; seats at tentpole screenings are scarce and allocated | Publicity coordinator |
| **Screening** | 10am or 7pm, studio screening room or a booked commercial house; phones bagged or surrendered; watermarked print; a security guard for tentpoles | Critic, publicist, security |
| **Note-taking** | In the dark, in a notebook, largely unreadably; some outlets forbid laptops and all forbid phones | Critic |
| **Filing** | 600–1,200 words for a daily, 1,500–3,000 for a magazine, filed hours to days later | Critic |
| **Editing** | Sub-editing, headline, star rating (which the critic often does not choose) | Section editor, sub |
| **Embargo hold** | The piece sits ready, scheduled to publish at the embargo minute | Editor, CMS |
| **Publication and aggregation** | Publishes; RT/Metacritic scrape or receive it within hours | Aggregator staff |

Details that outsiders consistently get wrong:

- **The star rating is frequently not the critic's.** On many outlets the
  editor sets it, or house style forces a four-point scale onto an opinion
  that does not fit it. This is the single largest source of noise in
  aggregate scores (§4.3).
- **The headline is never the critic's.** Aggregators and readers both react
  to headlines the writer did not choose.
- **Volume is punishing.** A working freelance critic may see 250–400 films a
  year and file on most of them. Reviewing at that rate is a craft of
  comparison, which is why critics reward the unfamiliar: they have seen the
  familiar hundreds of times, and the audience has not.
- **Deadlines shape verdicts.** A review filed at 1am for an 8am embargo is a
  first reaction. Long-lead critics have weeks to think, which is why monthly
  magazine reviews are systematically more considered — and often warmer to
  difficult films — than same-night filings.

### 2.2 The economics, and why the landscape collapsed

This is not trivia; it changed the shape of reception.

| Era | Structure | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **1960s–80s** | Staff critics at every metropolitan daily and alt-weekly; a small number of nationally famous voices | A handful of critics could make a specialty film; reviews were local and geographically distributed |
| **1980s–90s** | Mass-market television criticism reaches tens of millions weekly; the thumbs-up as a marketing asset | Criticism at its widest consumer reach; a positive verdict was directly sellable |
| **2000s** | Newspaper advertising revenue collapses; film-critic posts are among the first cut; alt-weeklies fold | Staff positions become freelance; the local critic disappears |
| **2010s–20s** | Freelance-dominated, aggregator-mediated, plus a large creator economy on video and podcasts | Individual critics matter less; the *number* matters more, because it is a score |

Working economics, directionally:

- A freelance review pays roughly **$50–$400**; a few prestige outlets pay
  more. Almost nobody supports themselves on reviews alone.
- The realistic living is **staff position at a surviving outlet**,
  **criticism plus another job** (teaching, programming, books, festival
  work), or **an audience-owned channel** — newsletter, podcast, YouTube —
  where the income is subscriptions and advertising rather than commissions.
- Travel to festivals is usually **self-funded** for freelancers, which
  quietly determines whose voices are heard on the films that premiere there.
- Junket travel and accommodation is usually **paid by the distributor**,
  which is exactly why serious outlets refuse it and why the outlets that
  accept it produce reliably softer coverage (§3.6).

The net effect on reception: **criticism lost its individual gatekeepers and
gained a collective one.** No single critic can make or break a film the way
one could in 1975. The aggregate number can.

### 2.3 Which outlets actually move something

Influence is not evenly distributed and is not proportional to reach.

| Outlet type | Moves | Does not move |
|---|---|---|
| Major national paper's lead critic | Specialty and arthouse attendance in that city; awards conversation | Wide-release opening weekend |
| Trade (Variety/THR/Screen) | Acquisition prices, festival sales, awards narrative, exhibitor confidence | Consumer demand |
| Elite long-form (magazines, journals) | Canon and reappraisal over years (§11) | Anything in week one |
| Large genre site | Horror, action, and animation enthusiast turnout | Adult drama |
| Large video/social reviewer | Awareness and teenage/young-adult first choice | Awards, prestige |
| Any single approved critic | **One data point in the aggregate**, which is where the real leverage now sits | — |

A useful rule: for a **platform release**, one review in the right outlet can
be a meaningful fraction of the opening audience. For a **wide release**, no
single review is detectable; only the aggregate is.

### 2.4 How aggregator approval is actually granted

This is the real gatekeeping mechanism and it is administrative, not
editorial.

- **Outlet approval** comes first. An outlet applies; the aggregator assesses
  editorial standards, staffing, publication history, volume, and reach.
  Approved outlets can then have their critics counted.
- **Individual critic approval** exists for freelancers and independent
  creators. Typical requirements: a track record of a couple of years, a
  minimum publication cadence (on the order of dozens of reviews a year across
  approved outlets), a body of work that is criticism rather than
  news/recaps, and — for video and audio — a demonstrable audience.
- **Top Critic** status is a separate, much smaller designation assigned by
  the aggregator, and it is what gates the "top critics" component of badge
  thresholds (§4.2).
- **Broadening happened deliberately.** In the late 2010s the major aggregator
  reformed its criteria explicitly to admit more freelance, video, and
  under-represented critics, having been criticised for a pool that skewed
  heavily male and white. This changed the composition of scores, not just
  their count.
- **Approval can be revoked** — for inactivity, for review-selling, for
  fabricated reviews, or for publishing before an embargo in a pattern.

Why this matters commercially: admission to the pool converts an opinion into
a *vote*. An excellent critic outside the pool has readers; a mediocre one
inside it has a share of the Tomatometer.

### 2.5 What separates a good critic from a bad one

The skill axis, as practitioners and editors describe it:

1. **Describing the film accurately before judging it.** The reader must be
   able to tell what the film *is* even if they disagree with the verdict.
   Most bad criticism fails here first.
2. **Reviewing the film that was made**, not the one the critic wanted, and
   not the marketing campaign.
3. **Genre literacy.** Judging a slasher by the standards of slashers, and
   knowing the fifty that came before it, rather than penalising it for not
   being a drama.
4. **Craft specificity** — being able to say *which* department is failing and
   how (§5), instead of "badly made".
5. **Prose that is readable at speed** and quotable without distortion.
6. **Consistency and calibration.** A critic whose four-star ratings mean
   something is more useful than a better writer whose ratings drift.
7. **Independence from access.** The critics whose verdicts carry weight are
   the ones who can be negative without losing anything.
8. **A point of view that accumulates.** Readers follow critics whose taste
   they can triangulate against, even in disagreement — that is the whole
   value proposition of a named critic over a score.

Failure signatures: plot summary with an adjective at the end; reviewing the
discourse rather than the film; contrarianism as a brand; blurb-writing (§4.5);
and the reliable tell of the underprepared — attributing to the director work
that is obviously the editor's, the DP's, or the composer's.

## 3. How a review gets made

**Who** — the distributor's **publicity department** owns the entire process:
a **SVP/VP of Publicity** setting strategy, a **national publicity director**
running the screening plan, **field publicists** covering regional markets,
and — for specialty titles and awards campaigns — an **outside PR firm** whose
partners have personal relationships with individual critics. The **unit
publicist** (`09-…` §2) supplies the production notes and stills the reviews
will draw on. The filmmakers are not in this loop, except as junket talent.

**When** — the plan is fixed 8–12 weeks out; screenings run from roughly 4
months out (long-lead) to the night before release.

| Stage | What happens | Who |
|---|---|---|
| **Long-lead screening** | 6–16 weeks out, for monthly magazines and features writers working on long lead times | Publicity, long-lead press |
| **Press screening** | The distributor screens for reviewing critics, typically 1–3 weeks out, in multiple cities | National publicity, field publicists, critics |
| **Festival premiere** | For festival titles, reviews land months before release and are the film's permanent first impression (`09-…` §10) | Festival press office, trades, international critics |
| **Junket** | Interviews, separate from reviews; access is sometimes implicitly traded on coverage | Publicity, talent, entertainment press |
| **Embargo** | The contractual date and time critics may publish | Publicity, editors |
| **Publication** | Reviews land together at the embargo lift, producing a single visible consensus event | Critics, editors |
| **Aggregation** | Scores are collected into the RT/Metacritic number within hours | Aggregator staff |
| **Pull-quote harvest** | Marketing mines the reviews for advertising copy the same day | Creative advertising, publicity |

### 3.1 The embargo as a signal

The embargo date is a message the industry knows how to read:

| Embargo timing | Reads as |
|---|---|
| Weeks early (from a festival) | Confidence; the studio wants the reviews working for the campaign |
| ~1–2 weeks out | Normal |
| Reactions-only early, reviews late | Managed: enthusiasm harvested, judgement deferred |
| Day of release, or opening-night | Low confidence |
| No press screenings at all | The studio expects to be savaged — and the *absence* becomes its own negative news story |

Withholding a film from critics rarely protects it. It reliably produces
coverage about the withholding, and trade and consumer press both write the
"not screened for critics" story, which reaches more people than the reviews
would have.

### 3.2 What a press screening is actually like

Mechanics matter because they shape the verdict.

- **Time and place.** Los Angeles and New York screenings are the ones that
  set the tone; other markets are served later or by link. Studio screening
  rooms seat 30–80 and are technically excellent. Overflow goes to booked
  commercial houses, which are not.
- **The 10am problem.** Morning screenings are standard for working critics
  and are a genuinely different viewing environment from a Friday-night
  audience. Comedies in particular play worse at 10am with forty silent
  professionals than they ever will with a paying crowd — publicists know
  this, and it is the main argument for screening comedies with a recruited
  audience present.
- **Audience salting.** For broad comedies and horror, distributors often
  screen with a partly recruited civilian audience precisely to supply the
  laughter or the screaming that the film was built for. Critics notice and
  are divided on whether it is cheating.
- **Security.** For tentpoles: phone pouches, wanding, bag checks, numbered
  seating, forensic watermarking on the print, and occasionally a signed
  non-disclosure agreement covering specific plot points.
- **Materials.** Critics receive **production notes** (a booklet of credits,
  synopsis, and interview material) and access to stills. Weak reviews often
  reproduce the production notes' framing wholesale, which is one way to spot
  a critic who was not paying attention.
- **Screeners and links.** Since 2020, secure streaming links have partly
  replaced physical screenings for smaller titles and for regional critics.
  Distributors dislike them for tentpoles (piracy) and filmmakers dislike them
  for everything (a film watched on a laptop, paused twice, reviews worse).
- **No Q&A, no talent.** A review screening is deliberately austere; a
  screening with the director present and applause at the end is a *promo*
  screening and critics attending one are expected to disclose it.

### 3.3 Long-lead versus short-lead

Two different press populations on two different clocks, and campaigns are
built around the distinction.

| | Long-lead | Short-lead |
|---|---|---|
| **Who** | Monthly magazines, quarterlies, glossies, features desks, some podcasts | Dailies, weeklies, online outlets, broadcast, most aggregated critics |
| **Sees the film** | 6–16 weeks before release, sometimes unfinished (temp VFX, temp score) | 1–21 days before release, final print |
| **Publishes** | On the magazine's own schedule, often a month out — cover stories, set-visit features, interviews | At the embargo lift, days before release |
| **What it buys the studio** | Awareness, positioning, cover placement, the film's "story" | The consensus event and the aggregate score |
| **Risk** | An unfinished film reviewed as finished; an early negative that anchors everything after | Everything lands at once — no time to correct a bad narrative |

Long-lead is where the *narrative* is set — "the comeback", "the passion
project", "the troubled production" — and by the time short-lead critics see
the film, they have read that narrative too. Publicists spend real effort
placing long-lead features precisely to shape the frame the reviews arrive in.

### 3.4 How embargoes are negotiated and policed

An embargo is a **condition of access**, not a contract most outlets sign. It
is enforced socially and economically.

- **Setting it.** The distributor's publicity chief picks the lift time in
  consultation with marketing and, on festival titles, with the festival press
  office. Typical lifts: at the end of a festival premiere screening; two
  weeks out; the Tuesday or Wednesday of release week; 12:01am on the day
  previews begin.
- **Tiering.** Embargoes are commonly split: **social reactions** (short,
  first-impression posts) permitted immediately after a premiere or early
  screening, **full reviews** held until later. This lets a studio harvest
  enthusiasm without the caveats, and it is now standard on tentpoles.
- **Negotiation.** Large outlets negotiate exceptions — an earlier lift for a
  print edition that goes to press before the embargo, or a later screening in
  exchange for a longer piece. Trades negotiate hardest because they have the
  most leverage; a national daily's Sunday print deadline is a real constraint
  publicists routinely accommodate.
- **Policing.** There is no enforcement body. The publicist notices, and the
  sanction is exclusion: removal from the invitation list for that studio,
  loss of junket and interview access, loss of screener links, and — because
  publicists talk to each other — sometimes across studios.
- **What happens when one breaks.** In practice the review stays up (asking
  for a takedown makes news), other outlets follow within minutes because the
  competitive reason to hold has evaporated, and the aggregate score starts
  forming early off a small, unrepresentative sample (§4.4). The studio then
  usually lifts the embargo formally rather than fight it.
- **Asymmetry.** Positive breaks are quietly tolerated. Negative breaks are
  punished. Everyone knows this, which is itself a small upward bias in early
  coverage.

### 3.5 Publicity failure modes around reviews

| Failure | What happens | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Embargo set too late | Reviews land after the audience has decided | The campaign gets no benefit from good reviews it actually earned |
| Embargo set too early on a weak film | Two weeks of negative consensus before release | Tracking softens; the bad number becomes the story |
| No screenings | "Not screened for critics" coverage | A guaranteed negative narrative at zero critical cost to the film |
| Screening a temp cut | Critics review unfinished VFX and temp music | Permanent first impression of a film that no longer exists |
| Wrong room / wrong time | Comedy at 10am to forty professionals | Systematically worse reviews than the film would otherwise get |
| List mismanagement | A key outlet not invited, or an outlet snubbed | An aggrieved review, and a relationship damaged for years |
| Over-restrictive NDAs | Critics publicly complain about the terms | Story about the secrecy, not the film |
| Junket-only access for a difficult film | Soft coverage, no credibility | Pull quotes nobody believes; discounted by readers |

### 3.6 Junkets, access, and the quiet economy

The junket is a separate machine from reviewing and is often confused with it.

- **Format.** Talent sits in a hotel suite; outlets rotate through in 4–8
  minute slots, or in roundtables of 6–10 journalists. Everything is recorded
  by the studio, and syndicated versions are supplied to broadcasters.
- **Who pays.** For international and regional press, the distributor
  frequently pays flights and hotels. Major outlets refuse this on ethics
  grounds and pay their own way; many smaller outlets cannot.
- **The trade.** Access is finite and allocated. An outlet that publishes a
  savage review may find the next film's interviews unavailable. Nothing is
  written down; the mechanism is entirely informal and completely understood.
- **Blurb culture.** A small set of outlets exist mainly to supply enthusiastic
  pull quotes and are treated by critics with contempt and by marketing as a
  supply chain. The category has a documented low point: in 2001 a studio was
  found to have invented a critic at a small newspaper to generate ad quotes,
  and eventually settled a consumer class action over it.

## 4. Aggregate scores and their maths

This matters because the two major aggregators measure genuinely different
things, and are routinely confused.

**Rotten Tomatoes** — each review is classified **fresh or rotten** (a binary),
and the Tomatometer is **the percentage of reviews that are positive**.

- It measures **consensus, not quality**. A film every critic finds mildly
  acceptable scores higher than a film half of them consider a masterpiece and
  half consider a failure.
- It is **non-linear near the top**: 99% and 80% represent similar average
  opinion but read as vastly different.
- **Certified Fresh** is a threshold badge (a minimum score, a minimum number
  of reviews, and a minimum number of top critics) and is used in advertising.
- The separate **Audience Score** measures ticket-buyers and diverges from the
  Tomatometer in predictable, genre-linked ways.

**Metacritic** — a **weighted average of numerical scores** (0–100), with
critics weighted by perceived stature.

- Tracks critical esteem far more faithfully.
- Compresses toward the middle: a Metascore of 80 is exceptional, where a
  Tomatometer of 80 is ordinary.

**Practical consequence for a model**: if you want "how well-regarded is this
film", that's Metacritic-shaped. If you want "is there a negative story about
this film", that's Tomatometer-shaped. They are not interchangeable.

### 4.1 How a review actually becomes a number

The conversion step is where most of the distortion enters.

**Rotten Tomatoes — the binary:**

1. If the critic is approved and self-submits, **the critic designates** their
   own review fresh or rotten. Many outlets do this systematically.
2. If not, **aggregator staff read the review** and assign the designation
   from the text, using the overall verdict rather than the star rating.
3. Star ratings map roughly, with the borderline as the contested zone:

| Rating | Usually counted |
|---|---|
| 3–4 of 4, 3.5–5 of 5, B− or better | Fresh |
| 2.5 of 4 | **The battleground** — historically rotten, frequently disputed by the critic who wrote it |
| 2.5 of 5, 2 of 4, C+ or worse | Rotten |
| No rating at all (most long-form criticism) | Read and judged from the prose |

4. A **consensus line** — one editorial sentence summarising the reviews — is
   written by aggregator staff once enough reviews accumulate. It is not a
   critic's opinion and is frequently mistaken for one.

**Metacritic — the number:**

1. Staff assign each review a **0–100** score. Where the outlet publishes a
   grade or stars, it is converted on a published scale; where it does not,
   staff read the review and assign.
2. Reviews are **weighted** by the aggregator's own assessment of outlet
   stature. The weights are not published, which is a standing complaint.
3. A minimum number of reviews (a handful) is required before a Metascore
   displays at all.
4. Scores are colour-banded — green for favourable, yellow for mixed, red for
   unfavourable — and the band, not the number, is what most readers absorb.

**The consequence.** A single review can be a 55 on Metacritic (mixed, mildly
negative) and simultaneously **rotten** on the Tomatometer, where it counts
exactly as much as a review that called the film worthless. The Tomatometer
throws away the intensity of opinion; that is not a bug in the method, it is
the method.

### 4.2 Badges and thresholds

Thresholds are what marketing actually buys, because a badge is a graphic that
can go on a poster.

| Badge | Roughly requires | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| **Fresh** | Score at or above the 60% line | Baseline; "Fresh" in ads |
| **Certified Fresh** | A higher score sustained (75%+), a minimum review count (more for wide releases than limited), a minimum number of Top Critic reviews, and stability over time | Posters, TV spots, trailers |
| **Rotten** | Below 60% | Nothing — but it is quotable by everyone else |
| **Metacritic "Must-See"** | High Metascore with sufficient reviews | Prestige and awards advertising |

Two mechanical consequences worth knowing:

- **Certified Fresh can be revoked.** If later reviews drag the score below the
  threshold, the badge comes off — which is why studios push to get the badge
  early, while the sample is small and enthusiastic, and put it on the
  artwork immediately.
- **The 59/60 cliff** is worth real money. A film at 60% is advertised as
  Fresh; a film at 59% is advertised as nothing and written about as Rotten.
  The difference between them is a single review out of a hundred.

### 4.3 Edge cases and known distortions

| Edge case | What actually happens |
|---|---|
| **Critic doesn't use ratings** | Prose is read and judged; long-form ambivalence is forced into a binary |
| **Editor set the star rating** | The number that gets aggregated is not the critic's judgement (§2.1) |
| **Mixed review of a flawed masterpiece** | Counts identically to a review calling it unwatchable |
| **Small early sample** | The score after 8 reviews from a festival is volatile and frequently 15–25 points from where it settles |
| **Festival-to-release gap** | A film reviewed at a festival can carry a score for a year before anyone can buy a ticket, and the score is set by a specialist audience |
| **Score drift after release** | Late-adding outlets, re-reviews, and international critics move the number for weeks |
| **Re-designation requests** | Critics can ask for a fresh/rotten flip on their own review; studios ask on the critic's behalf far more often than the public assumes |
| **Review removal** | Reviews from de-listed critics or retracted pieces are pulled, and the score changes with no visible cause |
| **Audience score manipulation** | Coordinated positive or negative rating campaigns before anyone has seen the film (§12.4) — partly addressed by "verified" ratings tied to a ticket purchase |
| **Recut or re-released films** | New reviews append to the old score rather than starting a new one, unless the aggregator opens a separate page |

The deepest distortion is structural: **the Tomatometer is a measure of the
absence of dissent.** A safe, competent, unambitious film reliably out-scores
an ambitious, divisive one — and the divisive one is often the more valuable
film both artistically and, sometimes, commercially (§9).

### 4.4 Why the number moves before it settles

A practical timeline for a wide release with a festival premiere:

| Point | Typical sample | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Festival night | 5–20 reviews | Extremely volatile; skewed enthusiastic (people who chose to attend) |
| Festival week | 20–60 | Settles 5–15 points, usually downward |
| Embargo lift | 60–200 | The number the public and the marketing department react to |
| Opening weekend | 150–350 | Small drift; occasionally a genuine slide as broader outlets file |
| Weeks after | 250–450 | Long tail of international and regional critics |

The number the audience remembers is the **embargo-lift number**, because that
is the one the news coverage reports.

### 4.5 How studios and publicists actually try to influence the score

Nobody buys a score. The levers are all upstream of it.

1. **Who gets invited.** The single most effective lever. Screening a genre
   film for genre outlets and not for generalist dailies changes the
   composition of the pool that scores it.
2. **When they see it.** A festival-first strategy loads the sample with
   critics predisposed to that kind of film; a Wednesday-night strategy loads
   it with whoever is available.
3. **Where they see it.** Room, format, audience mix, and time of day (§3.2).
4. **The frame.** Long-lead features and interviews set the narrative the
   review arrives inside (§3.3).
5. **The tiered embargo.** Harvest social reactions early, hold full reviews —
   so the first public sentiment is enthusiasm, not judgement.
6. **Chasing designations.** Publicists do ask critics and aggregators to
   reconsider a fresh/rotten call, and outlets to submit reviews that were
   never indexed. Both are legitimate; both are used asymmetrically.
7. **Certified Fresh timing.** Push for the badge while the sample is small
   and favourable, then advertise it (§4.2).
8. **Audience-side campaigns.** Promotional screenings, fan events, and
   loyalty-programme mailings put enthusiastic viewers in front of the film
   early and encourage them to rate it — the legal version of what
   review-bombing does illegitimately.
9. **Avoiding the number entirely.** If both aggregate numbers are bad, the
   campaign switches to audience quotes, the CinemaScore grade if it is good,
   and box-office milestones ("#1 movie in America"), and never mentions a
   percentage again.

What does not work: arguing publicly with critics; suing; and asking for
takedowns. Each converts a bad review into a news cycle.

## 5. What critics respond to

Not a mystery, and reasonably consistent:

- **Originality and authorial voice** — the strongest positive signal.
- **Screenplay quality**, particularly structure and dialogue.
- **Performance**, especially transformative or against-type work.
- **Formal craft** — cinematography, editing, production design, score, when
  they are doing something rather than merely being competent.
- **Thematic seriousness** and contemporary relevance.
- **Direction as a coherent point of view** rather than assembled competence.

And negatively: derivativeness, incoherent structure, tonal confusion,
excessive length, franchise obligation over story, and effects standing in for
drama.

### 5.1 By department: what gets praised, what gets panned

Criticism has a stable working vocabulary, department by department. Knowing
it is useful because it maps directly onto what the crafts actually do
(`05-departments-and-crew.md`), and because it is the language reviews are
written in.

**Screenplay** — the department blamed for the most and named the least.

| Praised | Panned |
|---|---|
| "Tight", "economical", "not a wasted scene", "lean", "propulsive" | "Baggy", "shapeless", "meandering", "a third act problem" |
| "Sharp", "crackling", "quotable" dialogue | "On-the-nose", "expository", "characters explain the theme to each other" |
| "Earns its ending" | "Unearned", "a cheat", "the film doesn't know how to end and so ends four times" |
| "Trusts the audience" | "Hand-holding", "spoon-fed", "over-explained" |
| "Specific" characters, "lived-in" world | "Thin", "types not people", "a mouthpiece" |
| Structural control: "the setup pays off" | "Contrived", "coincidence doing the work of plot" |

The most common structural complaint in modern criticism is the **collapsing
third act** — a film that is well-liked for eighty minutes and then resolves
in noise. It is also the most common cause of a good-reviews/bad-word-of-mouth
split, because audiences leave on the last twenty minutes (§8.4).

**Direction** — critics judge whether decisions were *made*.

| Praised | Panned |
|---|---|
| "Assured", "confident", "in complete command of tone" | "Anonymous", "workmanlike", "assembled rather than directed" |
| "A point of view"; "unmistakably a [director] film" | "Impersonal", "committee-made", "house style" |
| "Control of tone" across register shifts | "Tonally confused", "doesn't know what film it wants to be" |
| "Lets scenes breathe"; "trusts silence" | "Restless", "cuts away from its own best moments" |
| Staging: "knows where to put the camera" | "Coverage-driven", "flat", "televisual" |

The insult of record is **"competent"**. In critical usage it is a synonym for
absence of authorship, and it is worse than a mixed review because it is
unquotable.

**Performance** — the department with the most reliable critical rewards.

| Praised | Panned |
|---|---|
| "Transformative", "unrecognisable", "career-best" | "Mannered", "showy", "an impression, not a performance" |
| "Against type", "reveals something new in an actor" | "Coasting", "collecting a paycheque", "doing the thing they always do" |
| "Generous" — playing to the other actors | "Mugging", "acting at the camera" |
| "Lived-in", "interior", "does it with the eyes" | "Miscast" — the single most damaging performance note |
| Ensemble: "everyone is on the same film" | "Nobody in this cast is in the same movie" |
| Small parts: "steals the film in ten minutes" | "Wasted" — a good actor with nothing to play |

Critics systematically over-reward **visible difficulty** — accents, weight
change, prosthetics, illness, real singing — which is why awards bodies
(`13-…` §2) reward the same thing. They under-reward comic performance and
physical performance, both of which look easy when done well.

**Cinematography** — praised when it is legible and expressive at once.

| Praised | Panned |
|---|---|
| "Painterly", "luminous", "beautifully lit", "expressive" | "Murky", "underlit", "you can't see what's happening" |
| "Compositional rigour", "every frame is composed" | "Ugly", "flat", "digital-looking", "grey and desaturated" |
| Camera movement that means something: "restless handheld that never becomes seasick" | "Shaky-cam", "incoherent geography", "the camera has an opinion the scene doesn't" |
| A specific palette, "committed to a look" | "Teal-and-orange", "looks like everything else" |
| Long takes that are dramatically motivated | "A showy oner that's about itself" |

The recurring modern complaint is **dim, desaturated imagery**, a real
consequence of grading choices and of exhibition conditions, and one of the
few technical arguments that has reached general audiences.

**Editing** — invisible when good, and therefore usually mentioned only when
bad.

| Praised | Panned |
|---|---|
| "Propulsive", "rhythmic", "the film has a pulse" | "Choppy", "over-cut", "you can't tell what's happening in the action" |
| "Sharp", "moves like a bullet", "not a wasted frame" | "Baggy", "twenty minutes too long", "a director's-cut problem" |
| Ellipsis: "gets out of a scene a beat early" | "Lingering", "the scene keeps going after it's over" |
| Coherent action geography | "Cut every 1.5 seconds and none of it reads" |
| "Builds a sequence" — montage and parallel action | "Music-video editing", "no sense of space" |

**Length** deserves separating out: it is the most frequent critical complaint
of the current era, it correlates with the editorial power of successful
directors (`07-postproduction.md` §2), and audiences complain about it
independently — it shows up in exit data as a top-three negative.

**Score and sound** — usually noticed only at the extremes.

| Praised | Panned |
|---|---|
| "Propulsive", "haunting", "a theme you leave humming" | "Wall-to-wall", "never shuts up", "the score is telling me how to feel" |
| Distinctive instrumentation, a composer's voice | "Generic tension drone", "temp-track music" |
| Restraint: "knows when to stop" | "Emotionally manipulative", "swelling strings doing the actor's job" |
| Sound design as authorship — "the silence is deafening" | "A mix you can't hear the dialogue in" |
| Needle-drops that earn their place | "A jukebox of licensed nostalgia in place of a score" |

Unintelligible dialogue is now a mainstream complaint as well as a critical
one — a mix problem interacting with home-viewing conditions
(`07-…` §3), and one of the most common reasons audiences turn subtitles on.

**Production and costume design** — praised for coherence more than expense.

| Praised | Panned |
|---|---|
| "Immersive", "you believe the world", "lived-in" | "Backlot", "sets that look like sets", "everything is brand new" |
| Period detail that is specific rather than decorative | "Costume-drama fancy dress", "period as wallpaper" |
| Design that carries theme and character | "Production-design showreel" |
| "Tactile", "practical", "you can feel the materials" | "Weightless", "obviously a volume/greenscreen stage" |

**Visual effects** — critics judge weight and integration, not budget.

| Praised | Panned |
|---|---|
| "Seamless", "invisible", "serves the story" | "Cartoonish", "video-game cutscene", "weightless CG" |
| Physical stakes: "you feel the impact" | "Unfinished-looking", "the third act is a grey CG sky-beam" |
| Restraint and practical/digital blending (`08-vfx-and-specialty.md`) | "Spectacle without consequence" |

### 5.2 What is being judged underneath the vocabulary

Strip out the adjectives and most reviews are testing four things:

1. **Intention** — did anyone decide anything, or is this the average of a
   hundred safe choices?
2. **Coherence** — do the departments agree with each other? A film where
   score, performance, and design are pulling in different directions reads as
   "tonally confused" even when each department is individually good.
3. **Execution against ambition** — a small film that achieves what it tried
   to do beats a large film that half-achieves something bigger. This is why
   modest genre films over-perform critically and enormous ones under-perform.
4. **Novelty against the reviewer's own history** — the critic has seen this
   before and the audience has not (§2.1).

### 5.3 Context effects critics are subject to

Reviews are not written in a vacuum, and the same film scores differently
depending on how it arrives:

- **Festival inflation.** A film premiering to a self-selected audience at a
  festival, after a director introduction and to applause, reviews warmer than
  the same film at a Tuesday press screening. The correction typically shows up
  months later at release, when a second wave of critics files.
- **Auteur credit.** An established director's weaker film is graded on a
  curve upward; a first film by an unknown is graded on the curve of its
  ambitions. Both are real and both are acknowledged by critics themselves.
- **Backlash cycles.** A film that is over-praised early attracts corrective
  negativity within weeks, and a film that is unfairly savaged attracts
  defenders. The aggregate number does not capture that the conversation has
  reversed.
- **Expectation.** A film sold as a masterpiece is reviewed as a
  disappointment; the same film sold modestly is reviewed as a discovery. This
  is the critical version of the mechanism that governs word of mouth (§8).
- **Sequence in the day.** At festivals, critics see 3–5 films a day. The
  fourth film of the day is not reviewed by the same person who saw the first.

### 5.4 Systematic genre bias

Durable and worth encoding:

| Genre | Critical reception vs audience reception |
|---|---|
| Prestige drama, biography | Reviewed **above** audience scores |
| Art film, foreign-language, documentary | Reviewed far above |
| Horror | Reviewed **below** — audiences reward what critics penalise |
| Broad comedy | Reviewed well below |
| Action / franchise | Reviewed below, though the gap narrows for the best-made |
| Animation (family) | Reviewed roughly in line, often generously |
| Faith-based, and films made for a specific community | Reviewed far below; the target audience does not consult critics at all |
| Romance / "chick flick" as historically categorised | Reviewed below, and the gap has been a long-running argument about the composition of the critical pool (§2.4) |

Two structural reasons rather than snobbery: critics see far more films in a
genre than any audience member, so formula reads as tiredness where an
audience reads it as satisfaction; and criticism rewards *departure* while
genre audiences are largely buying *delivery of the expected*.

## 6. What reviews actually do to box office

The effect is real but **highly conditional**:

| Film type | Review sensitivity |
|---|---|
| **Adult drama, prestige, specialty** | **Very high.** Reviews are the primary awareness mechanism; a strong consensus is the campaign |
| **Mid-budget comedy/thriller** | Moderate. Reviews affect the decision at the margin |
| **Horror** | **Low.** The audience is genre-loyal and reviews are discounted |
| **Pre-sold franchise / event** | **Very low on opening weekend** — the audience has already decided. But it shows up in **week two**, because bad reviews and bad word of mouth reinforce each other |
| **Family** | Low for the children, moderate for the parents deciding |

### 6.1 The four causal mechanisms

Keeping these distinct is what makes review effects predictable rather than
mysterious.

1. **Reviews as awareness.** For a film with no advertising budget, a rave in a
   major outlet *is* the marketing — it is the only reason anyone knows the
   film exists. This is the entire logic of the platform release
   (`09-…` §7.2), where a specialty distributor opens on 4–6 screens in New
   York and Los Angeles precisely to convert reviews into per-screen averages
   that justify expansion.
2. **Reviews as permission.** For a large, heavily marketed film, the audience
   already knows about it and reviews do not create awareness. What they do is
   license or withhold attendance for the marginal viewer — the person who was
   not going to go but could be persuaded, and the person who was going but is
   now happy to wait.
3. **Reviews as risk reduction.** A cinema ticket for a family of four,
   including travel, food and time, is an expensive and non-refundable
   decision. Reviews function as insurance against wasting it, which is why
   review sensitivity rises with the total cost of attendance and with the
   viewer's age.
4. **Reviews as accelerant on word of mouth.** Reviews and word of mouth are
   not independent: a critical consensus supplies the vocabulary that ordinary
   viewers use to describe the film, and it validates the opinions they were
   already forming. A bad consensus makes a disappointed audience *confident*
   in its disappointment, which is what converts private dissatisfaction into
   public discouragement.

### 6.2 By release pattern

Release pattern matters at least as much as genre, and is often ignored.

| Pattern | Where reviews bite | Magnitude |
|---|---|---|
| **Platform** (4–20 screens, expanding) | Everywhere. The reviews are the campaign, and the per-screen average in week one determines whether the expansion happens at all | **Decisive** — a specialty film with weak reviews frequently never expands beyond its opening cities |
| **Limited / specialty wide** (200–1,200 screens) | Awareness and the decision to make a special trip | High |
| **Wide** (2,000–3,000 screens) | Weekend two onward; opening is bought by the campaign | Moderate, lagged |
| **Saturation / event** (3,500–4,500 screens) | Almost nothing in weekend one; visible from Monday, decisive by weekend two | Low then high |
| **Day-and-date / streaming-adjacent** | The decision becomes "cinema now or home in six weeks" — bad reviews push the whole audience into the later window rather than eliminating it | Shifts *timing*, not total viewership |
| **Re-release / anniversary** | Reputation, not reviews (§11) | The reviews are already thirty years old |

The pattern-based rule of thumb: **review sensitivity is inversely
proportional to marketing spend, and directly proportional to how much the
viewer has to choose deliberately.**

### 6.3 Rules of thumb for magnitude

Directional, with wide spread, and best treated as elasticities rather than
constants:

- For a **platform specialty title**, the difference between a strong and a
  poor critical consensus is not a percentage — it is the difference between
  expanding to several hundred screens and never expanding. Call it a factor
  of several on total gross.
- For a **wide adult drama or mid-budget comedy**, a large critical gap
  plausibly moves opening weekend by something in the range of 10–25% and the
  multiple (`10-theatrical-release-and-box-office.md` §4) by more.
- For a **pre-sold tentpole**, opening weekend moves by a few percent at most;
  the second-weekend drop moves by 10–20 percentage points, which is where the
  money actually is.
- For **horror**, opening weekend is essentially review-insensitive, and even
  legs are driven more by the exit grade than by the reviews.

### 6.4 What reviews never do

- They do not save a film nobody has heard of. Awareness is bought; approval
  is earned. A rave with no distribution behind it converts to nothing.
- They do not overcome a bad date, a wrong rating, or a film that cannot be
  described in a sentence (`09-…` §12.3).
- They do not lengthen a bad film's run. Exhibitors reallocate screens on
  weekly performance, not on critical standing
  (`10-…` §2).
- They do not, on their own, produce awards. They gate them
  (`13-…` §6) — necessary, nowhere near sufficient.

## 7. Audience instruments

**CinemaScore** — polls opening-night audiences at a sample of cinemas and
publishes a letter grade, **A+ to F**.

- The scale is compressed and must be read on its own terms: **A** is good,
  **B+** is mediocre, **B−** is a warning, and **C or below is a disaster**.
  An **F** is exceedingly rare and almost always signals a film that ambushed
  its audience with a tonal or ending betrayal.
- It measures **satisfaction against expectation**, not quality — which is why
  it predicts legs so well and correlates so poorly with reviews.

**PostTrak** — exit surveys covering demographics, how people heard about the
film, and **definite recommend** percentage. More granular, used internally.

**Definite recommend** is the number that actually forecasts word of mouth: it
measures propagation, not approval.

### 7.1 How CinemaScore is actually conducted

Founded in the late 1970s and run from Las Vegas as an independent service
sold by subscription to studios, exhibitors, and press.

| Element | Practice |
|---|---|
| **When** | Opening night only — the Thursday previews and Friday of a wide release |
| **Where** | A rotating sample of roughly 25–45 cinemas across about five geographically spread US markets, chosen to approximate a national mix |
| **How** | Field staff hand cards to patrons entering the auditorium and collect them on exit; increasingly supplemented by handheld/digital capture |
| **Sample size** | On the order of **400–500 completed responses per title** — small, and the main technical criticism of the service |
| **What is asked** | An overall grade A+/A/A−/B+…F; whether the respondent would buy it on home video; whether they would recommend it; plus age and gender |
| **Published** | A single letter grade, released Friday night US time, in time for the weekend's box-office coverage |
| **Splits** | Grades by age band and gender are supplied to subscribers and are often more informative than the headline grade |
| **Coverage** | Only films opening wide enough to justify the survey; most specialty titles are never graded |

### 7.2 What the grades historically mean

The distribution is heavily skewed upward, because the opening-night audience
self-selected into the film.

| Grade | Frequency | Reads as |
|---|---|---|
| **A+** | Rare — a small percentage of graded wide releases in a year | Exceptional legs; crowd-pleasers, four-quadrant family, sports and uplift dramas, and the occasional franchise capstone |
| **A / A−** | The modal outcome for successful films | Healthy; supports a normal-to-strong multiple |
| **B+** | Very common | Mediocre. Frequently misreported in the press as a good result |
| **B / B−** | Common for horror and for divisive tentpoles | A warning; expect a steeper-than-normal drop |
| **C range** | Uncommon | Serious trouble; the film will fall off a cliff in week two |
| **D range** | Rare | Audience actively feels misled |
| **F** | A few dozen films in the service's entire history | Ambush — a film that broke a promise its own marketing made |

Patterns worth knowing:

- **Horror grades low by construction.** A film designed to make people
  uncomfortable is rated by people who have just been made uncomfortable.
  A C+ horror film can be a substantial hit; the same grade on a family film
  is fatal. Read grades **relative to genre**, always.
- **F is not "bad film".** The historical F list is dominated by films that
  deliberately violated audience expectations — a horror film with no monster,
  an art film sold as a thriller, an ending in which nobody is redeemed. Some
  of them are well-reviewed, and some have durable reputations (§11).
- **A+ is not "great film".** It clusters on emotional satisfaction and
  affirmation. Ambitious, unsettling, or ambiguous films essentially never get
  one, however good they are.
- **The grade is a gap, not a level.** It measures delivery against expectation
  — the same mechanism that governs word of mouth (§8) and the same one that
  makes mis-selling the most expensive marketing error (`09-…` §12.2).

### 7.3 PostTrak in detail

Run as a commercial research product (Screen Engine/ASI), and now the more
operationally useful of the two because of what it breaks out.

| Element | Practice |
|---|---|
| **When** | Opening weekend, and often on subsequent weekends |
| **How** | Exit interviews at a sample of cinemas plus a large online panel component |
| **Sample size** | Typically **1,000–3,000+ responses per title per weekend** — several times CinemaScore |
| **Headline metrics** | Overall rating (usually a five-point scale, reported as "% total positive" or "% excellent") and **"definite recommend" %** |
| **Breakdowns** | Age bands, gender, ethnicity, whether they came with children, ticket type (premium formats), and the crucial **awareness source** — what actually got them there |
| **Also captured** | First-choice status, whether they'd seen it before, plans to see it again, and reaction to specific elements |
| **Who reads it** | Distribution and marketing, on Saturday morning, to set the weekend estimate and decide whether to reallocate remaining spend |

Interpretation, directionally: **definite recommend above about 60% is
strong**; the low 50s is average; below the mid-40s the film has a legs
problem regardless of what the grade or the reviews say.

### 7.4 The known failure cases of exit polling

| Failure | Why it happens | Example pattern |
|---|---|---|
| **Fan-loaded sample** | Opening night is the most enthusiastic audience the film will ever have | A tentpole gets an A− on Friday and drops 65% the next weekend — the general audience never agreed with the fans |
| **Genre miscalibration** | A grade read without genre context | A B− horror film is reported as a disaster and legs perfectly well |
| **Small sample noise** | 400 responses cannot resolve half-grade differences | A− versus B+ on a mid-size film is frequently not a real difference |
| **No coverage of specialty** | The service only grades wide openings | Platform releases have no exit grade at all; distributors rely on per-screen averages and PostTrak instead |
| **Satisfaction ≠ advocacy** | A viewer can be satisfied and still not tell anyone | High grade, low definite-recommend: pleasant, forgettable, no legs |
| **Late-breaking negative** | The problem is in the last act and shows up in conversation, not in a card filled out in the lobby | Grade looks fine; the online conversation over the weekend is savage |
| **Format skew** | Premium large-format and 3D audiences rate differently from standard screens | Grades from a sample weighted to premium screens run high |

The single most useful discipline: **never read a grade without the genre, the
demographic split, and the definite-recommend number next to it.**

## 8. Word of mouth as a mechanism

The most important reception effect and the least directly measurable. It is
also the only one that is *endogenous*: word of mouth is produced by the
audience the marketing delivered, and then determines the audience that
follows.

### 8.1 The propagation model

Think of it as three populations and a transmission rate.

```
┌──────────────┐   marketing   ┌──────────────┐  transmission  ┌──────────────┐
│  AWARE, NOT  │ ────────────▶ │  ATTENDED    │ ─────────────▶ │  PERSUADED   │
│  DECIDED     │   converts    │  (seeded)    │  r > 1 grows   │  (arrive     │
│              │               │              │  r < 1 decays  │   later)     │
└──────────────┘               └──────────────┘                └──────────────┘
       ▲                              │                               │
       │                              │  negative transmission        │
       └──────────────────────────────┴───────────────────────────────┘
                    "wait for streaming" / "don't bother"
```

- **Marketing sets the seed size.** It buys the opening audience and nothing
  else (`09-…` §12.3).
- **Word of mouth sets the transmission rate.** Each viewer tells some number
  of people, with some proportion acting on it. Above break-even the audience
  grows into weeks two and three; below it the film decays at its natural
  rate.
- **The transmission rate is set by the expectation gap**, not by quality in
  the abstract — which is why an honestly sold modest film out-legs an
  over-sold good one.
- **Saturation ends it.** Every propagation curve terminates when the
  interested population is exhausted; this is why even the best word of mouth
  produces a finite multiple (`10-…` §4).

### 8.2 The clock

1. **Previews and opening night** — the pre-sold, most enthusiastic segment.
   Their verdict is measured by CinemaScore. Social reaction begins the moment
   the first preview screening lets out — on a modern tentpole, that is
   Thursday evening, and the first hundred thousand opinions are public before
   Friday's audience has woken up.
2. **Friday into Saturday** — the first non-pre-sold audience arrives partly
   on the strength of Thursday and Friday word of mouth. **Saturday's
   performance relative to Friday is the earliest reliable tell**: a strong
   Saturday bump above the normal weekend shape means the film is being
   recommended; a flat or negative Saturday on a film with big previews means
   it is being warned against.
3. **Sunday** — the internal weekend multiple (weekend ÷ Friday, or weekend ÷
   previews) is the first hard number distribution trusts.
4. **Monday to Thursday** — weekday holds are the purest early word-of-mouth
   signal available, because there is no event pressure and marketing is
   largely spent.
5. **Week two** — the general audience, deciding largely on what they've
   heard. The second-weekend drop is the result
   (`10-theatrical-release-and-box-office.md` §3).
6. **Weeks three onward** — pure word of mouth; marketing is largely spent and
   exhibitors are deciding weekly whether to keep the screens.
7. **Post-theatrical** — word of mouth continues to operate in home windows,
   where it determines a title's discovery curve on streaming and, over years,
   its library value (§11).

### 8.3 Anticipation and advocacy are different forces

The most common modelling error is treating pre-release buzz and post-release
word of mouth as one variable. They are separate populations doing separate
things, and they are only weakly coupled.

| | **Pre-release anticipation** | **Post-release advocacy** |
|---|---|---|
| **Who** | Fans, enthusiasts, the terminally online; a small, self-selected, highly vocal population (§12) | Everyone who bought a ticket |
| **Based on** | Trailers, casting, IP, the director, the campaign, other people's excitement | The film itself, measured against what was promised |
| **Measured by** | Tracking, trailer views, presales, social volume (`09-…` §8) | Exit polling, weekday holds, second-weekend drop |
| **Buys** | Opening weekend | Everything after it |
| **Can be manufactured?** | Substantially, yes — that is what a campaign is | No. This is the hard limit |
| **Direction of error** | Over-states general demand; fandom is loud and small | Under-states early, because advocacy takes days to reach non-fans |
| **Failure signature** | Huge previews, catastrophic Saturday | Modest opening, tiny drops, a large multiple |

The two interact badly in one specific way: **high anticipation raises the
expectation bar that the film must clear to generate positive advocacy.** A
campaign that is too good is a real, recurring, expensive failure mode.

### 8.4 What makes a film recommendable

Being liked is not the same as being recommended. Advocacy has its own
requirements:

1. **It delivers the promise of its own campaign.** The single strongest
   predictor, and the one entirely within the studio's control.
2. **It gives people something to say.** A scene, a twist, a performance, a
   sequence, an image, a joke — a discrete object that can be described
   without describing the whole film. Films that are diffusely good are hard
   to recommend.
3. **The recommender looks good for recommending it.** Recommendation is a
   social act with reputational stakes. Films that make the recommender seem
   perceptive, funny, or in-the-know propagate; films that make them look
   naive do not.
4. **Low risk for the recipient.** "You'll like it" is a promise. People are
   conservative about spending someone else's money and evening.
5. **It rewards the theatre.** A film whose value is scale, sound, or shared
   reaction generates "see it in a cinema" — the most valuable sentence in
   word of mouth, because it converts a deferred viewing into a ticket.
6. **It sustains a second conversation.** Films with ambiguity, a twist, or a
   readable subtext generate discussion after the recommendation, which
   extends the propagation window.
7. **It ends well.** Audiences weight the last fifteen minutes far out of
   proportion. An ending that lands can rescue a mediocre film's word of
   mouth; an ending that collapses can destroy a good one's.
8. **Repeatability.** Films people see twice have both a direct box-office
   benefit and a compounding advocacy benefit, because the repeat viewer
   usually brings someone.

### 8.5 What makes word of mouth negative

- **Mis-selling** (`09-…` §12.2) — the single most reliable cause.
- **Running long**, or a third act that collapses (§5.1).
- **An ending that betrays the audience's investment** — the classic F-grade
  mechanism (§7.2).
- **A gap between the trailer's best moment and the film's best moment.** If
  the trailer was the highlight, every viewer discovers it independently.
- **Being merely fine.** Indifference doesn't propagate, and a film that
  generates no conversation dies quietly regardless of its reviews.
- **A single describable defect.** Negative word of mouth needs a *reason*,
  and one memorable flaw — a bad performance, an unintelligible mix, a
  ridiculous third act — supplies it in a shareable sentence.

### 8.6 Why negative travels faster

The asymmetry is real, large, and worth building into any model of reception:

1. **Fewer exposures required.** One credible warning from a trusted source is
   usually enough to cancel a plan; positive recommendations typically need
   repetition before someone acts.
2. **Negative is more entertaining to transmit.** A savage description of a bad
   film is better social content than a sincere endorsement of a good one, so
   it is shared more, and by more people, including people who have not seen
   the film.
3. **The audience of a negative message is larger.** Praise is directed
   ("you'd like this"); complaint is broadcast.
4. **Cheap credibility.** Nobody suspects the motives of someone panning a
   film. Enthusiasm gets discounted as hype, fandom, or marketing.
5. **The deferral is frictionless.** In a world with a six-to-eight-week
   theatrical window, negative word of mouth does not have to persuade someone
   not to watch — only to **wait**. "It's fine, watch it at home" is
   devastating to theatrical grosses and is not even a negative opinion.
6. **Aggregation amplifies it.** A poor score is a headline; a good score is
   a marketing asset nobody reports on.

Rule of thumb: **positive word of mouth is a growth rate; negative word of
mouth is a step change.** Good films build over weeks; bad ones collapse
between Friday and Sunday.

### 8.7 What social platforms changed

The mechanism is old; the clock is not.

| Then (pre-2005) | Now |
|---|---|
| Word of mouth propagated in person over **days to weeks** | The first mass verdict exists within **hours of the first public screening** |
| A bad film could have a good opening weekend and a bad second one | A bad film can have a bad **Saturday** — the collapse now happens inside the opening weekend |
| Geography mattered; a coastal opinion took a week to reach the middle | Simultaneous and national |
| Spoilers were containable | Spoilers are a permanent risk, and studios now run anti-spoiler campaigns as part of the release plan |
| Critics were the only published opinions | Everyone is published; critics are a minority of the visible verdicts |

Platform-specific behaviours that distributors actually track:

- **Short-form video** (TikTok, Reels, Shorts) drives discovery for young
  audiences and can create demand for a film with no advertising at all,
  usually via one clip, sound, or meme rather than the campaign.
- **Letterboxd and rating apps** function as a permanent, public, cinephile-
  skewed verdict that forms within 48 hours and does not decay. Its scores
  diverge from both aggregators, and it is where reappraisal now begins (§11).
- **Reddit and forums** are where the durable arguments live, and where the
  post-opening reappraisal of tentpoles happens.
- **X/Twitter and Threads** set the first-24-hours narrative, which is why the
  reaction embargo (§3.4) exists.
- **YouTube** review and reaction channels have larger audiences than most
  newspapers and behave as a hybrid of criticism and fandom (§12).
- **Group chats** are invisible and probably the largest single channel; none
  of the measurable proxies capture them, which is why exit polling still has
  a job.

### 8.8 Measurable proxies for something unmeasurable

Nobody observes word of mouth; distributors observe its shadows.

| Proxy | Reads as | Available |
|---|---|---|
| Saturday-to-Friday ratio | Immediate recommendation, before reviews of the weekend exist | Saturday night |
| Weekend multiple (weekend ÷ previews or ÷ Friday) | Whether the pre-sold audience was the whole audience | Sunday |
| Weekday holds Mon–Thu | The cleanest early signal; no event pressure, no marketing | Tuesday onward |
| **Second-weekend drop** | The verdict (`10-…` §3) | Day 10 |
| Definite recommend (PostTrak) | Advocacy intent, directly | Saturday |
| Presales for the following weekend | Forward demand, before it shows in grosses | Continuous |
| Search and social volume, sentiment-weighted | Conversation quantity and sign; noisy and easily gamed | Continuous |
| Repeat-viewing percentage | The strongest single indicator of a phenomenon | Weekend two |
| Screen retention offered by exhibitors | The industry's own bet on the film's legs | Weekly |

**Modelling note**: word of mouth is *multiplicative and lagged*, not additive.
It acts on the audience the marketing already delivered, it compounds
week over week, and its sign is set by the gap between expectation and
delivery — not by quality in the abstract.

## 9. The reception matrix

The combinations that actually occur, and what each means:

| Critics | Audience | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| High | High | The best case. Legs, awards, and a long ancillary life |
| High | Low | Critical darling, commercial failure. Common for difficult prestige films; still valuable for reputation and library |
| Low | High | The crowd-pleaser. Horror, broad comedy, franchise. Profitable, no awards, and the critics' verdict ages irrelevant |
| Low | Low | Total failure. The only case with no recoverable value |
| Divisive (split) | Divisive | Often the most commercially interesting: argument is propagation, and controversy sells better than indifference |

Note the asymmetry a simulation should preserve: **a mediocre reception is
commercially worse than a divisive one**, because indifference generates no
word of mouth in either direction.

### 9.1 The nine-cell version, with consequences

Adding the middle band makes the useful distinctions visible. Read the columns
as the *audience* verdict (exit grade plus definite recommend) and the rows as
the *critical* verdict.

| | **Audience high** | **Audience middling** | **Audience low** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Critics high** | Awards run, long legs, strong library, careers made | The respectable underperformer: good reviews, polite audience, no legs. Very common for adult drama | The alienating art film. Reviews are the whole asset; the exit grade may be an F and it may still be a permanent title (§11) |
| **Critics middling** | The professional crowd-pleaser. No awards, real money, sequels commissioned | **The dead zone.** Nothing to say in either direction; dies quietly and is the worst outcome per pound spent | Bad word of mouth with no critical defence; collapses in week two |
| **Critics low** | The critic-proof hit: horror, broad comedy, faith-based, franchise. Reviews are simply not consulted by this audience | Opens on marketing, drops hard, breaks even on ancillary if the budget was disciplined | Total failure; write-down, franchise stopped, executive consequences |

### 9.2 Consequences beyond the box office

Reception outcomes propagate into decisions that outlast the release.

| Cell | Awards | Sequel / franchise | Talent effect | Library value |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Critics high / audience high | Strong contender (`13-…` §1) | Commissioned immediately, usually too fast | Everyone's quote rises (`12-talent-labor-and-careers.md` §3) | Highest — durable, re-releasable, licensable forever (`11-…` §3) |
| Critics high / audience low | Very possible; the classic nominee that nobody saw | No | Directors and actors gain standing even as the film loses money | Good and slow-building; reappraisal likely (§11) |
| Critics low / audience high | Essentially none, occasionally craft categories | Yes, and quickly | Bankability rises; critical standing does not | Solid but flat; sells on familiarity, not reputation |
| Divisive / divisive | Splits voting bodies; often nominated in craft, rarely wins top prizes | Contested internally; depends on the number, not the argument | Polarising talent become more, not less, employable | Frequently the highest long-run growth — arguments keep a film alive |
| Middling / middling | None | No | Neutral to negative; nobody's reel improves | Lowest — nothing to re-package, nothing to re-release |
| Low / low | None | Franchise terminated, and the failure is attached to whoever greenlit it | Real career damage, particularly for a director on their second film | Near zero |

### 9.3 The archetypes, in the terms practitioners use

- **The four-quadrant hit** — B+ reviews, A CinemaScore, huge multiple. The
  most profitable outcome in the business and the hardest to engineer.
- **The critic-proof franchise entry** — 35% Tomatometer, A− grade, enormous
  opening, steep but survivable drop. Everyone involved has priced the
  reviews in.
- **The prestige underperformer** — 90% Tomatometer, high Metascore, small
  opening, respectable multiple, awards nominations, loses money theatrically
  and earns it back over years.
- **The front-loaded disappointment** — big previews, poor Saturday, 65–70%
  second-weekend drop. Diagnostic of an anticipation/advocacy mismatch (§8.3).
- **The sleeper** — modest opening, tiny drops, weeks in the top five, a
  multiple of 4× or better. Almost always positive word of mouth on a film
  nobody was told to expect much from.
- **The ambush** — decent reviews, catastrophic exit grade. A film that
  betrayed the promise of its own marketing; the F-grade pattern (§7.2).
- **The cult in waiting** — poor reviews, poor grosses, an intense minority
  advocacy that becomes the film's whole future (§11.3).

## 10. Common misconceptions

1. **"Rotten Tomatoes score = quality."** It's a consensus percentage, and its
   distribution is nothing like a quality distribution.
2. **"Bad reviews kill films."** They kill *adult dramas*. They barely dent a
   pre-sold franchise opening, and they show up a week later instead.
3. **"Critics and audiences are opposed."** They agree more than they disagree;
   the disagreement is genre-structured and predictable.
4. **"Word of mouth is just quality."** It's the gap between what was promised
   and what was delivered — which is why a good film sold wrong underperforms a
   modest film sold honestly.
5. **"Reception is fixed at release."** Critical standing moves over years;
   a film's reputation and its opening weekend are only loosely related, and
   library value follows reputation (§11).
6. **"A B+ CinemaScore is good."** It is a mediocre result on a compressed
   scale, and it is routinely reported as a success (§7.2).
7. **"Online sentiment is audience sentiment."** The visible online population
   is small, self-selected, and systematically unrepresentative of ticket
   buyers (§12).
8. **"Studios can buy a good score."** They can shape the sample, the timing,
   and the framing. They cannot buy the verdict, and every attempt to do so
   directly has ended as a scandal (§3.6).
9. **"Reviews and word of mouth are the same signal."** They measure different
   populations at different times and diverge most exactly where the money is
   — genre film.

## 11. Reputation over time

Reception at release is a snapshot of one week's opinion held by one
population. A film's *standing* is a much slower variable, it moves in both
directions, and it is what determines the long-run value of a library
(`11-money-accounting-and-participations.md` §3).

### 11.1 Why standing moves at all

| Force | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Context disappears** | The hype, the backlash, the troubled-production story, and the competing releases all evaporate within a few years, leaving only the film |
| **The audience changes** | A film made for one generation gets re-judged by the next, with different assumptions about what is normal, funny, or offensive |
| **Access changes** | Home video, television syndication, and then streaming deliver audiences vastly larger than the theatrical one, and with no ticket price to risk |
| **Influence becomes visible** | A film that was called derivative gets re-read as originary once its imitators arrive |
| **Craft is re-evaluated** | Effects and techniques that looked cheap at release can look brave in retrospect, and vice versa: nothing ages worse than state-of-the-art digital work |
| **Critical fashion moves** | Genres previously beneath serious attention — horror, melodrama, action, animation — have each been institutionally rehabilitated over the last forty years |
| **The canon is maintained** | Decennial critics' polls, restorations, boutique physical-media labels, retrospectives, and academic syllabi all actively curate |

### 11.2 The typical trajectories

| Trajectory | Shape | Typical case |
|---|---|---|
| **Contemporary consensus holds** | Flat | Most films. Standing at release is standing forever |
| **Rehabilitation** | Poor-to-mixed at release, canonical within 15–30 years | Ambitious genre films savaged on arrival, particularly science fiction and horror of the late 1970s and 1980s |
| **Slow burn to ubiquity** | Modest theatrical, then television and home video build an enormous audience | Films whose reputation was made almost entirely after their theatrical run |
| **Decline** | Praised and awarded at release, embarrassing within 20 years | Prestige films whose politics or sentiment date; Best Picture winners are unusually exposed to this |
| **Cult formation** | Commercial failure, intense minority advocacy, permanent afterlife | Films with a distinctive voice and a small, evangelical audience (§11.3) |
| **Re-release value** | Standing converts back into theatrical revenue at anniversaries | 25th/30th/40th anniversary runs, restorations, and format upgrades |

Rough clock: the first serious reappraisal window opens at **5–10 years**
(when a film reaches a new audience on a new format), the canon question is
settled between **20 and 30 years**, and the awards-vs-standing gap is usually
obvious by then. A Best Picture winner and the film that lost to it can trade
places entirely within a decade, and frequently do (`13-…` §8).

### 11.3 How a cult actually forms

Cult status is not just "eventually liked". It has requirements:

1. **A distinctive, non-replicable voice.** Nobody develops a cult around
   competence.
2. **Initial rejection.** The rejection is load-bearing — belonging to the
   minority that likes it is part of the appeal.
3. **Repeat viewing.** Cults form around films people watch many times, which
   means density of detail, quotability, or ritual.
4. **A channel of transmission.** Late-night television, video rental,
   midnight screenings, a physical-media boutique release, or a streaming
   recommendation engine.
5. **A community.** Conventions, quote culture, fan production, and eventually
   an anniversary re-release with the surviving cast.

The commercial consequence is real: a cult title generates modest but
extraordinarily durable revenue — repeated licensing, physical-media reissues,
merchandise, and eventually remake or sequel value decades later, sometimes
exceeding what the original earned theatrically.

### 11.4 Why this matters commercially

- **Library valuation follows durable reputation, not opening weekend.** When
  a catalogue is bought, sold, or licensed to a streamer, the titles that
  command a premium are the ones people still ask for.
- **Re-releases and restorations** are near-pure margin: no negative cost, low
  P&A, and a built-in audience.
- **Remake, sequel, and adaptation rights** are priced off recognition, which
  is reputation with a long tail.
- **Talent reputation compounds separately.** A director whose commercial
  failures are later canonised accrues real financing power from it
  (`12-…` §4).
- **Awards standing decays; critical standing does not.** "Academy Award
  winner" is a permanent line on the packaging, but what keeps a film in
  circulation is that people still want to watch it.

## 12. Fan communities and pre-release sentiment

A distinct third population, separate from both critics and general audiences,
with its own mechanics. It is small, loud, early, and permanently mistaken for
the market.

### 12.1 What makes it different

| | Critics | **Fandom** | General audience |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Size** | Hundreds | Thousands to low millions per property | Tens of millions |
| **Timing** | 1–14 days pre-release | **Months to years** pre-release | Release week |
| **Basis of opinion** | The film | The property, the casting, the trailer, the discourse | The film, and what friends said |
| **Visibility** | Published, indexed, aggregated | Extremely high per capita — dominates social measurement | Almost invisible until they buy tickets |
| **What it moves** | Prestige, specialty grosses | Presales, previews, first-weekend intensity, and the narrative everyone else reads | Everything after weekend one |
| **Reliability as a forecast** | Fair for prestige | **Poor for gross, good for opening-night intensity** | The actual answer |

### 12.2 The pre-release sentiment timeline

| Moment | What is being judged | Typical fan response and its meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Announcement / IP acquisition** | The idea, and whether it should exist | Loud but low-information; the "why does this exist" cycle |
| **Casting** | Fidelity to the property, and identity politics around it | The most reliably explosive moment in the whole cycle |
| **First-look image** | Design, costume, tone | High-signal for design problems; a genuinely useful early warning |
| **Teaser** | Tone and scale | View counts and like ratios are tracked; comment sentiment is read closely |
| **Full trailer** | The pitch | The last cheap moment to change positioning (`09-…` §4) |
| **Reactions embargo lift** | The first-hand verdict of people who have seen it | Deliberately structured to be enthusiastic (§3.4) |
| **Reviews** | The film | Fans discount critics if the verdict conflicts with anticipation |
| **Opening weekend** | Delivery | Fandom converts either to advocacy or to the loudest possible negative |

Studios do respond to this. A visible, unanimous negative reaction to a
character design or a look has, more than once, produced an expensive redesign
and a release-date move — a multi-million-dollar VFX bill and months of delay,
taken because the alternative was a poisoned campaign.

### 12.3 What fandom actually predicts, and what it doesn't

**Predicts well:** presales and Thursday previews; the intensity of the first
48 hours of conversation; merchandising and premium-format uptake; whether a
property can support a spin-off.

**Predicts badly:** total gross; multiples; the reaction of the audience that
was never on social media in the first place. Highly online enthusiasm
routinely produces a big preview number and a poor Saturday (§8.3), and
sustained online hostility routinely fails to dent a film that the general
audience has no argument with.

**Inverts occasionally:** a property with a hostile fandom can perform
perfectly well because the hostility is itself free awareness, and because
angry fans buy tickets to be angry accurately.

### 12.4 Review bombing and score manipulation

Coordinated rating campaigns against — or occasionally for — a film, usually
motivated by a political or fandom dispute rather than the film.

- **Where it lands.** Audience score fields on aggregators, retail store
  ratings, and rating apps. It does not touch the Tomatometer or Metascore,
  which only accept approved critics.
- **Tells.** Volume spiking *before* release; a bimodal 1-and-10 distribution
  with nothing in the middle; a large mismatch with the exit grade of the
  people who actually bought tickets.
- **Countermeasures.** Verified ratings tied to a confirmed ticket purchase;
  disabling pre-release audience commenting; delaying audience scores until
  release. These substantially reduced the phenomenon without ending it.
- **The commercial reality.** Review bombing rarely moves grosses. Its damage
  is narrative: it produces "audiences hate it, critics love it" coverage,
  which is a story that then reaches people who never saw the score.
- **The legitimate mirror image.** Studios put enthusiastic audiences in front
  of a film early — fan screenings, loyalty-programme events, opening-night
  fan campaigns — for exactly the same effect by permitted means (§4.5).

### 12.5 Managing a fandom

The skill axis for the marketing and social teams who do this
(`09-…` §2):

1. **Knowing which population you are reading.** Confusing social volume with
   demand is the standard, expensive mistake.
2. **Feeding the fandom without being captured by it.** Fans are a small
   fraction of the gross for a mass-market property; designing the film for
   them shrinks the audience.
3. **Timing disclosure.** Releasing a design or a casting decision when there
   is enough context around it to survive first contact.
4. **Absorbing a negative cycle without over-reacting.** Most sentiment spikes
   decay within a fortnight; the ones that do not are design problems, not PR
   problems.
5. **Spoiler management.** For films whose value depends on a surprise, the
   plan starts at the first screening and includes the trailer cut, the
   embargo terms, and the talent's press-tour answers.
6. **Never arguing.** Studios and filmmakers who publicly fight a fandom lose
   the news cycle and hand the story a second week of life.

## 13. How reception differs by territory

Reception is not a global constant. The instruments, the gatekeepers, and even
the direction of the critic/audience gap vary by market — which matters
because international is typically **50–70%** of a big film's theatrical gross
(`10-…` §9).

### 13.1 What travels and what doesn't

| Travels well | Travels badly |
|---|---|
| Spectacle, action, and scale — legible without language | Comedy, above all. Wordplay, cultural reference, and timing do not survive dubbing or subtitles |
| Animation and family — dubbed natively, and children don't read reviews | Dialogue-driven drama without a star |
| Horror — a genre with near-universal grammar | Anything depending on domestic political or historical context |
| Franchises with existing local recognition | Sports films about sports the territory does not play |
| Star-driven vehicles, where the star is known locally | Regional accents and vernacular humour |

### 13.2 Territory instruments

| Market | Dominant reception instrument | Behaviour worth knowing |
|---|---|---|
| **US / Canada** | RT/Metacritic, CinemaScore, PostTrak | The system described above; the default in most discussion |
| **UK / Ireland** | National press critics, monthly film magazines, RT | A stronger and more centralised press-critic culture than the US retains; a broadsheet rave still moves specialty attendance |
| **France** | Press-versus-public split published side by side by the dominant local ticketing site | Critical culture is genuinely influential and treats genre auteurs seriously; American films can be canonised in France before they are at home |
| **China** | Local ticketing-platform scores and a cinephile rating site; social messaging | **Showtimes are reallocated daily on measured word of mouth**, so a poor opening-day score compresses a film's screens within 48 hours — the fastest word-of-mouth feedback loop in the world |
| **Japan** | Word of mouth over a long run; local review portals | Films play far longer; a slow build is normal rather than a failure, and domestic animation dominates |
| **South Korea** | Portal ratings and exhibitor-run audience indices | Highly engaged rating culture; audience scores are consulted before booking as a matter of routine |
| **Latin America** | Family and event attendance, social video | Comedy and family travel better than in most markets; premium formats over-index |
| **India** | Opening-day audience verdict, star-driven; local trade reviews | Verdicts are declared publicly and fast, and the first day's audience reaction is decisive |

### 13.3 Practical consequences

- **The critic/audience gap has a different sign in different places.** A film
  dismissed as disposable genre at home can be treated as authored work
  elsewhere, with real consequences for its long-run standing (§11).
- **Dating and word of mouth interact.** A staggered international rollout
  means every later territory opens into an existing global verdict; a
  day-and-date global release denies bad word of mouth the time to arrive
  first, which is exactly why event films now open worldwide in the same week
  (`09-…` §7.1).
- **Local reception instruments need local handling.** Territory marketing
  teams run their own screenings, their own critic relationships, and their
  own influencer programmes; a campaign translated rather than rebuilt reads
  as foreign and under-performs.
- **Awards standing is a US export.** Nomination and win announcements move
  international attendance, sometimes months after a film has finished its
  domestic run (`13-…` §1).

## 14. Who manages reception, and what separates good from bad

Reception is not weather; a department is accountable for it.

**Who** — the distributor's **publicity** and **research** functions, working
to the President of Marketing (`09-…` §2), with an **outside PR firm** on
specialty and awards titles, an **awards consultant** from autumn onward
(`13-…` §5), the **international marketing** heads per territory, and a
**social/community team** covering fandom (§12).

**When** — the plan is drafted with the release date (6–12 months out), the
screening and embargo strategy is fixed 8–12 weeks out, the reaction and review
events fall in the final fortnight, and exit data arrives on opening night.

### 14.1 The skill axis

1. **Choosing the right room and the right audience for the film.** A comedy
   screened cold at 10am and a horror film screened without a crowd are
   self-inflicted wounds (§3.2).
2. **Reading confidence honestly.** Knowing whether the film is a
   screen-it-early film or a hold-it-back film, and being right, is the whole
   job. Both errors are visible from outside.
3. **Sequencing.** Long-lead narrative, then reactions, then reviews, then exit
   data — each set up so the next lands into a frame that helps.
4. **Relationships that survive a bad review.** The publicists who last are the
   ones critics will still take a call from after a savaging.
5. **Knowing which numbers matter.** Definite recommend over exit grade; the
   genre-relative grade over the absolute one; weekday holds over weekend
   headlines (§8.8).
6. **Not fighting.** Never arguing publicly with a critic, an aggregator, or a
   fandom (§4.5, §12.5).
7. **Telling the truth internally, early.** A research department that softens
   bad tracking or a bad test result to keep the room calm costs the studio far
   more than the bad news would have.

### 14.2 Failure table

| Failure | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Screening strategy mismatched to the film | Reviews systematically worse than the film deserves |
| Embargo mistimed | Either no benefit from good reviews, or two weeks of bad narrative |
| Reaction embargo used on a film that can't survive scrutiny | Enthusiastic reactions followed by savage reviews reads as a con, and the gap becomes the story |
| Fandom mistaken for the market | Big previews, collapsing Saturday, a franchise plan built on a number that was never real |
| Exit data misread | Wrong spend decision on Saturday morning, and a wrong hold/expand call on Monday |
| Bad news suppressed internally | Marketing spends into a film that research already knew was in trouble |
| Aggregator score treated as quality | Wrong lesson learned, wrong film greenlit next (`02-development.md` §8) |
| Reputation left unmanaged after release | A library title quietly loses the standing that would have supported a re-release, a remake, or a licensing premium (§11.4) |
