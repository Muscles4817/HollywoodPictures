# 02 — Development

*From "there might be a film here" to a greenlit screenplay.* Development is
cheap relative to production and consumes years. Most projects die here, and
that is the system working as intended.

> **Domain reference.** Real industry, not this game. See `README.md`.

---

## 1. The shape of it

```
SOURCE MATERIAL ──► RIGHTS ──► WRITER ──► DRAFTS ──► PACKAGE ──► GREENLIGHT
   (idea, book,     (option,   (hired    (notes,    (director,   (money is
    life story,      purchase,  or spec)  rewrites,   cast,        committed,
    remake, spec)    chain of             polishes)   budget)      a date is set)
                     title)
```

Industry rule of thumb: of projects a studio puts into active development,
somewhere around **1 in 10 to 1 in 20** gets made, and the figure is worse for
speculative material than for pre-sold IP. Development spend on a studio slate
runs into the tens of millions a year and is largely written off.

**Development time** for a studio feature: **2–5 years** from acquisition to
principal photography is normal. Fast is 12–18 months. Slow is a decade.

## 2. Where material comes from

| Source | How it arrives | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Spec script** | Written on speculation, sent out by an agent | The romantic route; a small fraction of production now |
| **Pitch** | Writer/producer verbally pitches an idea in the room | Sold as "pitch" → studio pays for the script |
| **Literary IP** | Books, articles, comics, plays | Options negotiated with publishers/authors |
| **Life rights** | A real person's cooperation and story | Not strictly required for public facts, but bought to secure cooperation and reduce defamation/right-of-publicity risk |
| **Remake / adaptation of foreign film** | Rights bought from the original rightsholder | |
| **Sequel / franchise extension** | Studio already owns it | The dominant source of studio tentpoles |
| **Underlying game / toy / brand** | Licensed from the owner | Usually with heavy approval rights |
| **Original from a filmmaker** | A director's own project | Often depends entirely on their heat |

### 2.1 The spec market

A spec goes out to buyers ("**wide release**" to the town, or a controlled
list) on a Friday, executives read over the weekend, and offers come Monday.
A hot spec triggers a bidding war; the terms are typically "**against**" —
e.g. *$500K against $1M* means $500K on purchase, rising to $1M if the film is
made. The spec market has shrunk enormously since the 1990s; studios now buy
far more IP and pitches than finished specs.

## 3. Rights: options and purchases

You cannot develop what you don't control.

- **Option** — the exclusive right, for a limited period, to buy the underlying
  work at a pre-agreed price. Typical shape: **10% of the purchase price for
  an 18-month term**, with one or two extensions at a further payment.
  Options for modest literary properties can be low four figures; for hot books
  they can be six.
- **Purchase price** — payable on exercise, usually at the start of principal
  photography. Frequently expressed as a **percentage of budget** (e.g. 2–3%)
  with a floor and ceiling.
- **Reversion** — if the option lapses unexercised, rights return to the author,
  who can sell again.
- **Chain of title** — the documented, unbroken record that the producer owns
  what they claim to own. A distributor's legal department will not close
  without it, and **E&O insurance** (errors and omissions) cannot be bound
  without it.
- **Clearances** — trademarks, real names, song lyrics, artwork visible on
  screen, depicted brands. A research/clearance report is standard.

## 4. The writer

Writers are engaged step-by-step, not open-endedly. WGA minimums set floors by
**budget tier** (a "low budget" and "high budget" split, with the threshold
periodically renegotiated — historically around $5M, later higher).

A typical **step deal**:

| Step | What it is | Typical duration |
|---|---|---|
| **Treatment / outline** | Prose summary of the story, 5–40 pages | 4–8 weeks |
| **First draft** | The screenplay | 10–16 weeks |
| **Second draft / revision** | Addressing notes | 6–10 weeks |
| **Polish** | Dialogue and scene-level work, no structural change | 2–4 weeks |
| **Production polish / rewrite** | During prep or shoot, often a different writer | Days to weeks |

The studio holds **options on subsequent steps**: it can stop after any step.
This is what makes development cheap to abandon.

**Rewrites by other writers** are routine on studio films — a structure writer,
then a character writer, then a "dialogue punch-up," sometimes a genre
specialist. Big tentpoles can pass through a dozen writers. This is why credits
often look nothing like the actual authorship history (§7).

Rates: WGA scale for a high-budget original screenplay and treatment runs in
the low-to-mid six figures. Established writers command multiples of scale;
top-tier rewrite specialists have historically been paid **$200K–$500K+ a
week** for production rewrites.

## 5. Coverage and the reading system

Studios and agencies receive far more material than executives can read, so a
**reader** produces **coverage**: a one-page logline + synopsis + comment, with
box-scores on premise, story, structure, characters, dialogue, and a verdict of
**PASS / CONSIDER / RECOMMEND**.

- A RECOMMEND is rare and career-relevant for the reader.
- Coverage is *tracked*: a bad coverage follows a script around town, because
  executives share intelligence.
- Reading a script yourself is a status signal; being covered is the default.

---

## 6. Notes and the executive relationship

The creative executive's product is **notes**. Development notes come from:

- the creative exec and their superiors (studio notes);
- the producer(s);
- the director, once attached;
- the star, once attached (often via their own development person);
- **research** — see `09-marketing-and-distribution.md` for test screenings
  during post.

Notes conflict constantly, and reconciling them is the writer's real job.
Terms of art: *the note behind the note* (the actual problem, versus the
prescribed fix); *page one rewrite* (start again); *lateral* (a change that
neither improves nor worsens).

## 7. WGA credit arbitration

Screen credit is **not** decided by the studio. When there is any dispute — or
automatically whenever a production-company-affiliated writer claims credit —
the WGA runs a blind arbitration: three anonymous arbiters read every draft and
apply the Screen Credits Manual.

Rules that matter:

- Credit reads **"Written by"** (story + screenplay by the same hand),
  **"Screenplay by"**, **"Story by"**, and **"Screen Story by"**.
- **"&"** means a *writing team*; **"and"** means *separate, sequential*
  writers. This distinction is meaningful and load-bearing.
- A **subsequent writer** must contribute **more than 50%** of the final
  screenplay to gain credit (33% for an original screenplay's first writer
  protection). This is why so many rewriters end up uncredited.
- Credit determines **residuals**, **separated rights** (sequel/publication
  payments), and awards eligibility. It is worth real money.

Maximum credited writers on a screenplay is capped (generally three writers or
two teams).

## 8. Greenlight

The greenlight is the moment the studio commits the negative cost. It requires,
approximately:

1. **A script the studio believes in** — or at least believes it can shoot.
2. **A director** (see `03-financing-and-dealmaking.md` §2 on attachments).
3. **Cast** sufficient to justify the budget, sometimes "pay-or-play" already.
4. **A budget and schedule** validated by physical production, with a
   **contingency** (typically **10%**) and often incentive money already
   modelled.
5. **A release date and a marketing view** — can we sell this, to whom, when.
6. **A financial model** — projections across theatrical, international, home
   entertainment, licensing, with a downside case.

Committees vary; the decision usually sits with the studio chairman with the
head of production, marketing, distribution, and finance in the room.

**Soft greenlight / prep-to-greenlight**: the studio funds prep (hiring
department heads, scouting, building) *before* full commitment. This is common
and expensive — abandoning at this stage still writes off millions.

**Pay-or-play** is the hard commitment: the studio owes the talent their fee
whether or not the film is made. Once several pay-or-play deals are signed, the
economics of *not* making the film get worse than making it, which is how bad
films get made.

## 9. Turnaround and development hell

- **Turnaround** — the studio abandons a project and allows another buyer to
  take it, on condition of reimbursing costs (development spend plus interest,
  plus sometimes a premium) usually within a set window. Many well-known films
  were made in turnaround from another studio.
- **Development hell** — a project that cycles endlessly through writers and
  regimes without dying or being made. Caused by: the underlying rights being
  valuable enough to hold but not to make; regime change (a new head of
  production has no ownership of the previous one's slate); or an unsolvable
  script problem.
- **Shelving/abandonment for tax or strategic reasons** — a completed or
  near-complete film written off rather than released, an option that only
  makes sense under specific accounting conditions.

---

## 10. The development department's outputs

Concretely, what exists on paper at the end of development:

- **The screenplay**, in a locked-page revision colour sequence
  (white → blue → pink → yellow → green → goldenrod → …).
- A **series of drafts** with dates, which is the arbitration record.
- **The rights file** — chain of title, options, clearances.
- A **budget top sheet** and **schedule** (see `04-preproduction.md`).
- The **package** — director and principal cast attachments.
- **Comparables** ("comps") — the films this one is being modelled against
  financially, which is how marketing and finance argue about it.
