# 02 — Development

*From "there might be a film here" to a greenlit screenplay.* Development is
cheap relative to production and consumes years. Most projects die here, and
that is the system working as intended.

> **Domain reference.** Real industry, not this game. See `README.md`.

---

## 1. The shape of it

```
SOURCE MATERIAL ──► RIGHTS ──► WRITER ──► DRAFTS ──► PACKAGE ──► GREENLIGHT
   (idea, book,     (option,   (hired    (notes,    (director,   (money is
    life story,      purchase,  or spec)  rewrites,   cast,        committed,
    remake, spec)    chain of             polishes)   budget)      a date is set)
                     title)
```

Industry rule of thumb: of projects a studio puts into active development,
somewhere around **1 in 10 to 1 in 20** gets made, and the figure is worse for
speculative material than for pre-sold IP. Development spend on a studio slate
runs into the tens of millions a year and is largely written off.

**Development time** for a studio feature: **2–5 years** from acquisition to
principal photography is normal. Fast is 12–18 months. Slow is a decade.

Development is also the only stage where the cost of being wrong is small.
Every later stage multiplies the cost of an unsolved script problem by roughly
an order of magnitude: a structural flaw costs a writer's fee to fix in
development, a week of shooting to patch in production, and a reshoot plus a
recut to paper over in post (`07-postproduction.md` §2). This is why
experienced producers are willing to spend two more years and two more writers
rather than start shooting on a draft they don't believe.

### 1.1 The cast of development

Development has no crew. It is a small number of salaried people, a much
larger number of freelancers, and a lot of telephone.

| Role | Sits at | What they actually do | Judged on |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reader / story analyst** | Studio, agency, prodco | Reads submissions and writes coverage (§5). Often freelance or an assistant doing it after hours | Accuracy, and whether their RECOMMENDs hold up |
| **Creative executive (CE)** | Studio or prodco | Finds material, gives notes, shepherds projects, keeps writer relationships. The engine room | Relationships, taste, and how many projects reach production |
| **Director of development / VP** | Prodco or studio | Runs a slate of 8–20 active projects; hires writers, runs notes calls | Slate throughput; whether their projects survive regime change |
| **SVP / EVP Production** | Studio | Owns a genre or a band of the slate; presents projects upward | Getting films made that work |
| **President of Production** | Studio | Approves what gets bought, who writes it, and what goes to greenlight | The slate's performance, over a 3–5 year lag |
| **Studio chairman / CEO** | Studio | Says yes to the money (§8) | Everything |
| **Producer** | Independent, often with a first-look deal | Finds the material, hires and manages the writer, protects the project through regimes | Getting films made; a producer with no films made stops getting calls |
| **Business affairs executive** | Studio | Negotiates option, purchase and writer deals; polices chain of title | Deals closing on terms that hold up, quickly |
| **Literary agent** | Agency | Sells specs and pitches, gets clients onto assignment lists | Client income and client heat |
| **Manager** | Management company | Career strategy, material development, often produces | Same, plus producing credits |
| **Book / literary scout** | Independent, retained | Reads manuscripts before publication and flags them (§2.2) | Being first, and being right |
| **Writer** | Freelance, per step | Writes (§4) | Delivering a draft that solves the problem, on time |

The structural fact: **almost nobody in development has authority to say yes,
and almost everybody has authority to say no.** A project needs a chain of
advocates, each of whom must sell it to the next rung. This is why "who is
your champion at the studio?" is a real and load-bearing question, and why a
project loses momentum instantly when its champion leaves (§9.4).

### 1.2 The attrition funnel

Directional, for a major studio's live-action slate in a normal year. The
numbers vary enormously by studio and era, but the *shape* is stable.

| Stage | Order of magnitude | Survival to next stage |
|---|---|---|
| Submissions received (scripts, books, pitches) | 5,000–20,000/yr | <5% get past coverage |
| Material seriously considered by an executive | 500–1,500 | ~10–20% |
| Acquired: options, pitch buys, spec buys | 50–150 | — |
| Active development slate at any moment | 75–200 projects | — |
| Projects that reach a draft the studio likes | 30–60 | — |
| Projects that reach a real package | 20–30 | — |
| Greenlit | 10–25 | — |
| Released | 8–20 | — |

Reading it the other way: for every film on a studio's release schedule, a
dozen or more projects were bought, written, and abandoned, and hundreds were
read and passed on. The cost of that failure is the price of development, and
it is budgeted as such — a studio expects to write off **60–90%** of its
annual development spend.

## 2. Where material comes from

| Source | How it arrives | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Spec script** | Written on speculation, sent out by an agent | The romantic route; a small fraction of production now (§2.1) |
| **Pitch** | Writer/producer verbally pitches an idea in the room | Sold as "pitch" → studio pays for the script (§13) |
| **Literary IP** | Books, articles, comics, plays | Options negotiated with publishers/authors (§2.2, §2.3) |
| **Life rights** | A real person's cooperation and story | Not strictly required for public facts, but bought to secure cooperation and reduce defamation/right-of-publicity risk (§2.4) |
| **Remake / adaptation of foreign film** | Rights bought from the original rightsholder | Often via a sales agent at a festival; "remake rights" are frequently held back from the original financing deal |
| **Sequel / franchise extension** | Studio already owns it | The dominant source of studio tentpoles; development starts from the rights, not from the story |
| **Underlying game / toy / brand** | Licensed from the owner | Usually with heavy approval rights (§2.5) |
| **Original from a filmmaker** | A director's own project | Often depends entirely on their heat |
| **Article / long-form journalism** | Magazine feature, investigative piece | Cheap, prestigious, and fast-moving; often optioned within days of publication |
| **Podcast / documentary** | Narrative podcast or a doc's underlying story | A growth source since roughly the late 2010s |
| **Internal studio-generated idea** | An executive's idea, given to a writer | Studio owns it outright; the writer is hired to execute |

### 2.1 The spec market

A **spec** is a screenplay written without a buyer. Selling one is a
compressed, choreographed event, and the choreography is the same whether the
script sells for $50,000 or $3M.

**The pre-launch (2–6 weeks before going out).** The agent reads it, decides
it's ready or sends the writer back for one more pass, and — critically —
decides whether to *attach* first. A script that goes out naked competes on
its own merits; a script that goes out with a producer, a director, or an
actor attached is a different and usually more valuable proposition. So the
agent frequently does a quiet round to producers with studio deals first, to
generate an attachment, which also warms up the studio the producer is housed
at. The counter-argument is speed and heat: attachments narrow the field and
give the buyer a partner they may not want.

**The list.** The agent builds the buyer list — typically **8–20 targets**,
each a named executive rather than a company: the studio creative executives
who buy in that genre, streamers, and the well-capitalised independents. A
"wide" go-out hits everyone; a "controlled" or "soft" go-out hits three or
four, either to protect the writer from a public failure or because the agent
believes one buyer is obviously right.

**The weekend, hour by hour** (Pacific time; this is the classic pattern):

| When | What happens | Who |
|---|---|---|
| Wed–Thu | Agent calls buyers: "we're going out Friday with a Ridley-esque survival thriller from the writer of X." Loglines circulate; no script yet | Agent, execs |
| Fri 10am–1pm | Script emails out simultaneously with a cover note: title, writer, reps, attachments, comps, and the process ("we'll be discussing Monday") | Agent |
| Fri afternoon | Execs triage. Junior execs and assistants are handed rush reads; the exec reads it themselves if the logline is hot. Coverage is ordered on the rest | CE, assistants, readers |
| Fri evening | Tracking boards and group chats light up. Execs call each other for temperature: "are you in?" Everyone lies a little | Everyone |
| Sat | The exec who likes it starts internal advocacy: gets their boss to read overnight, sometimes pulls in marketing or physical production for a sanity read on cost | CE → SVP → President of Production |
| Sat–Sun | The agent works the phones, feeding real and implied interest back into the field. A serious buyer asks for a call with the writer — the "meet-and-charm" — to check the writer can talk about it and would take notes | Agent, writer, execs |
| Sun any time | **Preempt window.** A buyer who wants to end the process offers early at a premium with a short fuse | Buyer's business affairs |
| Mon 9–11am | Offers land by phone as terms, not paper: purchase price "against", bonuses, guaranteed steps, producer and director attachments, sequel/remake payments | Business affairs, agent |
| Mon midday | The agent runs rounds. "You're not the highest." Best-and-final by a stated deadline | Agent |
| Mon–Tue | Deal memo. Long-form contract follows in 4–12 weeks | Business affairs, lawyer |

**What a preempt actually is.** A pre-emptive bid is an offer made *before*
the buyer knows what the competition will pay, priced deliberately above the
market to take the script off it, and carrying an expiry — "this is good until
10am Sunday." The buyer is paying a premium (commonly **1.5–2×** what they
think it would clear in an open process) to buy certainty and to avoid a war
they might lose. The seller trades upside for a guaranteed close and, often,
better *non-price* terms: a bigger producer, a director commitment, a
guaranteed second step, a production bonus. Agents preempt-shop deliberately
by hinting a number they would accept early.

**What the money actually looks like.** Terms are quoted "**against**" — *$500K
against $1M* means $500K on signature and a further $500K if the film is
produced. Around that sit: **production bonus** (often tied to sole credit),
**box-office bonuses**, **sequel and remake payments** (typically **50%** of
original compensation for a sequel, **33%** for a remake, plus a fee for
episodic), **separated rights** under the WGA MBA for original material (§7),
and a **guaranteed rewrite step** — which for many writers matters more than
the headline number, because it keeps them on their own film.

**The failure mode.** Most scripts that go out do not sell. A script that goes
wide and gets passes from everyone is "**burnt**": it can only realistically
go out again after a substantial rewrite, a new attachment, and 6–12 months of
forgetting, and even then some executives will just re-read their reader's old
coverage (§5.5). This is why agents are conservative about going wide, and
why "we're going out to four people" is often a mercy.

**Scale.** The spec market peaked in the 1990s, when well over a hundred specs
sold in a year and seven-figure sales were regular news. It contracted sharply
after 2008 and again with the shift to franchise IP: the modern market is a
few dozen meaningful sales a year, weighted toward contained thrillers,
horror, and anything with an obvious one-line hook. The spec's main function
today is often not the sale but the **sample** — the script that gets the
writer meetings and assignments (§4.1), which is where most working screen
writers actually earn.

### 2.2 How material is actually found

Executives do not find material by waiting for it. The pipeline has specific,
unglamorous machinery.

**Book scouts.** A small profession, concentrated in New York and London,
retained on monthly fees (roughly **$2,000–$10,000/month**) by studios,
production companies, streamers and foreign publishers. A scout's job is to
read what is not yet public: manuscripts on submission to publishers, galleys
and bound proofs, editors' enthusiasms, and agents' upcoming lists. They send
short flash reports — a paragraph of pitch, a paragraph of assessment, an
explicit urgency rating ("read this tonight" vs "for your awareness"). Their
value is entirely in the **lead time**: seeing a manuscript six to eighteen
months before publication means bidding before there is a bestseller list to
bid against.

**How a book reaches a film buyer, in order:**

1. Author writes it; literary agent takes it on.
2. The literary agency's **film/TV rights department** (in-house, or a
   co-agent at a Los Angeles agency) reads the manuscript at the same time as
   publishers do.
3. The manuscript goes out to publishers "on submission." Scouts see it here.
4. If publishing interest is hot, the film agent goes out **before**
   publication, sometimes before the book deal closes, on the theory that
   heat is transferable and cheaper now than later.
5. Otherwise the film go-out is timed to a milestone: the book deal
   announcement, the galley, publication, a review, a list placement.
6. Producers and studio execs read; a producer who moves fastest often gets an
   exclusive negotiating window rather than an auction.

**Agency literary departments** perform the same function internally for
their own clients, and package aggressively: an agency that represents the
author, a writer to adapt, and a director will try to sell all three together
(`01-industry-structure.md` on packaging and its post-2019 upheaval).

**Articles.** Feature journalism is the cheapest strong source in the
business. The pattern is fast and specific: a long piece publishes on a
Tuesday, twenty producers email the writer or the publication's rights person
by Wednesday, and it is optioned by the following week for **$5K–$50K**
against a purchase in the low-to-mid six figures. Publications now run
standing film/TV rights operations; staff journalists' contracts vary on who
controls the rights, which is the first question a producer's lawyer asks.
Sophisticated producers ask the journalist's agent for a look *before*
publication.

**Tracking and the grid.** Every development department maintains a tracking
document — the "grid" — listing every property it is watching: title, source,
rightsholder, who controls it, status, option expiry date, competing interest,
and the internal champion. Two disciplines matter:

- **Option expiry tracking.** Options lapse constantly. A department that
  knows a rival's option on a good novel expires in March can be on the phone
  to the author's agent in February. Reversion (§3) creates a steady flow of
  re-available material, and it is one of the few genuinely cheap ways to
  acquire something already proven to interest somebody.
- **Competitive tracking.** Informal executive networks — historically
  bulletin boards and mailing lists, now group chats — share what has gone
  out, who is bidding, who passed and why. It is gossip with an operational
  function: it sets the price.

### 2.3 Optioning IP: what actually gets negotiated

The headline is the option fee. The deal is everything under it, and the
under-it items are what determine whether the project is makeable.

| Term | What it means | Typical shape |
|---|---|---|
| **Option fee** | Payment for exclusivity for a term | ~**10% of the purchase price**, applicable against it |
| **Term** | Length of exclusivity | **12–18 months**, one or two extensions of 12 months at a further payment (often 10% and *non*-applicable) |
| **Purchase price** | Payable on exercise, at start of principal photography | Fixed sum, or **2–3% of budget** with a floor and ceiling |
| **Rights granted** | What you actually bought | Motion picture, sequels, prequels, remakes, series, all media now known or hereafter devised, merchandising, in perpetuity, throughout the universe |
| **Reserved rights** | What the author keeps | Print publication, author-written sequels, sometimes stage, sometimes radio/podcast |
| **Holdbacks** | What the author agrees not to do meanwhile | Not to publish a sequel or license a competing adaptation for N years |
| **Backend** | Author's participation | **2.5–5% of net proceeds**, occasionally a small gross definition for a major author |
| **Bonuses** | Extra payments on events | Box-office bonuses, sequel/remake payments (50%/33% of purchase is common), award bonuses |
| **Credit** | How the author is billed | "Based on the novel by…", size and placement specified |
| **Consultation / approval** | The dangerous clause | Consultation is normal; *approval* over script, casting, or director is rare and makes a project harder to finance |
| **Reversion** | What happens if nothing is made | Rights return if the option lapses, sometimes with a right of first refusal or a repayment of costs |

The negotiation is with the author's **literary agent** and often the
publisher's subsidiary rights department, and the producer's counterpart is a
**business affairs executive** or, for an independent producer, an
entertainment lawyer. Two structural traps:

- **Underlying-work chain.** The author may not own everything: a novel that
  itself adapts something, a comic with a work-for-hire creator dispute, a
  memoir describing living people who have not signed anything (§2.4), or a
  co-authored work where one co-author is unreachable.
- **Copyright termination.** In the US, authors and their heirs can terminate
  a grant of copyright after a statutory window (broadly, 35 years for
  post-1978 grants; a different regime for older ones). For long-lived
  franchises this is a recurring and expensive fact of life, and it is why
  legal departments care about the *date* of the original grant.

**Shopping agreements** are the poor relation of options: no money, no
exclusivity of ownership, just a written right to take the material to buyers
for a short period (60–180 days). Authors' agents dislike them; producers
without development money live on them.

### 2.4 Life rights: what you are actually buying

There is no such thing as "life rights" in law. What the phrase describes is a
bundle bought in one agreement, and understanding the bundle explains what the
deal is for.

1. **A release and covenant not to sue** — the subject waives claims for
   defamation, false light, invasion of privacy, and violation of the right of
   publicity, and agrees the portrayal may be fictionalised. This is the legal
   core, and it is what the insurer cares about.
2. **Exclusive cooperation** — the subject agrees to work with this production
   and not a competing one, for a term. This is the *commercial* core: public
   facts are usually free to dramatise, but the subject's exclusive
   cooperation is not, and an authorised film with the subject's blessing
   beats an unauthorised one in the press and often at the sales stage.
3. **Access to material** — interviews, letters, diaries, photographs,
   home video, medical or court records the subject can release, and
   introductions to friends and family who then sign their own releases.
4. **Consultation** — the subject reads a draft and comments. Occasionally an
   approval right over specific sensitive depictions; full script approval is
   resisted hard by buyers.
5. **Confidentiality** while the project develops.

**How the negotiation actually goes.** Speed dominates. When a story breaks —
a rescue, a trial, a scandal — producers reach the subject's lawyer within
**24–72 hours**, and the subject frequently has no representation, so the
first call is often "you need a lawyer, here are three names." The subject's
lawyer then runs a small auction. Terms:

| Element | Typical range |
|---|---|
| Option (per year) | $5K–$75K, applicable against purchase |
| Purchase price | $50K–$500K; a genuinely major subject can exceed $1M |
| Consulting fee | $25K–$150K, sometimes plus expenses and a set visit |
| Backend | 1–5% of net proceeds |
| Term of exclusivity | 2–5 years, extendable |

**Multiple parties.** A single story almost never has a single subject. A
disaster film needs the survivors, the rescuers, the family of the deceased,
and sometimes the villain. Producers triage: buy the people whose consent is
legally necessary (living, identifiable, portrayed unflatteringly), buy the
people whose cooperation is *practically* necessary (the narrative's centre),
and rely on the public record for the rest. Composite and renamed characters
exist mostly to solve this problem.

**The dead and the distant.** In most US jurisdictions the dead cannot be
defamed, which is why historical subjects are cheap — but several states,
notably California, recognise a **post-mortem right of publicity** (California
runs 70 years after death), which can require the estate's consent for
commercial exploitation of name and likeness. Practice varies by state and by
country, and UK and European privacy law is materially less permissive toward
unauthorised depiction than US First Amendment practice.

**The annotated script.** For any fact-based film, the studio's legal
department will require an **annotation**: a copy of the script with every
factual assertion footnoted to a source — a transcript, a court document, a
published article, an interview recording. It is done line by line by a
researcher or the writer, takes **3–8 weeks**, and costs **$20K–$100K**. E&O
insurers ask for it and price off it. Scenes that cannot be annotated get
rewritten, softened, or moved onto a composite character.

### 2.5 Licensed brands, games, and toys

A licence from a brand owner behaves nothing like an option from an author.
The licensor has an existing business to protect and typically demands:

- **Approval over script, key art, casting, and merchandising**, often at
  multiple stages and with defined turnaround times.
- **Brand-safety restrictions** — rating ceilings (a PG-13 cap is common),
  content prohibitions, and limits on how the property's characters may
  behave.
- **A large guarantee against royalties**, plus merchandising splits that can
  be worth more than the film's own participation.
- **Term and territory limits**, and reversion if a film is not in production
  by a fixed date — which is the clock that drives an enormous amount of
  otherwise inexplicable studio behaviour.

The upside is pre-existing awareness, which is what the marketing department
is really buying (`09-marketing-and-distribution.md` §2). The cost is that
creative decisions now have a veto-holder who is not in the film business.

## 3. Rights: options and purchases

You cannot develop what you don't control.

- **Option** — the exclusive right, for a limited period, to buy the underlying
  work at a pre-agreed price. Typical shape: **10% of the purchase price for
  an 18-month term**, with one or two extensions at a further payment.
  Options for modest literary properties can be low four figures; for hot books
  they can be six.
- **Purchase price** — payable on exercise, usually at the start of principal
  photography. Frequently expressed as a **percentage of budget** (e.g. 2–3%)
  with a floor and ceiling.
- **Reversion** — if the option lapses unexercised, rights return to the author,
  who can sell again.
- **Chain of title** — the documented, unbroken record that the producer owns
  what they claim to own. A distributor's legal department will not close
  without it, and **E&O insurance** (errors and omissions) cannot be bound
  without it.
- **Clearances** — trademarks, real names, song lyrics, artwork visible on
  screen, depicted brands. A research/clearance report is standard.

### 3.1 What a chain of title actually consists of

"Chain of title" sounds abstract. It is a physical folder — now a shared
drive — that a distributor's lawyer, a completion guarantor
(`03-financing-and-dealmaking.md` §7) and an E&O underwriter will each read
document by document before releasing money. A complete chain contains,
roughly in this order:

| # | Document | Who produces it | Why it exists |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Copyright registration certificate** for the underlying work, plus registrations of any prior adaptations | Author / prior rightsholder | Proves what exists and who registered it |
| 2 | **Copyright search report** — a professional search of Copyright Office records for registrations, renewals, assignments, security interests and terminations | A copyright search firm (5–15 business days, ~$500–$2,500) | Finds the grants nobody remembered |
| 3 | **Title report** — search for conflicting film, TV and trademark titles | A title search service; MPA member studios also use the industry title registration system | Stops you naming your film something already registered |
| 4 | **Option / purchase agreement**, fully signed, with all exhibits | Business affairs / lawyers | The grant itself |
| 5 | **Short-form option and short-form assignment**, recorded with the US Copyright Office | Lawyers | Public notice of the grant, so a later buyer cannot claim ignorance |
| 6 | **Author's representations and warranties**, plus indemnity | In the purchase agreement | The insurer's fallback |
| 7 | **Certificates of authorship / writer employment agreements** for *every* writer who touched it — including the friend who did an unpaid pass and the writer the previous producer hired in 2014 | Business affairs | Establishes work-made-for-hire; a missing one is the single most common break |
| 8 | **Guild signatory paperwork and evidence that prior writers were paid** | Production company | The WGA can block assignment where obligations are unpaid |
| 9 | **Life rights agreements, depiction releases and consents** from named living people | Producer | Defamation / publicity exposure |
| 10 | **Assignments through every entity** — author → producer → single-purpose production company → financier/distributor | Lawyers | The film is owned by an SPV; each hop must be papered |
| 11 | **Lien and UCC searches**, plus release of prior security interests | Lawyer / search firm | An old financier's lien on the script kills a closing |
| 12 | **Script clearance report** and legal clearance memo (§3.2) | Clearance service + studio legal | Names, brands, addresses, artwork |
| 13 | **Music licences** — synchronisation *and* master use, per cue | Music clearance | Two separate rights; either can be refused |
| 14 | **Film clip, stock footage and still photo licences**, with any required guild reuse payments and performer consents | Clearance | Clips are the most expensive small item in a film |
| 15 | **Location agreements, appearance releases, product/trademark consents** | Production, during the shoot | Delivered at the end, but part of the same file |
| 16 | **E&O insurance policy** (commonly $1M/$3M limits; premium roughly $8K–$25K for a feature) | Broker | Nothing distributes without it |

The chain is assembled continuously, not at the end, and it is the reason
business affairs cares about paperwork that looks like bureaucracy: the
delivery schedule of a distribution agreement (`09` §1) lists these documents
explicitly, and **a missing item stops payment**.

### 3.2 How clearance is actually performed

Script clearance is a discrete, purchasable service, and the process is
mechanical.

1. The locked-ish script goes to a **script clearance company**. Turnaround is
   **5–10 business days**; cost runs roughly **$1,500–$5,000** for a feature,
   with rush and re-clearance passes extra.
2. The clearance report goes through the script **page by page** and flags
   every: character name, business name, brand, product, street address, phone
   number, licence plate, website, email address, radio/TV station, book,
   song, artwork, newspaper, logo, uniform, and any real person referenced.
3. Each flag is returned with a status: **clear as written**, **use a
   fictional substitute**, **needs written consent**, or **conflict — do not
   use**. Names are checked against directories and public records for real
   people with the same name in the same city and occupation as the character,
   because that is the fact pattern that produces a claim.
4. Studio legal issues a **clearance memo** to production, art department,
   props, and the writer. This is where the conventions come from: 555 phone
   numbers, invented area codes, fictional brands, blurred logos, and made-up
   corporations that sound almost real.
5. Anything requiring consent goes to a clearance coordinator to chase.
   Trademark owners refuse routinely, especially where the depiction is
   unflattering.
6. **Re-clearance** happens on script revisions and again on the picture-lock
   cut, because things enter the frame that were never in the script — a
   poster on a wall, a T-shirt, a magazine on a table (`07` §7).

Music is a separate track and the slowest one: a needle-drop requires
**both** the publisher's synchronisation licence and the label's master use
licence, either party can refuse for any reason, and quotes for a recognisable
song in a studio film routinely land in the **$25K–$500K+** range per side.
Writing a specific irreplaceable song into the *story* is a well-known
unforced error.

### 3.3 What actually kills a rights deal

| Failure | How it shows up | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Missing certificate of authorship** | A writer from three regimes ago never signed | Cannot close distribution; that writer now has leverage and knows it |
| **Undisclosed co-author** | A collaborator claims joint authorship of the underlying work | Injunction risk; the deal freezes until bought out |
| **Prior option never formally terminated** | The old producer's paperwork says they still have rights | Weeks-to-months of lawyer time, sometimes a payment |
| **Rights narrower than assumed** | The option covers the novel but not the sequel characters, or excludes TV/stage | The franchise plan evaporates |
| **Copyright termination notice** | Author's heirs serve notice under the statutory window | The property may become unusable, or expensive, on a fixed future date |
| **Public-domain confusion** | The novel is public domain, but the elements you love were invented by a later film | Removing them guts the adaptation |
| **Estate refusal / competing authorised project** | The family signed with someone else | Yours becomes the unauthorised version — harder to cast, harder to insure |
| **Unclearable real person** | A living, identifiable individual portrayed as a criminal without a release or an annotation | Legal requires rewriting, compositing, or fictionalising |
| **Unclearable music** | The plot depends on a song the estate won't licence | Rewrite, or pay a fee that distorts the budget |
| **Chain break discovered late** | Found during delivery, not development | Delivery fails, payment is withheld, release date at risk |
| **No E&O** | Underwriter refuses on the clearance report | The film cannot be distributed at all |

The pattern is consistent: rights problems are **cheap in development and
catastrophic at delivery**, and their cost curve is steeper than any other
category of problem in filmmaking, because the remedies at the end are legal
rather than creative.

## 4. The writer

Writers are engaged step-by-step, not open-endedly. WGA minimums set floors by
**budget tier** (a "low budget" and "high budget" split, with the threshold
periodically renegotiated — historically around $5M, later higher).

### 4.1 How a writer gets the job

Two routes, and they feel completely different from the inside.

**Route 1 — the writer sells something** (a spec, §2.1, or a pitch, §13). The
writer owns the idea and the buyer is buying it, so the writer has leverage,
gets a guaranteed rewrite step, and usually keeps the project longer.

**Route 2 — the open writing assignment (OWA)**, which is how most working
screenwriters actually earn. The studio or producer controls the material — a
novel, a remake, a sequel, an internal idea, or a script that needs
rebuilding — and needs a writer. The sequence:

| Step | What happens | Typical timing |
|---|---|---|
| **The list** | Exec and producer build a list of 10–30 writers, ranked, annotated with samples, availability, quote and genre fit | 1 week |
| **Availability check** | Calls to agents: is the client free, would they read it | Days |
| **The material goes out** | Book/script/brief sent, often with a short statement of what the studio thinks the movie is | — |
| **The writer reads and decides whether to "have a take"** | Unpaid. This is the contested part of the profession | 1–2 weeks |
| **The take meeting** | Writer pitches their version to producer and exec, 20–45 minutes | — |
| **The bake-off** | Several writers pitch the same job, sometimes six or more, sometimes over weeks | 2–6 weeks total |
| **The studio round** | The favourite re-pitches to the studio exec's boss | 1–2 weeks later |
| **Offer and deal** | Business affairs negotiates steps, money, and start date | 2–8 weeks |
| **Kick-off / launch meeting** | Everyone agrees what is being written before the writer leaves the room | Day 1 of the step |

**The take is the product.** A "take" is not a summary of the source material;
it is the writer's argument for *what the movie is*, and specifically their
solution to the problem the buyer already knows they have. Excellent takes do
four things: they name the problem out loud before anyone else does ("the
book's ending is internal, and internal endings don't film"), they propose a
structural solution rather than a tonal adjective, they show the writer's
personal reason for wanting it, and they include two or three concrete scenes
vivid enough that the exec repeats them upstairs — because the exec will have
to re-pitch this in a room the writer is not in. Mediocre takes recite the
plot, promise to be "faithful but cinematic," and leave the room with nothing
quotable.

Bake-offs are the profession's chronic grievance: preparing a serious take is
one to three weeks of unpaid work, most of it wasted, and the practice has
been repeatedly targeted in WGA negotiations along with free rewrites and
"sweepstakes pitching."

### 4.2 What actually happens inside a writing step

A step is not "the writer goes away and returns with a draft." It has an
internal shape, and producers who understand it get better drafts.

```
KICK-OFF ──► RESEARCH ──► BEATS ──► OUTLINE ──► PAGES ──► WRITER'S ──► DELIVERY
 (align on    (2 days–   (cards,   (approved   (drafting  DRAFT       (formal,
  the movie)   4 weeks)   board)    or not)     10–40      (private)   dated)
                                                pages/wk)
```

1. **Kick-off / launch meeting.** Producer, executive, and writer agree in one
   room what is being written: the spine, the protagonist, the ending, the
   rating, roughly the budget class. Skipping this is the most common cause of
   a first draft that everyone hates for reasons nobody can articulate.
2. **Research.** For fact-based or specialist material: interviews,
   ride-alongs, reading, a technical consultant. Days to a month.
3. **Beats and cards.** The story broken into 40–60 beats, physically on index
   cards or a board, or in the writer's software. This is where structure is
   actually decided, and it is where a strong writer spends a
   disproportionate share of the time.
4. **The outline / treatment.** 5–40 pages of prose or beat list. Often a
   *paid contractual step* on studio deals precisely because it is the last
   cheap place to fix the story. Approval here is the real gate: a writer who
   goes to pages on an unapproved outline is gambling the whole step.
5. **Pages.** A working professional drafts **5–15 pages a day** in the good
   weeks and produces a first draft in **6–12 weeks** of actual writing.
6. **The writer's draft.** Almost every experienced writer produces a private
   version, then rewrites it once before anyone sees it. The version that gets
   delivered is typically the second or third pass, not the first.
7. **Delivery.** Formal, dated, and sent to the company — the date matters for
   payment, for the option on the next step, and later for arbitration (§7).
8. **The reading period.** The company has a contractual period to read and
   respond. The writer is off the clock and typically unpaid during it, and
   this is where months disappear: notes that were promised in two weeks
   arrive in eight, because the exec's boss hasn't read it, or the producer is
   in production on something else.

Payment within a step is usually split — commonly **commencement / delivery**
in halves, or thirds with an intermediate milestone. WGA rules prohibit **free
rewrites** and require minimum payments per step; "one-step deals," where a
studio guarantees only a single step, were a major point of contention in the
2010s because they compress a writer's income and remove their ability to fix
their own work.

### 4.3 The step deal

| Step | What it is | Typical duration |
|---|---|---|
| **Treatment / outline** | Prose summary of the story, 5–40 pages | 4–8 weeks |
| **First draft** | The screenplay | 10–16 weeks |
| **Second draft / revision** | Addressing notes | 6–10 weeks |
| **Polish** | Dialogue and scene-level work, no structural change | 2–4 weeks |
| **Production polish / rewrite** | During prep or shoot, often a different writer | Days to weeks |

The studio holds **options on subsequent steps**: it can stop after any step.
This is what makes development cheap to abandon. The elapsed time is always
longer than the working time — a "10–16 week first draft" typically sits
inside a **5–8 month** calendar cycle once the deal close, the kick-off, the
reading period, and the notes call are added.

### 4.4 The chain of writers

**Rewrites by other writers** are routine on studio films — a structure writer,
then a character writer, then a "dialogue punch-up," sometimes a genre
specialist. Big tentpoles can pass through a dozen writers. This is why credits
often look nothing like the actual authorship history (§7).

The specialisms are real and executives hire against them explicitly:

| Type of writer | Brought in for | Typical engagement |
|---|---|---|
| **Originating writer** | The idea, the voice, the world | Spec or first two steps |
| **Structure writer** | A second act that doesn't work; a broken spine | One rewrite, 8–12 weeks |
| **Character / emotion writer** | Relationships that don't land; a flat protagonist | Rewrite or polish |
| **Dialogue / comedy punch-up** | Lines that don't sing; jokes that don't land | 1–4 weeks, sometimes a room of writers |
| **Genre specialist** | Action set pieces, horror mechanics, heist logic | Targeted pass |
| **Star's writer** | A specific actor's voice and comfort | Polish, often at the actor's request |
| **Director's writer** | The director's long-time collaborator | Prep rewrite |
| **Production writer** | On-set fixes (§4.5) | Weeks, on location |

The cost of the chain is **drift**: each writer solves their brief and
introduces new problems, the draft accumulates compromises from three
regimes' worth of notes, and the thing that made the original material
distinctive gets sanded off. Experienced producers know that the fourth writer
is usually a symptom, not a cure, and that the highest-value move is often to
go back to the second draft and restart from there.

### 4.5 What a production rewrite actually fixes

By prep and shooting, a rewrite is not about quality in the abstract. It is
about making the film shootable, and the notes come from physical reality
rather than from taste. The recurring jobs:

1. **Money.** The budget came in $8M over. The rewrite removes a location,
   collapses two sequences into one, converts a night exterior to day,
   replaces a crowd with a suggestion of a crowd, and cuts the scene with the
   helicopter (`04-preproduction.md` §3).
2. **Schedule.** A location fell through, an actor is available for four days
   instead of eleven, or a sequence needs to move indoors for weather. Scenes
   are combined, relocated, or given to a different character.
3. **Actor fit.** A star reads the part and it doesn't fit their instrument,
   or their deal gives them meaningful input. Dialogue is retuned, an arc
   adjusted, a scene added to give them the moment they were promised.
4. **The table read.** Hearing the whole script aloud with the cast, usually
   1–3 weeks before shooting, exposes dead scenes, repetition, and jokes that
   don't work with brutal clarity. A pass follows immediately.
5. **Unshootable action.** "They fight through the market" is three words on
   the page and nine days on a schedule. The stunt coordinator and 2nd unit
   director need beats they can stage and cut.
6. **Clarity.** The read-through or the storyboards reveal that the audience
   will not understand a plot turn. A scene of setup gets added — usually the
   cheapest scene in the film, two people in a car.
7. **On-the-day fixes.** Pages issued in revision colours during the shoot
   (§10), sometimes written at 6am, for a scene being shot at 11am.
8. **Post-preview rewrites.** After test screenings (`07` §8), pages are
   written for reshoots — a new ending, a clarifying scene, a voiceover.

A production rewrite is paid by the **week** rather than the step, which is
why the rates look absurd relative to the page count: the studio is buying
availability, speed, and the ability to solve a $500,000-a-day problem
overnight.

### 4.6 What separates a good screenwriter from a bad one

Craft is treated at length in §12; this is the *professional* axis — what
makes a writer someone a studio hires again, which is not the same list.

1. **Structural diagnosis.** Being able to say what is wrong with a story —
   theirs or someone else's — in one sentence, and to say it at the outline
   stage rather than at page 90.
2. **Executability instinct.** Writing a film that can be made for what it is
   supposed to cost. A writer who reliably delivers a $60M script when asked
   for a $60M script is worth a premium, and everyone in the business knows
   who they are.
3. **Speed and reliability.** Delivering on the date. An enormous proportion
   of assignment work goes to the writer who will deliver in six weeks over
   the more talented writer who might deliver in six months.
4. **Taking notes without capitulating.** Hearing a bad note, extracting the
   real problem behind it (§6.4), and solving that. Writers fail in two
   symmetrical directions: those who take every note literally and produce
   incoherent drafts, and those who take none and are not rehired.
5. **The room.** Being articulate, likeable, and quick in a meeting. A writer
   who pitches well gets assignments a better writer doesn't (§13).
6. **Voice on the page.** The read experience — the script is a document
   read by a hundred people who are tired, and the writers who get made are
   the ones whose pages are *fun to read* (§12.8).
7. **Knowing whose film it is.** In development it is the studio's; in prep it
   becomes the director's. Writers who fight that at the wrong moment lose the
   job; writers who navigate it stay through production, which is where the
   credit protection and the relationships come from.

### 4.7 Rates

Rates: WGA scale for a high-budget original screenplay and treatment runs in
the low-to-mid six figures. Established writers command multiples of scale;
top-tier rewrite specialists have historically been paid **$200K–$500K+ a
week** for production rewrites.

The distribution is extreme and worth stating plainly: a small number of
writers earn seven figures a year on assignments, a working middle class earns
one or two studio steps a year, and the median WGA member earns nothing from
features in a given year. Guild membership numbers in the low five figures;
the number of writers who get a studio feature made in any year is in the low
hundreds (`12-talent-labor-and-careers.md` §1).

## 5. Coverage and the reading system

Studios and agencies receive far more material than executives can read, so a
**reader** produces **coverage**: a one-page logline + synopsis + comment, with
box-scores on premise, story, structure, characters, dialogue, and a verdict of
**PASS / CONSIDER / RECOMMEND**.

- A RECOMMEND is rare and career-relevant for the reader.
- Coverage is *tracked*: a bad coverage follows a script around town, because
  executives share intelligence.
- Reading a script yourself is a status signal; being covered is the default.

### 5.1 Who the reader actually is

Three populations, doing the same task under different conditions:

| Reader | Where | Pay | Volume |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Staff story analyst** | Major studios and networks; in the US organised under an IATSE story analysts local | Salaried, with a weekly script quota | 8–15 scripts/week |
| **Freelance reader** | Studios, prodcos, financiers, festivals, contests | **$40–$75 per script**, sometimes $100+ for a novel | As many as they can stand |
| **Assistant / intern** | Every agency, management company and prodco in town | Salary or nothing; reading is on top of the day job | Weekends and evenings |

The third group is the largest, and the most consequential: a very large share
of the industry's first-pass judgement is exercised by 24-year-olds reading at
11pm after a twelve-hour desk day. Everyone knows this, which is why a
producer's phone call carrying a script past the reader is worth so much.

### 5.2 What a reader actually does, and how long it takes

1. **Read** the script, once, straight through, taking notes in the margin —
   **90 minutes to 3 hours** for 110 pages. Novels take a day.
2. **Write the logline** — one or two sentences naming the protagonist, the
   inciting event, the goal, and the obstacle. This is the hardest single
   sentence in the job, and it is the sentence the executive will repeat.
3. **Write the synopsis** — **1–3 pages**, present tense, plot only, including
   the ending. Coverage synopses always spoil, because their purpose is to let
   an executive discuss a script they have not read.
4. **Write the comments** — half a page to two pages of assessment.
5. **Fill the grid and set the verdict.**

Total: **2.5–5 hours** per script for careful work. A reader clearing three
scripts a day is not reading them carefully, and everyone in the chain
knows it. Turnaround is typically **24–48 hours**, and overnight or same-day
for a weekend go-out (§2.1).

### 5.3 What a coverage document contains

```
TITLE: ...........................  FORM: Screenplay / Novel / Pitch
AUTHOR: .........................  PAGES: 112     DRAFT DATE: 3/14
SUBMITTED BY: ...................  GENRE: Thriller
SUBMITTED TO: ...................  LOCALE / PERIOD: Chicago, present
ELEMENTS ATTACHED: ..............  ANALYST: ......  DATE: ......

LOGLINE      one or two sentences
SYNOPSIS     1–3 pages, plot including the ending
COMMENTS     ½–2 pages of assessment

                    EXCELLENT   GOOD   FAIR   POOR
   PREMISE              ·         X      ·      ·
   STORY / STRUCTURE    ·         ·      X      ·
   CHARACTERS           ·         X      ·      ·
   DIALOGUE             ·         ·      X      ·
   PRODUCTION VALUE     X         ·      ·      ·

   SCRIPT:  PASS   CONSIDER   RECOMMEND
   WRITER:  PASS   CONSIDER   RECOMMEND
```

Two verdicts, not one, and the distinction matters enormously for careers: a
**PASS on the script, CONSIDER on the writer** is how writers get onto
assignment lists (§4.1) off material nobody will ever buy. Most coverage is a
PASS. CONSIDER means "an executive should read this." RECOMMEND means "I am
putting my name on the assertion that we should buy this," and a reader who
issues them freely stops being trusted; a reader who never issues one is
useless. The working rate is roughly one RECOMMEND in a hundred or worse.

### 5.4 Good coverage versus bad coverage

**A good coverage** does five things:

1. **Gets the logline right** — so the executive can pitch it in a hallway.
2. **Synopsises accurately and neutrally**, including the ending, without
   editorialising inside the plot summary. An executive who discovers in a
   meeting that the synopsis was wrong will not use that reader again.
3. **Assesses against the script's own intent**, not the reader's taste. A
   broad comedy is not failed for lacking psychological realism; the question
   is whether it is funny and whether it works as what it is trying to be.
4. **Diagnoses rather than complains** — names the specific structural problem
   ("the protagonist has no goal until page 47") instead of "it's slow."
5. **Answers the business questions the executive will be asked**: what does
   this cost, what's the rating, who's the audience, what are the comps, is
   there a role a star wants, is the third act achievable.

**A bad coverage** is recognisable at a glance: it regurgitates plot in the
comments, it is witty at the writer's expense (readers know this reads as
insecurity), it applies personal taste as a standard, it misses the genre's
rules, it hedges so thoroughly that no decision can be made from it, and it
fails to separate the writer from the script.

The stakes are asymmetric and this shapes behaviour: a reader who passes on
something that becomes a hit suffers almost nothing, because everyone passed;
a reader who RECOMMENDs something the executive reads and hates has spent
real credibility. The rational strategy is to pass, and the systemic
consequence is a machine biased against unusual material.

### 5.5 How coverage travels

Coverage is stored. Studios maintain searchable databases going back decades,
and when a script is resubmitted — new draft, new producer, new regime — the
first thing that surfaces is the old coverage. Practical consequences:

- A script that was covered PASS at a studio is often dead there for years,
  regardless of who is now championing it. Agents will retitle a script for
  precisely this reason.
- Executives share coverage and verdicts informally across companies. "It's
  been out, it got soft passes everywhere" is a real and damaging status.
- The database is also an asset: it is how a studio knows, in an afternoon,
  every project it has ever considered about the Apollo programme, and who
  controlled the rights.

## 6. Notes and the executive relationship

The creative executive's product is **notes**. Development notes come from:

- the creative exec and their superiors (studio notes);
- the producer(s);
- the director, once attached;
- the star, once attached (often via their own development person);
- **research** — see `09-marketing-and-distribution.md` for test screenings
  during post.

Notes conflict constantly, and reconciling them is the writer's real job.
Terms of art: *the note behind the note* (the actual problem, versus the
prescribed fix); *page one rewrite* (start again); *lateral* (a change that
neither improves nor worsens).

### 6.1 The notes hierarchy

Not all notes carry the same weight, and a writer's first task on receiving a
set of them is to work out which is which.

| Source | Weight | Character of the notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Studio chairman / president of production** | Absolute | Few, blunt, usually about the movie's premise or its audience |
| **The executive running the project** | High, but derivative | Aggregated and translated from above; the writer's day-to-day counterpart |
| **The producer** | High | Usually the most story-literate notes in the process; the producer has read every draft |
| **The director (once attached)** | Becomes dominant | Shifts from "is this good" to "can I shoot this, and do I want to" |
| **The star (once attached)** | High and narrow | Concerns their character, their scenes, their moments; delivered via a development person or an agent |
| **Financier / co-financier** | Variable | Territory-driven: "does this play internationally," "is this rated R" |
| **The star's or director's own writer** | Informal but real | Notes that arrive as pages |
| **Research / testing** | Late and blunt | Numbers, not opinions (`07` §8) |

A functioning process establishes explicitly whose note governs when two
conflict. A dysfunctional one leaves it ambiguous, and the writer discovers
the answer only after writing the wrong draft.

### 6.2 How a notes session actually runs

The unit is the **notes call** — historically an in-person meeting, now
usually a conference or video call — 60 to 120 minutes, scheduled **1–4 weeks**
after delivery.

Before it happens, there is an invisible stage: **notes on the notes.** The
creative executive writes their notes, sends them up to their boss, and gets
told which of them are actually studio positions. The producer does the same
internally. What reaches the writer is a negotiated document, and its
contradictions are frequently the residue of that negotiation.

The call itself follows a near-invariant order:

1. **The praise.** Three to five minutes on what is working — the voice, the
   world, a particular scene. This is sincere and it is also protocol; its
   absence is itself a signal, and experienced writers listen to *how
   specific* the praise is. Generic praise ("we love the world") means nobody
   liked anything in particular.
2. **The top-line.** The most senior person in the room states the one big
   thing: "our concern is that we don't know what he wants until the end of
   act two." Everything else on the call is subordinate to this, and if the
   writer takes away only this, the call has succeeded.
3. **The producer frames.** The producer restates the top-line in the terms of
   the story, often softening it, often supplying the diagnosis the studio
   note lacked.
4. **The act-by-act walk.** Working through the script in page order, notes
   are given by scene or sequence. This is the bulk of the call and it is
   where the density of trivia is highest.
5. **Line notes and small items** — usually deferred to the written document.
6. **Schedule.** How long the writer needs; when the next draft is due; who
   reads it first.
7. **The written notes document** follows within days: 2–15 pages, sometimes
   with a page-referenced list at the end.

**Norms of the room.** The writer mostly listens and takes notes, asks
clarifying questions, and does not argue point by point in the moment —
arguing in real time reads as defensiveness and wastes the call. The one
exception is the top-line: if the writer thinks the big note is wrong, that
argument has to happen now, because it cannot be had after the draft. Pushback
is delivered afterwards, in writing, as a proposal.

**The response memo.** The strongest professional habit in development: within
a few days of the call, the writer sends back a short document — one to four
pages — saying *this is what I heard, this is what I am going to do, and here
is what I am respectfully not doing and why.* It converts a diffuse set of
opinions into an agreed plan, it exposes contradictions before they are
written into a draft, and it is the single most reliable way to avoid
delivering a draft that solves the wrong problem. Studios sometimes ask for a
revised outline to serve the same function.

### 6.3 What makes a note good

1. **It identifies a symptom accurately.** "I got bored around page 60" is a
   more useful note than most prescriptions, because the boredom is real data
   even when the diagnosis is wrong.
2. **It names the problem, not the fix.** "We don't believe she'd stay" beats
   "give her a sick mother."
3. **It states intent.** What is this scene supposed to do? A note that
   articulates the intended function lets the writer solve it a better way.
4. **It is prioritised.** Three notes that matter, flagged as the ones that
   matter, beat forty notes of equal apparent weight. Good executives say
   explicitly: this is a must, this is a preference, ignore this if it's
   inconvenient.
5. **It is consistent with the last set.** Notes that reverse the previous
   round without acknowledging it destroy the writer's trust and their
   willingness to commit to anything.
6. **It respects what the film is.** A note that would turn a hard R thriller
   into a PG-13 four-quadrant film is not a note, it is a different film, and
   it should be said as such.
7. **It arrives when it can be used.** A structural note on the fourth draft
   that was available on the outline is a failure of the note-giver, not the
   writer.

**Bad notes**, by recognisable type:

| Type | Example | Why it's bad |
|---|---|---|
| **The prescription** | "Make him a veteran" | Solution without diagnosis; often solves nothing |
| **The taste note** | "I just don't like her" | Unactionable and unarguable |
| **The reversal** | Undoes the change demanded last round | Costs a full draft and all trust |
| **The mandate note** | "Corporate wants a franchise" | A different film, delivered as a fix |
| **The protecting note** | Given so the exec is on record if it fails | Optimises for the exec's career, not the film |
| **The literalism** | "Nobody would really do that" applied to a genre film | Wrong standard for the material |
| **The wish list** | Forty notes, unranked | The writer must guess what matters |
| **The absent note** | "We love it!" with no direction, then a pass | Wastes months; the commonest of all |

### 6.4 The note behind the note

The single most useful concept in development. Executives are trained
audiences, not writers: they are excellent at detecting that something is
wrong and unreliable at knowing why. The note they give is therefore usually a
*proposed remedy for a correctly-felt symptom*, and the writer's job is to
recover the symptom.

Worked examples:

| The note given | The note behind it | What the writer actually does |
|---|---|---|
| "The best friend should be funnier" | Act two has no energy; the audience is restless | Tighten the act, raise the pressure — the friend may not change at all |
| "We need to like her more" | Her behaviour is unmotivated, so it reads as unpleasant rather than driven | Show the want earlier; motivate the choice, don't soften it |
| "Can we add a scene explaining the plan?" | The reader was confused in the heist, twenty pages later | Fix the clarity of the *earlier* setup; a new scene of exposition would slow it further |
| "The ending feels abrupt" | The theme was never dramatised, so the climax resolves plot but not meaning | Plant the thematic conflict in act one and pay it in the climax |
| "Can he have a dog?" | The protagonist is isolated and the audience has nothing to attach to | Give him a relationship with stakes — possibly a dog, possibly not |
| "Does it have to be so dark?" | The tone wobbles, so the darkness feels unearned rather than intended | Commit harder and set the contract in the first ten pages |

The corollary: a writer who executes notes literally produces a draft that
addresses every note and is worse than the last one, and the process then
concludes that the writer is the problem.

### 6.5 Handling contradictory notes

Contradiction is not a malfunction; with five note-givers it is arithmetic.
The working method:

1. **Aggregate and cluster.** Put every note from every source in one document,
   grouped by *where in the script* it lands rather than by who said it.
2. **Count.** If four of six readers stumbled in the same ten pages, those
   pages are broken — regardless of the six different fixes proposed. Note
   frequency is far more reliable evidence than note content.
3. **Separate diagnosis from prescription.** Keep the symptoms, discard the
   remedies, and re-solve.
4. **Rank by authority.** Establish, explicitly and in writing if necessary,
   whose note governs. "I can do X or Y but not both — which do you want?" is
   a legitimate and career-preserving question, and asking it before writing
   rather than after is the mark of a professional.
5. **Trade openly.** Give the note-giver something. Executives need to see
   their note in the draft; a writer who solves the underlying problem
   differently should say so explicitly in the response memo, or the note will
   simply come back next round with more force.
6. **Protect the spine.** Some things cannot be traded — the ending, the
   protagonist's want, the tone contract — because losing them makes every
   other note unanswerable. Knowing which two or three elements are
   load-bearing, and defending only those, is the whole skill.

### 6.6 How the notes process fails

| Failure | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **Too many note-givers** | Averaging; the draft loses its point of view and satisfies nobody |
| **No named decision-maker** | Every round reopens settled questions |
| **Notes given late in the process that belonged at outline** | A page-one rewrite that costs six months |
| **Regime change mid-development** | New executive with no ownership of the project reopens everything (§9.4) |
| **The writer capitulating to everything** | A technically compliant, dead draft; the writer is replaced |
| **The writer refusing everything** | The writer is replaced |
| **Notes delivered only as praise** | The project drifts for a year and is quietly dropped |
| **Director and studio giving opposite notes without resolution** | The writer writes two films; both are rejected |
| **Star notes arriving after the draft is locked** | Prep rewrites (§4.5) at production prices |

## 7. WGA credit arbitration

Screen credit is **not** decided by the studio. When there is any dispute — or
automatically whenever a production-company-affiliated writer claims credit —
the WGA runs a blind arbitration: three anonymous arbiters read every draft and
apply the Screen Credits Manual.

### 7.1 What triggers it

At the end of production the company must send a **Notice of Tentative
Writing Credits** to the Guild and to every **participating writer** — anyone
who was employed on the project or whose literary material was purchased.
The notice states the credits the company proposes. From there:

- If no participating writer protests within the stated window (a matter of
  days — the notice specifies it, and it is short), the tentative credits
  become final.
- If any participating writer protests, the Guild convenes an arbitration.
- Arbitration is **automatic**, with no protest needed, when a **production
  executive** — a writer who is also a producer or director on the film —
  claims writing credit. The bar such a writer must clear is deliberately
  higher, because the conflict of interest is structural.

### 7.2 What gets submitted

The Guild assembles a package for the arbiters containing:

1. **Every draft, revision, treatment, outline and set of production pages**,
   in date order, with the writers' names removed — the arbitration is blind.
2. **The source material**, if any (the novel, article, prior screenplay).
3. The company's **statement of the tentative credits** and the writing
   chronology: who was employed when, for what step.
4. **Each writer's written statement** — the writer's own argument for their
   contribution. These run from two to forty pages; the effective ones cite
   specific scenes, characters and structural elements traceable to their own
   drafts, with page references, rather than arguing about fairness. Writers
   are explicitly forbidden from identifying themselves or the other writers,
   and from lobbying arbiters.

Assembling the drafts is the production company's obligation and it is why
studio legal departments keep every dated draft. A writer whose drafts are
missing from the record has, in practice, no case (§10).

### 7.3 How the arbiters work

- Three arbiters are drawn from the Guild's **Arbiters List** — experienced
  WGA members who have volunteered and been vetted. They are anonymous to the
  writers, to the company, and to each other.
- They read the **entire package** independently. This is a serious unpaid
  workload: a contested tentpole can run to twenty drafts and thousands of
  pages, and a conscientious arbiter spends **20–60 hours** on it.
- They do not confer with each other. Each communicates only with a Guild
  **arbitration consultant** — a member available to explain the rules — and
  submits an independent determination.
- The **majority** determines the credit. Unanimity is not required in the
  ordinary case.
- A **Policy Review Board** exists, but it hears procedural challenges only:
  it can find that the process was mishandled and order a new arbitration. It
  cannot re-judge who wrote what. There is no appeal on the merits.

### 7.4 The standards actually applied

Rules that matter:

- Credit reads **"Written by"** (story + screenplay by the same hand),
  **"Screenplay by"**, **"Story by"**, and **"Screen Story by"**.
- **"&"** means a *writing team*; **"and"** means *separate, sequential*
  writers. This distinction is meaningful and load-bearing.
- The arbiters assess contribution to the final shooting script across
  **dramatic construction, original and different scenes, characterisation,
  and dialogue**, weighting structure and character far above line-level
  work. A brilliant dialogue polish is, by design, hard to convert into
  credit.
- A **subsequent writer** must contribute **at least 50%** of the final
  screenplay to share screenplay credit on an **original** screenplay, and
  **at least 33%** on a **non-original** (adapted) one. The first writer of an
  original is therefore strongly protected — which is exactly why so many
  rewriters end up uncredited, and why studios hire rewriters knowing they
  probably won't be credited.
- **Story credit** can be separated from screenplay credit, and often is: an
  originating writer keeps "Story by" while later writers take "Screenplay
  by."
- Screenplay credit is limited to **two writers**, except that in unusual
  cases and only as the result of arbitration it may be shared by three
  writers or by two writing teams.
- Credit determines **residuals**, **separated rights** (for original
  material: publication and stage rights, sequel and remake payments, and a
  right of first negotiation on sequels), and awards eligibility. It is worth
  real money — often more over time than the original fee.

### 7.5 Timeline and what it blocks

| When | What |
|---|---|
| End of principal photography → post | Company prepares final shooting script and the writing chronology |
| Typically during post, before main titles are finalised | Notice of Tentative Writing Credits issued |
| +days | Protest window closes |
| +1–2 weeks | Guild assembles the package; writers' statements due |
| +2–4 weeks | Arbiters read and determine |
| — | Determination issued; Policy Review Board only on process |

The whole process typically runs **4–8 weeks** and occasionally longer. It
sits directly on the critical path for **main and end titles**, the **poster
and one-sheet billing block**, press materials, and the answer print — which
is why marketing departments chase it and why credits are among the last
elements finalised before delivery (`07` §7, `09` §5). Awards submissions
also depend on it.

### 7.6 Why it produces the results it does

Three structural consequences worth internalising:

1. **Credits under-describe the labour.** A film with two credited writers may
   have had eleven. This is a feature of a system designed to protect
   originators, not a scandal.
2. **Money follows credit, not contribution.** Two writers can do equal work
   and one receives residuals and separated rights for decades while the other
   receives nothing beyond their fee.
3. **Everyone knows the odds and behaves accordingly.** Rewriters price the
   likely absence of credit into their weekly rate, and originating writers
   fight hard to stay on their own projects — the guaranteed rewrite step
   negotiated at the spec sale (§2.1) is, among other things, a credit
   protection device.

## 8. Greenlight

The greenlight is the moment the studio commits the negative cost. It requires,
approximately:

1. **A script the studio believes in** — or at least believes it can shoot.
2. **A director** (see `03-financing-and-dealmaking.md` §2 on attachments).
3. **Cast** sufficient to justify the budget, sometimes "pay-or-play" already.
4. **A budget and schedule** validated by physical production, with a
   **contingency** (typically **10%**) and often incentive money already
   modelled.
5. **A release date and a marketing view** — can we sell this, to whom, when.
6. **A financial model** — projections across theatrical, international, home
   entertainment, licensing, with a downside case.

Committees vary; the decision usually sits with the studio chairman with the
head of production, marketing, distribution, and finance in the room.

### 8.1 Who is in the room

| Role | What they are answering | Their characteristic objection |
|---|---|---|
| **Chairman / studio head** | Do we make this | "This isn't what we are" |
| **President of Production** | Is the film ready and is the filmmaker the right one | "I want one more draft" |
| **EVP Physical Production** | Can it be shot for this number, on this schedule | "That budget is $12M light and the schedule is a fantasy" |
| **President of Marketing** | Can I sell it, to whom, for how much | "I can't tell you what this movie is in one line" |
| **President of Distribution** | Is there a date, and what does it open to | "There's no weekend for it before next October" |
| **International / global distribution** | Does it travel | "Comedy doesn't play; take $70M off the foreign number" |
| **CFO / SVP Finance & Strategy** | Does the model clear the hurdle | "It's break-even in the base case" |
| **General Counsel / Business Affairs** | Is it clean and are the deals closable | "The chain of title has a hole and the star's stop-date is immovable" |
| **Head of post / VFX** | Is the post plan real | "1,400 shots cannot be delivered by that date" |
| *(above a threshold)* **Corporate parent** | Does it fit the balance sheet | "Not at that number without a partner" |

Producers and filmmakers are usually **not** in the greenlight meeting. They
are presented, by their executive, in their absence.

### 8.2 What is physically in the room

The greenlight package. Every studio has its own name for it and its own
template, but the contents are near-universal:

1. **The current draft**, dated, with a one-page synopsis and a logline.
2. **The budget** — a detailed budget prepared by a line producer during a
   paid prep-lite, plus a **top sheet** (`04-preproduction.md` §3): above the
   line, below the line, post, other; contingency; incentive estimate; a net
   "cash cost" after rebates.
3. **The schedule and stripboard** — shoot days by unit, locations, the
   start date, the delivery date.
4. **The cast list** with deal status, quotes, and pay-or-play exposure, plus
   the director's deal.
5. **The financial model** (§8.3), with base, upside and downside cases.
6. **Comps** — five to fifteen films positioned as comparable, with their
   budgets, domestic and international grosses, and their multiples.
7. **The marketing point of view** — positioning, primary and secondary
   audience, an estimated P&A spend, sometimes a mock one-sheet or a
   concept-tested logline (`09` §4).
8. **The release-date map** — the competitive calendar for the proposed
   weekend and the four around it.
9. **The rights and legal memo** — chain of title status, guild status,
   underlying obligations, approvals granted to talent.
10. **The VFX and post plan**, with a shot count and vendor strategy
    (`08-vfx-and-specialty.md` §2).
11. **A risk memo** — what could go wrong and what it would cost.

### 8.3 How the financial model is actually built

Finance builds it, but the inputs are owned by the departments that will be
held to them, which is what makes the meeting political.

```
     COMPS ──► DOMESTIC BOX OFFICE ──► RENTALS (studio share ~50-55% dom.)
       │              │
       │              └─► INTERNATIONAL BO ──► rentals (~40-45%, varies)
       │
       ├─► ANCILLARY: digital/EST/VOD, subscription licence, TV, airlines
       ├─► P&A SPEND (set by marketing; often 50-100%+ of negative cost)
       ├─► NEGATIVE COST, net of incentives  (`03` §6)
       ├─► PARTICIPATIONS & RESIDUALS        (`11` §3, §5)
       └─► OVERHEAD / INTEREST charges
                     │
                     ▼
        ULTIMATES  →  studio contribution / net profit
                      base case · upside · downside
```

Mechanics that matter:

- **The model is driven by an opening weekend and a multiple.** Distribution
  supplies a plausible opening from the comps and the date; the multiple
  (`10-theatrical-release-and-box-office.md` §4) turns it into a domestic
  total. Everything else scales off those two numbers, which is why arguing
  about the comps *is* arguing about the greenlight.
- **International is a ratio, not a forecast**, at this stage: a genre-and-
  cast-specific multiple of domestic, adjusted per territory by international
  distribution.
- **Ultimates** run the film's revenue out over roughly ten to twenty years
  across all windows and are the number the studio actually manages to
  (`11-money-accounting-and-participations.md` §6).
- **Theatrical break-even rules of thumb**: a studio film needs global box
  office of roughly **2–2.5× negative cost** to break even theatrically once
  P&A and the exhibitor's share are accounted for, with ancillaries and
  licensing carrying the rest. Films with heavy first-dollar participations
  need more.
- **The downside case is the one that matters.** Nobody is fired for a base
  case; they are fired for a film that lost $150M. Serious models ask: if this
  opens at half the base case, what is the loss, and can the slate absorb it.
- **Incentives are modelled as cash**, not as a discount, and can move a
  decision on their own: a 25–40% rebate on qualifying spend (`03` §6) is
  frequently the difference between a yes and a no, and it dictates where the
  film shoots before a location manager has been hired.

### 8.4 The meeting itself

Green-lighting is rarely a single dramatic vote. The usual sequence:

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Circulation** | The package goes out 3–7 days ahead; everyone is expected to have read the script |
| **The pre-meetings** | The production executive lobbies each department individually. Most of the decision is made here |
| **Presentation** | The production executive presents the film in 10–20 minutes: what it is, who's in it, what it costs, what it makes |
| **The interrogation** | Each department states its position. Marketing and distribution speak last and loudest |
| **The number** | The discussion converges on a budget the room will approve, which is usually below the one presented |
| **The condition** | Approval is almost always conditional: "yes at $85M with that cast", "yes if we get a date in March", "yes subject to one more draft" |
| **The memo** | A greenlight memo records the approved budget, contingency, start date, delivery date, release date and conditions |

**Who says no, and why.** In practice, the veto is exercised most often by
marketing and by physical production, for opposite reasons: marketing kills
films that cannot be described, and physical production kills films whose
budget is a fiction. The chairman's no is usually about slate fit — this film
is fine, but it is the third of its kind this year, or it is not what this
studio is for.

### 8.5 Conditional and soft greenlights

**Soft greenlight / prep-to-greenlight**: the studio funds prep (hiring
department heads, scouting, building) *before* full commitment. This is common
and expensive — abandoning at this stage still writes off millions.

Escalating levels of commitment, each with a real cost of abandonment:

| Level | What is committed | Cost of pulling out |
|---|---|---|
| **Development** | Writer fees, option payments | $0.5M–$5M written off |
| **Prep money / soft greenlight** | Line producer, 1st AD, PD, scouting, early art | $1M–$10M |
| **Design and build authorised** | Construction, VFX pre-production, costume manufacture | $5M–$25M+ |
| **Pay-or-play executed** | Director and cast fees owed regardless | Their full fees |
| **Full greenlight** | The negative cost | The film |

**Pay-or-play** is the hard commitment: the studio owes the talent their fee
whether or not the film is made. Once several pay-or-play deals are signed, the
economics of *not* making the film get worse than making it, which is how bad
films get made.

The related pathology is the **date-driven greenlight**: a release date is
announced (often to claim a weekend against a competitor) before the script is
ready, and every subsequent decision is made under a clock that cannot move
without a public retreat. A large share of famously troubled productions
begin here rather than on set.

## 9. Turnaround and development hell

- **Turnaround** — the studio abandons a project and allows another buyer to
  take it, on condition of reimbursing costs (development spend plus interest,
  plus sometimes a premium) usually within a set window. Many well-known films
  were made in turnaround from another studio.
- **Development hell** — a project that cycles endlessly through writers and
  regimes without dying or being made. Caused by: the underlying rights being
  valuable enough to hold but not to make; regime change (a new head of
  production has no ownership of the previous one's slate); or an unsolvable
  script problem.
- **Shelving/abandonment for tax or strategic reasons** — a completed or
  near-complete film written off rather than released, an option that only
  makes sense under specific accounting conditions.

### 9.1 How a turnaround deal actually works

The turnaround right lives in the producer's agreement, negotiated at
acquisition, years before anyone needs it. Once the studio decides to abandon:

1. **The studio issues a turnaround notice** — or, more often in practice, the
   producer asks for the project to be "put into turnaround" and the studio
   agrees, because nobody wants the write-off on the books.
2. **The studio states the turnaround figure.** This is the sum a new buyer
   must pay to take the project: **actual direct out-of-pocket development
   costs** — option payments, writer fees, legal, research, sometimes
   allocated overhead — **plus interest**, commonly at a stated rate (prime
   plus one or two points is a familiar formulation) accruing from the date
   each cost was incurred. Typical figures range from a few hundred thousand
   dollars for a lightly developed project to **$5M–$15M** for a tentpole that
   has been through five writers and a design phase.
3. **The producer gets a window** — commonly **12–24 months**, sometimes as
   little as six — to set the project elsewhere. During it the producer can
   shop freely.
4. **The new buyer pays**, usually **at commencement of principal
   photography** rather than on transfer, which is what makes turnaround
   viable at all: the new studio takes the project now and pays when it makes
   the film.
5. **The old studio keeps something.** Almost always a **passive
   participation** — a small percentage of net proceeds, occasionally a
   fraction of a gross definition — and sometimes a co-financing option or a
   distribution right in some territories. Turnaround is not charity; it is
   converting a dead asset into a free option.
6. **The changed-elements clause.** If the package materially changes — a new
   director, a new star, a budget above a threshold — the project must
   normally be **re-offered to the original studio** on a right of first
   refusal or last refusal. This is the clause that produces the recurring
   scenario in which a studio passes, the producer attaches a major star, and
   the original studio takes it back.
7. **If the window expires** without a taker, rights revert to the studio,
   which can sit on them; if the *underlying* option also lapses, the material
   itself reverts to the author (§3) and the studio has nothing.

Turnaround is a routine, unembarrassing part of the business, and the history
of well-known films made this way is long — *E.T.* left Columbia for
Universal; *Pulp Fiction* left TriStar for Miramax. The reason it works is
that the abandoning studio's judgement was usually about *fit and slate*
rather than quality: the film they couldn't sell is a film another distributor
is built to sell.

### 9.2 Why studios abandon projects

| Reason | Frequency | Signature |
|---|---|---|
| **Script never got there** | The most common | Four writers, no draft anyone will shoot |
| **Regime change** | Very common | New president of production, new mandate, previous slate quietly de-prioritised |
| **Budget crept past the number** | Common | The film is good at $40M and unmakeable at $95M |
| **Comparable film failed** | Common | A same-genre release underperforms and the whole category is repriced overnight |
| **Package never closed** | Common | The director passed, the star's window moved, and the second choices weren't enough |
| **Strategic shift** | Increasing | The company decides it makes tentpoles and premium streaming, not $30M dramas |
| **Rights or legal problem** | Occasional | §3.3 |
| **Talent problem** | Occasional | Scandal, illness, insurability (`03` §8) |

### 9.3 The cost of holding versus killing

A project in development costs almost nothing to keep: an option extension is
tens of thousands of dollars, and doing nothing costs nothing at all. Killing
it crystallises a write-off and admits a mistake. This asymmetry — cheap to
hold, embarrassing to kill — is the entire mechanism of **development hell**,
and it is why hundreds of projects sit indefinitely at every studio in a state
that is neither alive nor dead.

### 9.4 The anatomy of development hell

The recognisable pattern, in order:

1. A property with genuine value is acquired, usually at auction, usually
   expensively.
2. The first writer's draft is decent but does not solve the hard problem the
   material contains.
3. The champion executive is promoted, leaves, or is fired. Studio executive
   tenure averages only a few years, and **a new head of production has no
   career interest in the previous one's projects** — its success validates
   their predecessor, its failure is theirs.
4. The project is inherited by someone who did not buy it and asks for a
   fresh take. New writer, new direction, 9–18 months.
5. Option extensions are paid to keep the rights alive; the sunk cost grows,
   which makes the turnaround figure larger and abandonment less attractive.
6. Occasionally a director attaches, the project surges for a year, the
   director departs for something with a date, and it settles back.
7. It is made, eventually, when three things coincide: an executive with
   ownership of it, a filmmaker with heat, and a market moment where the genre
   is wanted. That coincidence can take a decade.

## 10. The development department's outputs

Concretely, what exists on paper at the end of development:

- **The screenplay**, in a locked-page revision colour sequence
  (white → blue → pink → yellow → green → goldenrod → …).
- A **series of drafts** with dates, which is the arbitration record.
- **The rights file** — chain of title, options, clearances.
- A **budget top sheet** and **schedule** (see `04-preproduction.md`).
- The **package** — director and principal cast attachments.
- **Comparables** ("comps") — the films this one is being modelled against
  financially, which is how marketing and finance argue about it.

Two of these deserve emphasis because they are easy to under-value:

**The dated draft archive** is a legal instrument, not an archive. It is the
sole evidence in credit arbitration (§7), the record of what was approved at
each step for deal purposes, and the reference against which a production
rewrite's changes are measured. Studios keep every delivered draft
permanently, with its delivery date and the writer it came from.

**The comps set** is the most quietly consequential document in the package.
Choosing the comps determines the projected opening, the international ratio,
the P&A budget, and therefore whether the film gets made and at what number.
The argument about whether a film is comparable to a $700M global performer or
a $180M one is, in effect, the greenlight argument conducted in the third
person (§8.3).

## 12. What makes a screenplay good

The screenplay is the single largest quality input in filmmaking and the
cheapest to change. Everything downstream — casting, design, shooting,
editing, marketing — either serves it or compensates for it, and compensation
is expensive and rarely complete. It is worth setting out what practitioners
are actually assessing, because "good script" is otherwise a black box.

Two framing facts. First, a screenplay is not literature; it is a **set of
instructions and a persuasive document at the same time**. It must specify a
film precisely enough for two hundred professionals to build it, and it must
be exciting enough that financiers, directors and actors say yes after one
read. Second, **one page is roughly one minute of screen time**, so form is
constrained: a feature runs **95–120 pages** in studio practice, longer for
epics, shorter for comedy and horror.

### 12.1 Premise

Everything begins here, and premise problems are the ones that cannot be
fixed later.

- **Legibility.** The film can be described in one sentence that contains a
  protagonist, a situation, and a conflict — and the sentence makes someone
  want to see it. This is not a marketing afterthought: a film that needs a
  paragraph is structurally hard to sell (`09` §12.1) and that difficulty is
  knowable at development stage.
- **Inherent conflict.** The premise generates story on its own. "A man
  grieves" does not; "a man has one day to stop his own funeral" does.
- **A promise of scenes.** A good premise makes the reader imagine three
  scenes before they've read any. Executives literally test this: if they
  can't picture the trailer, the premise is thin.
- **Freshness against familiarity.** The commercial sweet spot is a
  recognisable form with one genuine twist — familiar enough to sell,
  different enough to be worth making.

### 12.2 Structure

Structure is not a template; it is the management of **causality, escalation
and information**. The commonly taught three-act shape (setup to roughly page
25–30, confrontation, resolution from roughly page 85–90, with a midpoint
reversal and a low point before the climax) is a description of what usually
works, not a rule — but a script that departs from it must supply its own
engine, and most that depart do not.

What is actually being assessed:

1. **Causality.** Scenes connect with *therefore* and *but*, not *and then*.
   An episodic script — a series of things that happen to the protagonist —
   is the most common structural failure and reads as "flat" or "slow" long
   before anyone can name why.
2. **Escalation.** Each sequence costs the protagonist more than the last.
   When a script "loses energy in act two," the usual cause is that the
   obstacles stopped growing.
3. **A clock or a container.** Pressure comes from a deadline, a confined
   space, a countdown, a pursuit, or a rule that cannot be broken. Scripts
   without one drift.
4. **Information management.** The audience should always know *what they are
   waiting for*. Suspense is created by giving information away, not by
   withholding it; confusion is not tension, and readers who report being
   "confused" are usually reporting an information failure two acts earlier.
5. **The turn on the ending.** The climax must be caused by the protagonist's
   choice, and that choice should be the one the whole film has been setting
   up. An ending resolved by a coincidence, a rescue, or a character who has
   not changed reads as unearned no matter how well staged.
6. **Runtime discipline.** Structure is also arithmetic: a 140-page script for
   a $30M drama is a budget problem as well as a pacing problem.

### 12.3 Character

1. **Want and need.** A conscious, active, *specific* goal that drives
   behaviour, and an underlying lack the story is really about. When they
   conflict, you have a film; when there is only a want, you have a plot; when
   there is only a need, you have a mood piece.
2. **Agency.** The protagonist makes decisions with costs. Passive
   protagonists — characters things happen to — are the second most common
   fatal note in development, and they are the reason a competent script can
   still feel inert.
3. **Contradiction.** Interesting characters want two incompatible things.
   Consistency without contradiction reads as a function, not a person.
4. **Differentiation.** Cover the character names on the dialogue and the
   speakers should still be identifiable. If they are not, the script has one
   voice — usually the writer's.
5. **The antagonist has a case.** The opposing force should be right about
   something, and stronger than the protagonist for most of the running time.
6. **Wanting in every scene.** Every character in a scene wants something from
   the other, including the ones who are only there to deliver information.
7. **Castability.** A well-written role gives an actor something to *play*: a
   change, a secret, a decision under pressure. This is a craft property, not
   a marketing one, and it is why some scripts attract stars and others with
   the same premise do not (`04` §8).

### 12.4 Scene craft and economy

The scene is the unit of screenwriting and the level at which the difference
between competent and excellent is most visible.

- **Every scene turns.** Something changes between the first line and the
  last: a relationship, an intention, the audience's understanding. A scene
  that ends in the same state it began is cuttable, and it will be cut — in
  the edit, at a cost (`07` §2).
- **Enter late, leave early.** Start after the greeting, end before the
  goodbye. The most reliable single improvement to an amateur script is to
  cut the first and last quarter-page of every scene.
- **Conflict carries information.** Exposition delivered by two people who
  agree is dead; the same information delivered by two people arguing is
  invisible.
- **One scene, three jobs.** Economy is the mark of a professional: a single
  scene advances the plot, reveals character, and plants something that pays
  later. Amateur scripts use three scenes to do it.
- **Scene count and rhythm.** A feature typically has **60–120 scenes**;
  varying their length deliberately is what "pace" actually consists of.
- **Visual thinking.** Information conveyed by behaviour and image rather than
  statement. The test is whether a scene would still play with the sound off.

### 12.5 Dialogue

1. **Subtext.** Characters rarely say what they want; the scene is the gap
   between what is said and what is meant. "On the nose" dialogue — people
   announcing their feelings and intentions — is the most common surface
   failure and often signals a structural problem underneath.
2. **Compression.** Real speech is redundant; screen dialogue is speech with
   the redundancy removed but the rhythm kept. Fewer words, sharper.
3. **Voice.** Vocabulary, syntax and rhythm differ by character. This is
   partly research and partly ear, and it is not teachable past a point.
4. **Lines actors want to say.** A small number of speeches in any good script
   are the reason an actor takes the part.
5. **No speeches unless earned.** A monologue must be a character *doing*
   something to someone, not the writer explaining the theme.
6. **The theme is never stated.** A character who articulates the film's
   meaning aloud usually indicates the writer does not trust the story to
   carry it.

### 12.6 Tone

The most under-discussed and most frequently fatal property.

- **Tone is a contract with the audience**, and it is set in the first five to
  ten pages: how funny is this, how real, how violent, are we safe. Breaking
  it later without preparing the break destroys trust.
- **Blended tones are the hardest thing in the craft.** Comedy-drama, horror-
  comedy and action-satire scripts fail more often on tone than on structure,
  and the failure is invisible to a reader who is only checking plot.
- **Genre rules are load-bearing.** Each genre has a promise — a horror film
  must scare, a thriller must have a ticking problem, a romance must make the
  audience *want* the couple together — and satisfying it is not formula, it
  is the reason the audience came.
- **Tone is a director's problem too**, which is why "tone" is the word that
  appears most often when a good script becomes a bad film.

### 12.7 Executability

The property that distinguishes a good screenplay from a good *screenplay to
shoot*, and the one non-practitioners consistently miss.

| Question | Why it decides things |
|---|---|
| **What does it cost?** Night exteriors, water, crowds, children, animals, period, snow, and VFX shot counts are all written on the page | A script is a budget document; a writer who can't price their own pages loses control of the film (`04` §3) |
| **Can the action be staged?** "They fight through the market" vs beats a stunt coordinator can build | Vague action gets invented on set, expensively, by someone else |
| **How many locations and how many company moves?** | The schedule's biggest waste (`06` §5) |
| **How many speaking parts?** | Every one is a casting session, a deal, and a day |
| **Is the third act shootable at all?** | Third acts written as spectacle without geography become the reshoot |
| **What's the rating?** | A single scene can move a film from PG-13 to R and halve its audience (`09` §7) |
| **Does it travel?** | Dialogue-driven comedy and culturally specific idiom cost the international number (`10` §7) |
| **Can it be cut down?** | Scripts with no removable scenes are dangerous; scripts that are all removable scenes are worse |

### 12.8 Readability

A screenplay is read before it is filmed, by people who read constantly and
under time pressure. Craft on this axis is real and measurable:

- **White space.** Action described in blocks of one to four lines. Dense
  paragraphs get skimmed, and a skimmed script is a passed script.
- **Momentum.** Professional readers form a judgement in the first ten pages
  and confirm it by thirty. This is unjust and universal; scripts are written
  accordingly.
- **Character introductions that land.** Two lines that make a person vivid.
- **Precision without direction.** Describing what is seen and heard, not
  camera moves and lens choices — directing on the page antagonises the
  people whose job that is.
- **The script is fun to read.** The professional consensus is that a script
  which is a pleasure to read gets made; one that is merely correct does not.

### 12.9 Emotion, which is the point

All of the above is machinery in service of one thing: whether the reader
*feels* something and whether the audience will. It is the only criterion that
matters and the only one that cannot be checked from a list. A script can be
structurally immaculate and emotionally inert, and the industry has a specific
phrase for it — "well-built, doesn't move me" — which is a pass.

### 12.10 How screenplays fail

| Failure | How it reads | What it costs downstream |
|---|---|---|
| **Passive protagonist** | "I didn't care about him" | Unfixable in the edit; a star will decline the part |
| **Episodic structure** | "It's slow", "it sags in the middle" | Reshoots, or twenty minutes cut out of act two |
| **Premise that can't be stated** | "What is this movie?" | Marketing cannot build a campaign (`09` §12.2) |
| **On-the-nose dialogue** | "Everyone says exactly what they mean" | Actors rewrite it on set; scenes play false |
| **Tonal wobble** | "I didn't know how to feel" | The film tests badly and cannot be recut into coherence |
| **Theme stated, not dramatised** | "It's preachy" | Bad reviews specifically (`16-critics-reviews-and-word-of-mouth.md`) |
| **Too many characters** | "I lost track of who's who" | Cast cost, and scenes cut that were carrying plot |
| **Unearned ending** | "It just stops" / "deus ex machina" | Poor word of mouth and a collapsed second-weekend multiple |
| **Unshootable at the number** | Budget comes in 40% over | Rewrite in prep, or the greenlight is withdrawn (§8) |
| **No role a star wants** | Package won't close | The film doesn't get financed at all |
| **Great scenes, no spine** | "There's a lot to like here" | Endless development; the classic hell candidate (§9.4) |

## 13. The pitch

Pitching is a distinct professional skill, exercised constantly and taught
nowhere. Writers pitch to win assignments (§4.1), writers and producers pitch
to sell originals, executives pitch upward to get a project approved, and
producers pitch to attach directors and stars. The mechanics below apply to
all of them.

### 13.1 The kinds of pitch

| Kind | Purpose | Length |
|---|---|---|
| **The general** | A meet-and-greet; no project. The point is to be memorable and to leave with the room's mandate | 30 min |
| **The take** | Winning an assignment on material the buyer controls | 20–45 min |
| **The original pitch** | Selling a story the writer/producer owns | 15–30 min |
| **The room pitch to a director or star** | Attaching an element | 20–40 min |
| **The internal pitch** | An executive selling upward, often in 3 minutes in a hallway | 3–10 min |
| **The elevator / logline** | The one-sentence version everyone must be able to give | 15 sec |

### 13.2 Who is in the room and what they are doing

For a studio pitch: the **creative executive** who took the meeting (and who
will have to re-pitch it upstairs), sometimes their **boss**, the
**producer(s)** who set the meeting, and an **assistant** taking notes. Four
to six people. The producer's job is to frame at the start and close at the
end; the writer performs the middle. On a competitive go-out, the same pitch
is delivered **five to ten times in one to two weeks**, in a scheduled sweep,
and it improves noticeably by the third.

### 13.3 The anatomy of a pitch

1. **Small talk** (3–5 minutes). Not decorative — this is where the room
   decides whether they'd like to work with you for two years.
2. **The personal in** ("why me"). One short, true reason this story matters
   to you. It converts a transaction into a collaboration and it is the part
   most often skipped and most often missed.
3. **The logline.** One or two sentences. Then stop talking and let it land.
4. **The tone comp.** "It's *X* meets *Y*", or "it's *X* if it were made by
   *Y*." Cliché and indispensable: it tells the buyer what shelf the film sits
   on, what it costs, and who buys a ticket.
5. **The world and the rules.** Especially for genre and science fiction: what
   is possible here and what is not.
6. **The characters.** Two or three, by want, in a line each.
7. **The story, told in present tense**, in acts, hitting the four or five
   big scenes — the ones the trailer would use — with enough texture that the
   room can see them. Fifteen minutes maximum. Do not narrate every plot turn:
   a pitch is not a synopsis, and detail past a point actively loses the room.
8. **The ending.** *Always tell them the ending.* Withholding it to preserve
   the surprise is the most common amateur error; buyers must know the film
   has one, and a good ending is often what closes the sale.
9. **The theme**, in one sentence: what it's about underneath.
10. **The case.** Why now, who the audience is, what it costs, sometimes the
    franchise or sequel logic.
11. **Questions.** The room's questions are the actual test — this is where
    they find out whether the writer has thought about it or only prepared it.
12. **The leave-behind.** A one-page document, or increasingly a designed
    deck or "look book" with images and references. It exists so the exec can
    re-pitch it accurately in a meeting the writer will never attend.

### 13.4 What separates a good pitch from a bad one

1. **It is rehearsed and it does not sound rehearsed.** Professionals run it
   aloud a dozen times and never read from paper. Notes on a card for the beat
   order are acceptable; reading is fatal.
2. **It is the right length.** The most common failure is a pitch that runs
   forty minutes because the writer refuses to cut plot. Buyers stop absorbing
   at twenty.
3. **It reads the room and adapts live.** If the buyer leans in at the
   antagonist, expand; if they check their phone at the world-building, cut to
   act three. This is the difference between a performer and a reciter.
4. **It has a spine, not a plot.** The room should be able to repeat what the
   movie is *about* an hour later. They will not remember the plot.
5. **It answers the obvious objection before it is raised** — cost, rating,
   similarity to a recent flop, the second-act problem inherent to the
   premise. Pre-empting the objection is the single strongest move available.
6. **Energy.** The buyer is estimating whether this person can sustain
   enthusiasm through two years of notes. Flat delivery of a good idea loses
   to lively delivery of an equal one, consistently.
7. **It ends decisively**, with the ending and then silence, rather than
   trailing off into "so, yeah, that's kind of it."

### 13.5 Reading the response

| What they say | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| "We love it — let us discuss internally" | Pass, in most cases |
| "Can you come back and tell it to *my boss*?" | Genuine interest; this is the real signal |
| Detailed questions about budget, casting or date | They are modelling it, which means they are serious |
| "What else have you got?" | Pass on this, interest in the writer |
| Silence for two weeks | Pass |
| A call to the agent within 24–72 hours | Offer |

A pitch sale closes fast or not at all. If it sells, the buyer pays for the
pitch and hires the writer to write the screenplay; if the pitch is bought
from a producer with a writer attached, the writer's deal closes alongside.
If it does not sell anywhere, the common fallback is for the writer to write
it as a spec (§2.1) and try the same buyers again with pages — which
frequently works, because a pitch and a script are judged by different people
in different moods.

## 14. The development executive as a job

Development is staffed by a profession that is invisible in the credits and
decisive in what gets made.

### 14.1 The ladder

| Rung | Typical tenure | What the job actually is |
|---|---|---|
| **Assistant** | 1–3 years | Rolling calls, scheduling, reading everything, writing coverage after hours. The apprenticeship, and it is brutal |
| **Story editor / creative assistant** | 1–2 years | Reading, tracking, first-pass notes |
| **Creative executive** | 2–4 years | Finding material, hiring writers, giving notes, running projects day to day |
| **Director of Development / VP** | 3–5 years | A slate; authority to recommend purchases |
| **SVP / EVP Production** | Variable | A genre band; presents at greenlight |
| **President of Production** | 3–5 years, often less | The slate; hires and fires the executives below |

Careers move sideways as much as up — studio to production company to
streamer — and the whole structure turns over on a **three-to-five-year**
cycle, which is the single most important fact about how projects survive
(§9.4).

### 14.2 What separates a good executive from a bad one

1. **Taste that can be defended.** Not "I liked it" but a reasoned position on
   what a piece of material could become, held consistently enough that
   writers and agents learn to read it.
2. **Notes writers can use.** The skill in §6.3 — diagnosing rather than
   prescribing, and prioritising. Writers talk to each other, and an executive
   with a reputation for good notes gets first look at material.
3. **Relationships that return calls.** The concrete asset. An executive is
   substantially the sum of the agents, managers, producers and writers who
   will pick up the phone.
4. **Being early.** Getting to the manuscript, the article or the writer
   before the auction. Everything found in an open auction is expensive.
5. **Selling upward.** A project only advances if the executive can re-pitch
   it convincingly to their boss and then to a room (§13.2). Executives who
   cannot pitch cannot get anything made regardless of taste.
6. **Protecting a project through regimes** — keeping it alive, cheap and
   quiet until the moment is right.
7. **Knowing when to stop.** Recommending the write-off of a project they
   bought. Rare, valuable, and rewarded by nobody.

They are ultimately judged on one number, on a **three-to-five-year lag**:
what got made, and how it did.

## 15. Where projects actually die

A consolidated view, because the failure modes are distributed across every
section above and the shape only becomes visible when they are put together.

| Stage | Typical cause of death | Money already spent | Recoverable? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Submission** | Coverage PASS; nobody with power read it | ~$75 | Yes — resubmit later, retitled, with an attachment |
| **Acquisition** | Outbid; rights encumbered; author refused | Legal time | Sometimes — options lapse (§2.2) |
| **First draft** | The draft did not solve the hard problem | $200K–$500K | Yes, with a new writer |
| **Notes cycle** | Contradictory notes; no decision-maker; drift | $500K–$2M | Rarely, without a new champion |
| **Second/third writer** | Accumulated compromise; the material lost its point | $1M–$4M | Occasionally, by reverting to an early draft |
| **Packaging** | Director passed; star's window moved; list exhausted | $1M–$5M | Yes — packages re-form |
| **Budget** | Comes in far over the number the film can support | $2M–$6M | Yes, with a rewrite for cost (§4.5) |
| **Greenlight meeting** | Marketing can't sell it; model doesn't clear; no date | $2M–$10M | Turnaround (§9.1) |
| **Soft greenlight / prep** | Abandoned in prep after crew hired and sets designed | $5M–$25M | Rarely; the write-off is taken |
| **Post pay-or-play** | Almost never — the economics now favour making it | Cast and director fees | The film gets made regardless of merit (§8.5) |

The two things worth carrying away:

1. **The system is designed to kill projects**, and the deaths are not
   failures of the system. A studio that made everything it developed would be
   insolvent within two years. The cost of development is the price paid for
   optionality.
2. **The expensive deaths are the late ones**, and the late deaths are almost
   always caused by problems that were visible and cheap to fix at the outline
   stage. Every practice in this document — the kick-off meeting, the approved
   outline, the response memo, the annotated script, the tech-scout equivalent
   of a script breakdown — exists to move a decision earlier, where it is
   still nearly free.
