# 12 — Talent, Labour & Careers

*How careers actually work.* Reputation, availability, quotes, and relationships
are the persistent state of the industry — the films are temporary, the people
are not.

> **Domain reference.** Real industry, not this game. See `README.md`.

---

## 1. The representation stack

| Party | Function | Reports to / answers to | Typical fee |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Agent** | Procures employment, negotiates deals, works the market | The agency; a department head; ultimately the client | 10% |
| **Manager** | Career strategy, development, producing, day-to-day counsel | The client, almost exclusively | 10–15% |
| **Entertainment lawyer** | Papers the deal, negotiates long-form | The client; firm partnership | 5% or hourly ($600–$1,500/hr) |
| **Publicist** | Press, image, crisis, awards campaigning | The client; the firm | $5K–$25K/month |
| **Business manager** | Money, tax, investments, the loan-out company | The client | ~5% |

A fully-represented star pays 25–30% off the top, before tax, before the
loan-out company's costs, and before the agent's 10% is charged on the *gross*
deal rather than the net. This is why gross quotes and net take-home differ so
sharply, why buyouts and box-office bonuses are negotiated so hard, and why an
actor on a $2M quote is not a person with $2M.

Not everyone has all five. A working actor typically has an agent and,
increasingly, a manager, and hires a lawyer per deal. A publicist is bought
when there is something to publicise — a release, a campaign, or a problem. A
business manager arrives when the income becomes lumpy enough to mishandle.
Below-the-line crew are largely unrepresented; **department heads** —
cinematographers, production designers, editors, composers, costume designers —
are represented, usually by a specialist agent inside a big agency's
below-the-line department or a boutique, on the same 10%.

### 1.1 What each one actually does, week to week

**The agent.** An agent covers a *market*, not just a client. A typical
feature-side talent agent carries **20–60 clients** with an assistant on the
desk, and their week is:

- **Monday staff meeting** — the agency-wide ritual. Every agent in the room
  says what their clients need and what jobs they know about. This is the
  mechanism by which one agent's knowledge of a casting becomes another
  agent's submission, and it is the single largest structural advantage a big
  agency has over a boutique.
- **Tracking and grids** — maintaining a live picture of every project in
  development and production: what stage, who's attached, who's casting, which
  roles are open, who is a competitor for them. Junior staff maintain the grid;
  the agent's value is knowing which line on it is real.
- **Servicing** — 20–50 calls and messages a day to clients, casting
  directors, producers, studio and streamer executives, and other agents.
  "Servicing" mostly means being the person a buyer thinks of at the moment
  they have a hole to fill.
- **Submissions and pitches** — putting the client forward for specific roles,
  usually against a breakdown (`04-preproduction.md` §8.1) but more valuably
  against a role that hasn't been broken down yet.
- **Availability management** — holding, releasing, and stacking dates. See
  §5.6; this is the part of the job outsiders never see and the part that most
  determines whether a client can take a job.
- **Deal-making** — the actual negotiation with business affairs, §2.4.
- **Signing** — pursuing new clients, which is how an agent grows, and which
  every agent is doing to every other agent's list at all times.

**The manager.** Fewer clients — typically **8–20** — and a longer horizon.
The manager is the one who reads the client's scripts, talks them through a
decision at 11pm, tells them the truth about a bad choice, and takes the call
when the agent's advice and the client's instinct disagree. Managers also
**develop and produce**: a manager who finds material for a client and sets it
up as a producer earns from the film as well as the commission, which is the
main economic reason management companies exist. In California a manager may
not legally procure employment; in practice the line is stretched constantly
and enforced rarely, usually only when a client sues to void commissions.

**The lawyer.** Two distinct jobs. First, **deal negotiation** — on many
deals the lawyer, not the agent, actually negotiates the terms, because the
terms are legal (approvals, definitions of gross, sequel options) rather than
commercial. Second, **papering** — turning a two-page deal memo into a 60–120
page long-form agreement, a process that routinely finishes after the film has
wrapped. Lawyers are frequently the most durable relationship in a career:
agents move agencies and managers get fired, but the lawyer who set up the
loan-out company in year one is often still there in year thirty.

**The publicist.** Distinguish two roles: the **unit publicist** works for the
film (`09-marketing-and-distribution.md`), the **personal publicist** works for
the client. The personal publicist's week:

- Fielding and filtering press requests; deciding which outlets get access and
  in what order (a cover is traded for exclusivity, and exclusivity is the only
  currency a publicist has).
- Preparing the client — what the story is this cycle, what questions are
  coming, what not to say, media training before a first big tour.
- Approvals — photo approval, quote approval, image selection. A star with
  photo approval and a slow publicist is a real bottleneck on a key-art
  deadline (§6).
- Awards campaigning in season (`13-awards-and-critical-reception.md` §5) —
  from October to March this can be a full-time job on one client.
- **Crisis** — the part the retainer is really for. Getting ahead of a story,
  trading it down, timing an announcement into a noisy news day, or deciding
  the correct response is silence.

**The business manager.** Pays the client's bills, runs the **loan-out
corporation** (the single-member company that most talent is actually employed
through, for tax and liability reasons), handles quarterly estimated taxes,
tracks residuals and participation statements, and — the underrated part —
tells the client what their actual burn rate is. A large fraction of visible
career desperation is a business-management failure rather than a career
failure: an actor whose fixed costs assume the good year has to keep taking the
job.

### 1.2 How the four coordinate — and clash

The team talks to each other constantly, usually without the client, and the
recurring structural tensions are predictable:

| Tension | Who's on each side | What it's really about |
|---|---|---|
| Take the money vs build the career | Agent vs manager | The agent commissions this year's income; the manager is playing a five-year game and has a producing stake in it |
| Exposure vs scarcity | Publicist vs manager | Press builds awareness but spends mystique; over-covered talent gets discounted |
| Deal speed vs deal terms | Agent vs lawyer | The agent wants it closed before the studio cools; the lawyer wants the definition of gross fixed |
| Producer credit | Manager vs agent | A manager attaching as producer takes a fee the agent sees as a conflict, and takes control of material |
| Whose client is this | Agent vs agent, agency vs manager | Commission stacking makes everyone's incentive to be the indispensable one |
| Say yes to the studio vs protect the client | All of them vs the client | Reps live on continuing relationships with buyers; a client who burns a studio costs the rep more than it costs the client |

The good version: a weekly or per-decision call where the agent brings the
market picture, the manager brings the strategy, the lawyer brings the risk,
and one of them is designated to give the client the answer. The bad version:
four people telling a client four things, the client picking the flattering
one, and nobody owning the outcome.

### 1.3 What separates a great agent from a bad one

The skill axis. It is not the contacts list — everyone at that level has the
contacts list.

1. **Knowing what the client should be doing next**, which requires an actual
   opinion about the client's talent, not just their market price. The best
   agents talk about roles; the worst talk about numbers.
2. **Getting a script read by the right person, unsolicited.** Anyone can
   submit. Getting a director to actually open it on a Sunday is the job.
3. **Being told the truth by buyers.** A studio executive tells a trusted agent
   "he's third on the list and the director isn't excited"; they tell an
   untrusted one "we love him, we'll come back to you". An agent who cannot
   tell a stall from a maybe wastes a client's window.
4. **Reading the room ahead of the market** — knowing a project is falling
   apart, or that a director is about to be replaced, or that a role is being
   quietly reconceived, before it is public.
5. **Negotiating without poisoning the relationship.** A deal is worth one
   picture; the relationship with that studio's business affairs head is worth
   twenty. Agents who win every point stop getting the early call.
6. **Knowing when to say no on the client's behalf** — and having the standing
   to make the pass land as strategy rather than unavailability.
7. **Managing the calendar as an asset.** Sequencing two films and a press
   commitment so all three are possible is worth more to a client's year than
   any single negotiation.
8. **Being findable.** An unglamorous but real differentiator: the agent who
   answers at 9pm on a Friday when a start date moves gets the job for their
   client.
9. **Willingness to lose money on a client's behalf** — advising the passion
   project at scale when it's right, which is exactly what the commission
   structure disincentivises and the thing that keeps clients for decades.

The bad agent, concretely: submits the client for everything so nothing is a
signal; quotes an unverifiable number and gets caught; lets an offer sit
because they were on a plane; commits the client to overlapping dates; is the
last to know a project moved; sells the client hard into a job that is wrong
for them because the commission is large; and, most commonly, simply does
nothing — the client's list entry goes untouched for months.

### 1.4 How a client is signed, developed, and lost

**Signed.** The typical sequence, over 2–8 weeks:

| Stage | What happens | Who |
|---|---|---|
| **Signal** | Something makes the client visible: a festival premiere, a breakout episode, a self-tape circulating, a play, a short, a big audition that lost but impressed | The market |
| **The call** | Agents call the manager, lawyer, or casting director who has the client. Multiple agencies within days | Agents, junior agents |
| **General meetings** | The client meets agents individually. Both sides are auditioning | Client, agent |
| **The signing meeting** | The big-agency version: a room of 6–15 agents from film, TV, talent, endorsements, books, live, each describing what they'd do. Impressive and slightly terrifying by design | Client, department heads |
| **The plan** | The best version is specific: three directors they'd get the client in a room with, a named role they think is gettable, a strategy for the next 18 months | Agent |
| **Signature** | Agency agreements are typically **1–3 years**, and guild franchise rules give the client an out if the agent fails to procure work over a defined window (historically ~91 days) | Lawyer |

**Developed.** What a rep actually does with a client over years: manufacture
visibility between jobs (a play, a short, a supporting role in a prestige
film), correct the trajectory when the client is being offered the same thing
repeatedly, spend relationship capital to get a meeting that is a reach, and
choose the moment to raise the quote (§2.3). The unglamorous version of
development is refusing eight offers so the ninth is possible.

**Lost.** The realistic causes, roughly in order of frequency:

| Cause | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Poaching** | A rival agency runs at a client with a specific, better plan — often after a hit, and often within days of it |
| **The agent leaves** | Agents move agencies constantly and the clients they can carry are their dowry. Clients are forced to choose between the person and the institution |
| **Drift** | Nothing happens for 12–24 months and the relationship is quietly dead before it's formally ended |
| **Blame** | A career decision goes badly and the rep is fired for it, whoever actually made it. This is common and often unjust |
| **Being dropped** | Agencies prune. A client whose annual commission doesn't support a share of a desk is let go — usually softly, by being reassigned to a junior and then ignored |
| **Growing out** | A client who becomes a producer or moves to a different centre of gravity restructures the whole team |
| **Conflict** | The agency represents a competing client for the same role, or a buyer the client is suing |

## 2. The quote system

A talent's **quote** is what they were last paid for a comparable job. It is
the single most important number in any negotiation because it sets the floor,
and because it is the only piece of the negotiation both sides treat as an
external fact rather than an opinion.

### 2.1 What a quote actually is, and how many of them there are

There is no single quote. A working performer has a small portfolio of them,
and conflating them is the fastest way to look amateur in a negotiation:

| Quote | What it covers | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Studio theatrical lead quote** | Fee for a lead in a mid-to-large studio feature | The headline number, and the one people mean by "his quote" |
| **Independent quote** | A lower number the actor will genuinely work for on a low-budget film | Often 10–30% of the studio quote, and deliberately not treated as precedent |
| **Streaming buyout quote** | A single figure covering all rights with no back end | Usually *above* the theatrical fee, as compensation for the lost upside |
| **Series quote** | Per-episode fee for a returning series | Escalates by season under the contract, and is negotiated as a package with episode count |
| **Supporting / limited-days quote** | A week or two of work in a bigger film | Priced on days, not on the actor's lead value |
| **Voice quote** | Animation and games | A different, lower market with its own scale |
| **Endorsement quote** | Commercials and brand deals, by territory and term | Frequently the largest number in the portfolio and the one least discussed |

Quotes are also **budget-relative**. "Ten against fifteen" (a $10M fee against
15% of some defined gross) on a $200M tentpole tells you nothing about what the
same actor takes on a $25M drama.

### 2.2 How a quote is established and tracked

There is no registry. A quote is an **assertion by the agent that the buyer
tries to verify**, and the verification apparatus is entirely informal:

- **Business affairs institutional memory.** Every studio's business affairs
  department keeps its own deal database. They know precisely what *they* paid,
  and they know a great deal about what their peers paid because the same
  lawyers paper both sides.
- **Agency grids.** Agencies track competitors' clients' deals for exactly
  this reason; a big agency's knowledge of the market is one of its products.
- **The trades.** Deadline, Variety and THR report deal figures that are
  leaked, usually by the side the number flatters. A number in the trades is
  frequently a negotiating tactic rather than a report.
- **Cross-pollinated staff.** Business affairs executives, lawyers and agents
  move between employers and carry the numbers in their heads.
- **The completion bond and the finance plan.** On independents, the cast cost
  is visible to financiers and sales agents, so it leaks structurally.

The result is a market where the *approximate* number is common knowledge and
the *exact* structure — how much is cash, how much is bonus, what the backend
definition is, what was paid for by perks — is not. Almost all quote gamesmanship
happens in that gap.

### 2.3 The manoeuvres used to protect a quote

Talent will do almost anything rather than accept a lower headline number,
because next year's floor is this year's number. The standard moves:

| Manoeuvre | How it works | What it protects |
|---|---|---|
| **Favoured nations** | Everyone above the line takes the same terms | Nobody is "worth less"; the low number is a stated condition of the film, not a valuation of the actor |
| **Scale plus ten** | Guild minimum plus the 10% that covers the agent's commission | The canonical prestige/indie gesture; universally understood not to set a quote |
| **Backend in lieu** | Small cash, large participation or box-office bonuses | The realised number can exceed the quote if the film works, and no precedent is set if it doesn't |
| **Deferment** | Fee paid out of receipts at a defined point | Cash-flows the production; the *contracted* fee is still the quote |
| **Producer or EP fee** | Part of the compensation arrives as a producing fee | Two payments, one quote |
| **The no-precedent letter** | Side letter stating the fee is unique to the project and may not be quoted | Legally weak, socially effective |
| **Days-limited deal** | Full quote pro-rated for two weeks' work | Preserves the daily/weekly rate implied by the quote |
| **Perk loading** | Travel, housing, security, trainer, chef, assistants, private jet | Real money to the production, invisible to the quote |
| **Escalators and bumps** | Fee rises if the budget rises, if the schedule extends, if the film opens above a threshold | Makes the low number conditional |
| **Second-picture protection** | Discount now, quote-or-better guaranteed on the next picture with that buyer | Converts a concession into an option |
| **Equity** | A slice of the production entity or the IP | Increasingly common on packages the talent helped assemble |

Coming *down* is done the same way in reverse and is never announced: the
actor "does an independent for scale because they love the director", takes a
streaming series, takes a big supporting role in a franchise film at a
supporting price, or simply lets a year pass during which the old quote goes
stale and is quietly not re-asserted. What an agent will not do is say a lower
number out loud into a market that will remember it.

### 2.4 How a negotiation actually proceeds

A studio feature offer to an established actor, turn by turn. Typical elapsed
time from first contact to a signed deal memo is **2–8 weeks**; the long-form
agreement lands **1–6 months** later, often after photography.

| # | Turn | Who | What is actually said |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Avail check** | Casting director → agent | "Is she free March to June, and would she read this?" No numbers, no offer, no obligation. Purely temperature |
| 2 | **Materials out** | CD/producer → agent | Script, sometimes a director's letter. For a wanted actor the letter matters more than the money (`04-…` §8.1) |
| 3 | **Interest** | Agent → CD | "She responded. She'd want to meet him." Or the stall: "she's reading it" for three weeks, which means no |
| 4 | **The meeting** | Director + actor | Where star casting is actually decided. A mutual audition |
| 5 | **Opening call** | Business affairs → agent | "We'd like to make an offer. Where are we?" The buyer almost never opens with a number |
| 6 | **The ask** | Agent → business affairs | The quote, the structure, and the reach: "Her quote is 6 against 5% of adjusted gross; for a March start with these dates she'd need 8, first position, and the sequel option at 12" |
| 7 | **Verification** | Business affairs, internally | Was the quote real? Was it a foreign-financed picture that overpaid? Was it a buyout that shouldn't compare? Was it two years ago? |
| 8 | **The counter** | Business affairs → agent | Structure, not just level: less cash, more bonus, backend moved to cash break, the perks package built out |
| 9 | **The elements** | Both, in parallel | Dates and stop date, exclusivity, credit position and size, approvals, travel and perks, publicity days, sequel/option terms, pay-or-play trigger (§6) |
| 10 | **The blow-up** | Either | The deal dies over something that isn't money — usually credit, a stop date, or an approval — roughly as often as over money |
| 11 | **Deal memo** | Lawyers | 2–5 pages, signed, and typically the moment **pay-or-play** attaches. From here the actor is effectively cast and the studio is effectively committed |
| 12 | **Long form** | Lawyers | 60–120 pages. Definitions of gross, warranty and morals clauses, insurance obligations, digital-replica consent. Closes late; sometimes never formally, if the parties operate off the memo |

**Where leverage sits, and when it moves.** This is the part that is invisible
from the outside:

- *Before the offer:* the studio has it. The list is 20 deep and the actor
  doesn't know their position on it.
- *After the director has met the actor and wants only them:* the actor has it,
  and everyone in the room knows.
- *After the start date locks and sets are being built:* the actor has enormous
  leverage and both sides pretend otherwise. This is when perks, approvals and
  publicity limits are actually won.
- *After the film is shot:* the studio has it back, except for reshoot and
  publicity obligations, which is precisely why those are negotiated up front.
- *On a sequel:* the actor has the most leverage in the business, which is why
  sequel options are locked in the original deal (§6) and why studios that
  forget to do so pay for it.

### 2.5 How quote negotiations fail

| Failure | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **Quoting a number that can't be verified** | The buyer discovers the deal was a one-off; the agent's credibility takes a lasting hit and the offer resets low |
| **Holding out past the studio's decision date** | The list moves on. The role is offered to number two and the actor learns from the trades |
| **Winning every point** | The deal closes and the relationship doesn't survive; the next early call goes elsewhere |
| **Ignoring the structure** | A big headline fee with a worthless backend definition (`11-…` §2) reads as a win and pays like a loss |
| **Two agencies bidding the client against themselves** | Manager and agent both negotiating produces two numbers and an embarrassed retreat |
| **Overlapping holds** | The client is committed to two films whose dates move into each other; one of them has to be broken, at real cost |
| **Breaking the quote publicly** | The lower number becomes the new floor for everyone, including on the next film |
| **Renegotiating on the day** | Effective once. It buys money and costs the relationship with a studio that has a long memory and a long slate |

### 2.6 Directional fees

Studio feature, US, contemporary. Every figure is a wide band.

| Tier | Fee |
|---|---|
| SAG scale day player | ~$1,200/day |
| SAG weekly performer | ~$4,300/week |
| Working actor, supporting role | $50K–$500K |
| Recognisable lead, mid-budget | $1M–$3M |
| A-list star | $10M–$20M+ |
| A-list with backend/bonuses | $20M–$100M+ realised on a hit |
| Streaming buyout for a star | Often above the theatrical quote, as compensation for no backend |
| Prestige/indie lead at scale-plus | Low five figures, plus points |
| Series regular, streaming drama | $75K–$500K+/episode; established stars far above |
| Voice, animated feature | $50K–$1M+ for a name |

## 3. What actually makes a star bankable

Bankability is a market judgment, not a talent judgment. It is a forecast — a
claim about how much money the presence of a specific name adds to a specific
film in a specific market — and it is made by people whose own jobs depend on
the forecast being defensible afterwards.

### 3.1 Who actually assesses it, and with what

| Assessor | What they're deciding | What they use |
|---|---|---|
| **Studio distribution & research** | Whether the name justifies the budget and the date | Tracking history, opening comps, awareness/likeability surveys, the star's last three openings, franchise vs star attribution |
| **Studio business affairs** | What the name is worth paying | Quote history, competing offers, what the finance plan can bear |
| **International sales agent** | What a package will pre-sell for, territory by territory | The **estimates grid** (§3.2), buyer feedback from the last market, the star's local box-office history |
| **Foreign distributors/buyers** | Whether to commit money to an unmade film | Their own territory's history with that actor, local awareness, genre fit, dubbing/star-vehicle traditions |
| **Equity financiers and gap lenders** | Whether the estimates support the loan | The sales agent's grid, discounted; the completion bond; the cast's insurability |
| **Streamers** | Whether the name drives sign-ups or retention | Internal viewership data, which is proprietary, unshared, and therefore unarguable |
| **Insurers and completion bond** | Whether the person is a risk (`03-…` §§7–8) | Medical exam, claims history, prior production incidents |

The **estimates grid** is the concrete artefact and worth understanding
precisely: a spreadsheet with one row per territory (Germany, France, Japan,
Korea, Latin America, and so on) and low/mid/high dollar estimates for a given
package — this genre, at this budget, with this director and this cast. Change
the lead actor and the grid changes; the *difference between two grids* is the
most literal statement of a star's value that exists anywhere in the industry.
Financiers then discount the grid (typically lending against something like
60–80% of the low estimates for contracted territories, far less for
un-presold ones) — see `03-financing-and-dealmaking.md` §4.

### 3.2 Three different kinds of star value

These are routinely collapsed into one number and they behave completely
differently.

| | **Opening-weekend value** | **Pre-sale / international value** | **Prestige value** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | Converts awareness into first-weekend ticket sales | Persuades foreign buyers to commit money before the film exists | Attracts directors, material, and awards attention |
| **Who pays for it** | Studios on wide domestic releases | Independent financiers, sales agents, foreign distributors | Prestige producers, specialty labels, streamers building credibility |
| **Evidence used** | Prior opening records where the star was the draw, not the IP | Territory-level history; a name that "travels" | Nominations, critical standing, the directors who want to work with them |
| **Genre-bound?** | Strongly | Very strongly — action and thriller travel; comedy famously does not | Weakly |
| **Decay rate** | Fast: two under-performing openings is enough | Slow: foreign value persists years after domestic value has gone | Very slow; often *rises* with age |
| **Failure mode** | The film opens to the name and collapses in week two | The picture is financed on estimates it can never earn back; the gap loan is exposed | The film gets made and nobody sees it |

A star can be strong in one column and worthless in the others. An action lead
with a decade of foreign hits can carry an $40M pre-sold picture and add
nothing to a domestic opening. A three-time nominee with no commercial record
can get a film financed by a specialty label and cannot open a multiplex. This
is the single most important corrective to the idea of "star power" as a scalar.

### 3.3 The components, expanded

1. **Opening record** — has the name previously converted awareness into
   opening weekend, *distinct* from the IP they were attached to. The
   attribution question is the whole question, and studios argue about it
   internally with real heat: was that a star opening or a franchise opening
   with a person standing in front of it?
2. **International value** — foreign distributors' estimates by territory.
   Some names are worth multiples abroad of what they're worth at home, and a
   few are worth almost nothing outside one region.
3. **Genre fit** — bankability is genre-bound. An action star's name is worth
   little on a romantic comedy; a comedy star's international grid is often
   brutally thin because comedy doesn't translate.
4. **Recency** — the market discounts fast. Two flops materially reduce a
   quote, and the second one is worth much more than the first because it
   converts "bad luck" into "a pattern".
5. **Reliability** — insurable, professional, shows up, does the press, and
   doesn't cost the production days. Priced explicitly by the bond company and
   implicitly by every producer who has been burned.
6. **Awards standing** — raises prestige-project value and directorial access
   far more than it raises commercial value.
7. **Awareness vs affinity** — measured separately in research. High awareness
   with low affinity (a famous person audiences don't want to spend two hours
   with) is worse for a film than obscurity, because the campaign can't fix it.
8. **Willingness to promote** — a star who will do the full tour is worth an
   enormous amount of earned media (`09-…` §12.1); a contractually minimal
   press commitment measurably reduces what the name is worth.
9. **The floor, not the ceiling** — much of what a name buys is *downside
   protection*: a foreign minimum, a streaming licence value, a reason the film
   gets bought at all. Financiers care about the floor far more than studios do.

### 3.4 What is not bankability, despite being widely assumed to be

- **Social media following.** Heavily discounted by everyone who has tested it.
  Followers are a distribution channel for a message, not a ticket-buying
  commitment.
- **Critical acclaim.** Buys access and awards traction, not opening weekend.
- **Being "famous".** Fame from a non-film source converts unreliably; the
  graveyard of stunt-cast celebrity leads is large.
- **Streaming viewership.** Real, but the data is held by the platform that
  owns it and is deployed selectively in negotiations, which makes it a
  rhetorical instrument as much as evidence.

### 3.5 The honest consensus, and the lag

The industry consensus of the last two decades: **IP outperforms stars** for
opening weekend on tentpoles, while stars still matter enormously for
mid-budget films, for international pre-sales, and for getting a film financed
at all.

Two structural facts make bankability strange to model:

- **It is measured on films that were cast 12–30 months earlier.** A star's
  current standing is being assessed on results from decisions made before
  their last two films came out. Reputation lags performance by a film or two,
  in both directions.
- **It is only ever tested jointly.** No one ever observes the counterfactual
  film without the star, so every attribution is an argument. This is why the
  market moves in narratives — "he can't open a film any more" — rather than
  in measurements.

## 4. The career arc

A rough but recognisable shape:

```
Unknown ─► Working actor ─► Breakout ─► Heat ─► Established ─► Plateau
                                          │                       │
                                          └─► Overexposure ──────►┘
                                                       │
                                          Reinvention ─┴─► Character actor /
                                                            elder statesman
```

Very few people traverse the whole diagram. The overwhelmingly common career
is *working actor*, indefinitely: a person with credits, a modest quote, and a
constant low-grade problem of finding the next job. Most of the mechanisms
below describe the minority who move.

### 4.1 What actually causes a breakout

A breakout is not "being noticed". It is a specific market event with three
ingredients, and it requires all three:

1. **A singular role.** Something that only that actor appears able to do — a
   part with a shape, not a function. Second-lead-who-explains-the-plot never
   breaks anyone out, however well played.
2. **A film or show people actually see, or that the right people see.** Either
   scale (a hit) or concentration (a festival premiere where 400 of the correct
   people are in the room in one night).
3. **A moment of legibility.** The performance has to be attributable. If
   audiences remember the character and not the actor, or the film and not the
   performance, nothing transfers.

The mechanism afterwards is fast and quite mechanical:

| Day | What happens |
|---|---|
| 0 | Premiere, or release, or the episode drops |
| 1–3 | Trade reviews single the performance out; the agency's tracking desk flags it |
| 3–14 | Offers and meeting requests arrive — often 20–100 scripts in a month where there were previously none. Rival agencies call |
| 2–8 weeks | The client's team is restructured: a bigger agency, a manager added, a lawyer upgraded, a publicist hired |
| 1–3 months | The quote resets, typically **3–10×**, on the first job that closes at the new level |
| 3–12 months | The second confirming job. Without it, the breakout deflates and the quote quietly reverts |

**The confirmation requirement is the part people miss.** A breakout is a
hypothesis the market has taken out; the second job is the test. An actor who
follows a breakout with a well-chosen second role becomes established. An actor
who follows it with nothing, or with three interchangeable versions of the
first one, is back to being a working actor within two years, with a slightly
better address book.

### 4.2 What heat consists of, and how it is spent

**Heat** is the market's forward belief that this person is about to be more
valuable than they currently are. It is not fame and it is not quality. Its
observable symptoms:

- Offers arrive without auditions; **offer-only** status is granted (`04-…`
  §8.1).
- Scripts are sent before they are finished, and directors take the meeting.
- Attaching the name moves financing — a project that couldn't close, closes.
- The quote resets upward in a single step rather than incrementally.
- Studios begin to describe the actor as an "element" in packaging conversations.
- Everyone is suddenly very nice, which is genuinely disorienting and is a real
  contributor to bad decisions in this window.

Heat lasts roughly **12–36 months** and is a depleting asset: it is spent
whether or not it is used. The strategic question of a career is what it gets
converted into.

| Way of spending heat | What it buys | What it costs | Typical outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cash** — take the biggest offer | A quote reset and financial security | Nothing, if the film works; the quote if it doesn't | The default, and often correct if the money is life-changing |
| **A franchise** | 3–8 years of guaranteed employment, global recognition, a huge international grid | Availability (first position for years), typecasting, association with a single character | The most powerful and most confining conversion |
| **A prestige director** | Standing, awards traction, access to directors who were previously unreachable | Money, often a lot of it, and a year of availability | Converts commercial heat into durable prestige value — historically the highest-return move |
| **A passion project** | Creative satisfaction; occasionally a career-defining film | Everything, if it doesn't work; a passion project flop is the flop that gets blamed on the actor | High variance |
| **A producing deal** | Control of material; a second career that outlives the acting one | Time and attention during the years the acting career is hottest | Slow-burning and frequently the best long-run choice |
| **Nothing** — hold out for perfect | Preserved mystique in theory | The window, in practice. Heat cannot be banked | The most common way heat is wasted |
| **Everything at once** — four films in eighteen months | Maximum immediate income | Overexposure; the market discounts a face it sees constantly | Reliably burns the asset |

**Overexposure** is a real market mechanism, not a moral one: too many films,
too similar, too fast means the next opening is competing with the actor's own
prior films for the same audience, while the campaign's novelty value —
the thing publicity is actually selling — approaches zero.

### 4.3 What a plateau looks like from inside

Plateau is not failure and is where most successful careers spend most of their
time. The internal experience is specific and recognisable:

- The work continues. The money is fine. Nothing has gone wrong.
- The offers are all the same role, and they arrive later in the process — the
  actor is now on the list rather than at the top of it.
- Roles that would once have come as an offer now come as "would he read it?"
- The quote is flat for three or four years, which in a rising market is a
  quiet decline.
- The interesting scripts go to two or three other people first, and the actor
  can name them.
- The team's language changes: from "what do you want to do next" to "this one
  is a good payday and it shoots in the summer".

A plateau ends in one of three ways: a reinvention (§4.5), a slide into
character work (which is frequently the most creatively rewarding phase of a
career), or a slow decline that nobody ever announces.

### 4.4 How decline actually operates

A flop does not end a career. **A flop that is attributed to you** starts the
chain, and the chain is mechanical:

```
Under-performance ─► attribution ("nobody came for him")
      └─► quote becomes unverifiable — buyers refuse to pay the last number
            └─► offers shift to smaller budgets and foreign-financed pictures
                  └─► international estimates soften territory by territory
                        └─► the name becomes a "value element": helps the sale,
                            costs little, cannot carry the film
                              └─► the directors who want the actor shrink to
                                  those who need the name, not the actor
```

Each step takes 12–24 months, which is why decline is usually visible to the
industry long before it is visible to the public, and why the actor is often
the last to be told. The countervailing fact: the chain is only reliably
triggered by *attributable* failure. Being in a film that fails for its own
reasons — bad date, bad campaign, an incoherent movie — costs far less than
being the reason the film was made.

### 4.5 How a career actually recovers

Recovery is not a change in the actor. It is a change in who publicly wants
them, and it requires a *sponsor with standing*:

| Route | Mechanism | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| **The prestige director** | A filmmaker whose taste the industry trusts casts against the actor's current market value | The director's judgment is borrowed. The market re-rates the actor because someone credible has vouched |
| **The role that reframes** | A part that uses the accumulated public image rather than fighting it — often playing a version of the persona with the varnish off | Requires the career history to work; a newcomer literally cannot play it |
| **Elevated genre** | Horror and low-budget thriller are cheap, risk-tolerant, and reach a big audience | The lowest-cost place in the industry to be seen doing something new |
| **Prestige television / streaming series** | Once a demotion, now a legitimate rehabilitation route with a large audience and awards traction | Long-form gives an actor 8–10 hours to re-establish what they can do |
| **The scene-stealing supporting role** | Two weeks' work in a big film that everyone talks about | Cheap for the production, low-risk, and immediately legible |
| **The franchise legacy role** | Returning to, or joining, an established property | Restores the international grid instantly; restores prestige not at all |
| **Festival exposure** | A small film that plays well at a major festival | Concentrated attention from exactly the people who make casting decisions |

The requirement common to all of them: someone else takes the risk first. An
actor cannot decide to be reinvented.

### 4.6 Typecasting, mechanically

Typecasting is the industry's memory, not its malice. Casting is a
risk-reduction exercise (`04-…` §8): a director choosing between an actor
they've *seen* do the thing and an actor they *believe could* do the thing will
take the evidence almost every time, because they have 40 shooting days and no
capacity to be wrong. Every performance is therefore both an achievement and a
constraint on the next one.

The escape is standard and expensive:

1. **Counter-cast with a trusted director** — the actor takes a much smaller
   fee and often a smaller part, in exchange for evidence.
2. **The evidence has to be visible.** A counter-cast performance in a film
   nobody sees does not update the market.
3. **Repeat once.** One counter-cast role is an anomaly; two is a range.

Typecasting is commercially useful and creatively fatal, which is the tension
of the whole thing: the type is what generates the offers that fund the escape.

### 4.7 How ageing actually operates

Ageing is not a gentle taper; it is a change in the *shape of the available
role distribution*, and it has always been sharply asymmetric by gender.

- **Role supply.** The number of written lead roles peaks for women in their
  twenties and thins markedly from the mid-thirties, while male lead roles
  hold into the fifties. This is a well-documented feature of role availability
  and pay, and it is a property of what gets written and financed rather than
  of any single casting decision.
- **The character-actor transition.** For most careers, the move from lead to
  supporting is the actual event, and it is usually made *for* the actor rather
  than by them. Handled well — accepting it early and choosing good parts — it
  produces the longest and steadiest phase of a career.
- **Physical and insurance constraints.** An older lead on a physically
  demanding film means more doubling, more stunt scheduling, more coverage
  planned around what they can do, a stricter medical for the completion bond
  (`03-…` §§7–8), and sometimes an uninsurable answer that quietly ends
  consideration.
- **The elder-statesman premium.** Late-career actors with long records attract
  a specific class of prestige supporting role, are attractive to awards voters,
  and are cheap relative to their name value. Voice work, franchise legacy
  parts, and narration form a real late-career annuity.
- **Directors invert this entirely.** Directorial careers frequently peak in the
  fifties and sixties, because the currency is trust and track record rather
  than being photographed.

### 4.8 Scandal, behaviour, and insurability

Consequences vary enormously and unpredictably, but the *mechanism* is
consistent, and it is commercial rather than moral. Three questions get asked:

1. **Is the person still insurable and bondable?** If not, they cannot be cast
   at all, regardless of anyone's opinion.
2. **What is the studio's existing exposure?** A finished, unreleased film with
   the person in it is the real hostage. Cost of reshooting or replacing scales
   from "trivial" to "the film is unreleasable".
3. **Does the campaign still work?** Marketing is built around press access and
   a face on a poster. If neither is usable, the release plan collapses.

Timing therefore dominates severity:

| When it breaks | Typical consequence |
|---|---|
| Before casting closes | The actor is quietly not cast. No announcement, no cost |
| After deal memo, before shooting | Recast; the pay-or-play obligation is often still owed (§6) |
| During photography | Extremely expensive: replace and reshoot, or suspend. Insurance may or may not respond |
| After wrap, before release | Recut, delay, dump, or shelve. Reshooting a supporting role is possible; a lead is usually not |
| After release | Affects future employment only, which is where the variance is widest |

The same machinery handles the far more common, far less dramatic version:
someone who is simply difficult. Reputation for behaviour is priced by
producers before it is ever priced by the public (§11.6).

### 4.9 The same arc for directors

The arc applies to **directors**, where the currency is not opening weekend but
*the ability to get the next film financed*: a critical success buys a bigger
budget, a commercial failure buys a smaller one, and two failures in a row can
mean a decade in development. Directors have a longer half-life than actors,
recover more slowly but more completely, and are far more likely to be ended by
a reputation for *cost* than by a reputation for *quality*. See §12.

## 5. Relationships and repeat collaboration

The industry runs on repeat collaboration far more than outsiders expect:

- Directors bring **regular DPs, editors, composers, and production
  designers** — often for decades.
- Directors have **stock companies** of actors.
- Producers and studios sustain **filmmaker relationships** over multiple
  films, sometimes at a loss, to retain access.
- HODs bring their own **crews** (`05-departments-and-crew.md` §1).

### 5.1 How a repeat collaboration actually forms

It is almost never a decision. The observable sequence:

1. **A first job, usually under pressure.** The pair meet on a film that is
   under-resourced or in trouble. Nothing bonds like a bad week.
2. **A trust event.** Something specific happens: the DP finds a way to shoot
   the scene when the day has collapsed; the editor says the third act doesn't
   work when nobody else will; the actor takes a note in front of the crew
   without defending themselves. This is remembered permanently.
3. **The shorthand forms.** After 40 days together the pair stop explaining.
   "A bit more" acquires a precise meaning. Vocabulary that would take an hour
   to establish with a stranger is already there.
4. **The second job confirms it**, and from then on the collaborator is the
   default rather than a candidate. The director stops interviewing for that
   role entirely.

### 5.2 What a director gains from a regular team

Concretely, and it is much more than comfort:

1. **Decision throughput.** A director answers hundreds of questions a day
   (§12.2). A regular team pre-answers most of them correctly, because they
   already know what the answer will be. This is worth days of schedule.
2. **Advocacy when the director isn't in the room.** The production designer
   defends the palette to the studio; the editor protects the pacing intent
   through a note session; the first AD holds the shape of the day.
3. **Honest dissent.** The single hardest thing to obtain as a director is
   someone with nothing to lose telling them the take doesn't work. That is a
   function of accumulated trust, not of hierarchy.
4. **Risk absorption.** A trusted DP will attempt something ambitious, because
   they know they will not be blamed if it fails and they will be believed when
   they say it is possible.
5. **Continuity of taste across films.** A recognisable body of work is often a
   *team's* recognisable body of work.
6. **Known failure modes.** The director knows exactly how this editor gets
   stuck and this designer over-builds, and can manage around it in advance.

### 5.3 What the collaborator gains

Continuity of employment in an intermittent industry; upward budget mobility
(the team rises with the director); creative latitude that a hiring producer
would never extend to a stranger; the credit association itself, which is a
career asset; and — not trivial — the ability to plan a life around a known
calendar rather than a phone that may or may not ring.

### 5.4 How collaborations break

| Cause | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Date collision** | The director's film moves; the DP is already on another picture. The most common cause by a wide margin |
| **Outgrowing the budget** | The collaborator's rate rises past what the director's films can carry |
| **Attributed failure** | A film goes badly and, fairly or not, the blame settles on one department |
| **Studio insistence** | The financier requires an approved list; a first-time-with-this-studio director may not win this fight |
| **Personal rupture** | Exactly as ordinary as it sounds, and usually never explained publicly |
| **Retirement, illness, death** | The end of a working relationship measured in decades |

### 5.5 Institutional relationships

Studios and producers run the same mechanism at company scale: **overall
deals** and **first-look deals** (`01-industry-structure.md` §3) pay a
filmmaker or producer to be in the building, sometimes for years without a
released film, because access to their next idea is worth the carry. The
fragility of these arrangements is a personnel fact: the executive who
championed the relationship leaves, and the successor has no stake in it
(`02-development.md` §9).

### 5.6 The practical consequence: availability is relational

A filmmaker can get a crew that a first-timer with more money cannot, and a
start-date slip loses a whole trusted team to another film. The mechanics of
holding people are precise and worth knowing:

- **Avail** — an informal question: is the person free in this window? Costs
  nothing and commits nobody.
- **Pencil / pin** — a soft hold. The person is marked as likely for those
  dates but can take a firm offer.
- **First position / second position** — a contractual priority. If two films
  claim the same dates, first position wins; a person in second position on a
  film that suddenly needs them is a scheduling crisis.
- **Challenge / release** — a competing production forces a decision: commit or
  free them. Holds that are never challenged can sit for months.
- **Pay-or-play** — the only hold that is genuinely binding, because it costs
  money to break (§6).

Two consequences follow. First, the best person for the job is usually working,
so casting and crewing are exercises in sequencing, not selection. Second, a
slipped start date is not a neutral delay: it releases every soft hold at once,
and the film restaffs at a lower level.

## 6. Contracts: what is actually negotiated

Beyond fee and dates. The third column is the point: every one of these terms
has a specific fight inside it, and deals die over the fights far more often
than over the fee.

| Term | Meaning | What is actually fought over |
|---|---|---|
| **Pay-or-play** | Paid whether or not the film is made (`03-…` §2.1) | *When it attaches* — at deal memo, at a financing condition, or at a fixed date. The studio wants the latest possible trigger; the agent wants it at signature, because it converts an offer into a job |
| **Exclusivity / holds** | First and second position on the talent's calendar | Whether the actor can shoot something else in a gap, do a play, or start prep on the next film. Studios ask for total exclusivity from prep through post; agents carve out windows |
| **Start date / stop date** | Firm outer limits, often driven by another commitment | The **stop date** is the real battle. A hard stop means the schedule must protect the actor's last day, which constrains every other decision (`04-…` §2). Studios resist hard stops; stars insist |
| **Overage** | What happens if the shoot runs past the contracted days | Per-day, per-week, or pro-rated; whether weather days count; whether the rate is the same as the base or a premium |
| **Approvals** | Director, co-star, cut, photograph, likeness, marketing materials | **Still approval** is the genuine bottleneck: an actor with kill rights over their own images, exercised slowly, can hold up key art against a printing deadline. Also fought: whether approval means consultation, approval-not-unreasonably-withheld, or an outright veto |
| **Credit** | Position, size relative to title, paid ad requirements, shared cards | Order (first position, "and" credit, "with" credit), size as a percentage of the title, whether the credit appears in *all* paid ads or only those above a size threshold, and whether a card is shared. Two equal stars produce the **shared card / stacked-and-staggered** compromise where each is first in different territories |
| **Perks** | Travel class, entourage, security, accommodation, per diem, trailer specification, gym/trainer, chef | Real money — a top-tier package can run into six or seven figures over a shoot — and the headcount is the fight: how many assistants, trainers, and security travel at production cost |
| **Nudity/intimacy rider** | Specific, scene-by-scene consent, with an intimacy coordinator | Scope: exactly what is shown, in which scenes, who is on set, what happens to the footage, and whether consent can be withdrawn. Modern riders are explicit and enumerated rather than general |
| **Publicity obligation** | Number of days for junkets, premieres, and press tours | Days (typically 5–15 for a lead), territories travelled, whether social-media posting is included, and whether the days survive if the release slips |
| **Sequel/option terms** | Escalating fees for pictures 2 and 3, negotiated up front | The escalation percentage, how long the option runs, and whether the actor is obliged or merely offered. Studios that fail to lock this pay dramatically more later (§2.4) |
| **Digital replica / AI consent** | Post-2023 SAG-AFTRA terms: informed consent and compensation for scanning and synthetic performance | What the scan may be used for, for how long, whether consent survives the actor, and whether use in *other* productions is permitted. This is the most actively contested area in current talent contracts |
| **Morals clause** | Studio's right to terminate for conduct that brings the film into disrepute | Whether it is reciprocal, how "disrepute" is defined, whether allegation or finding triggers it, and what happens to the fee (§4.8) |
| **Insurance / medical** | The actor must pass an exam and be bondable | Excluded activities during the engagement — skiing, motorcycling, flying — which stars resent and bond companies insist on (`03-…` §8) |
| **Backend definition** | The participation formula | Not the percentage but the *definition*: gross, adjusted gross, or net (`11-…` §2). The percentage is theatre; the definition is the money |

### 6.1 The paper trail

| Document | When | Length | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Offer letter** | Days into the negotiation | 1 page | Not binding; states the shape |
| **Deal memo** | On agreement of the main terms | 2–5 pages | Binding. Usually where pay-or-play attaches. Films are cast, crewed, and sometimes shot entirely on deal memos |
| **Long form** | Weeks to months later | 60–120 pages | The full agreement; frequently signed after wrap |
| **Side letters** | Alongside | 1–3 pages each | No-precedent letters, favoured-nations confirmations, specific perk schedules |
| **Certificate of engagement / loan-out** | At signature | Short | The employment mechanics via the talent's corporation |

### 6.2 Crew and department-head deals

Below the line the same terms exist in miniature and are settled far faster —
often on a one-page **deal memo** issued by the production office, negotiated
by the UPM against the department head directly (`05-…` §1):

- **Rate**, quoted per day or per week, and whether the week is a 5-day
  or 6-day week — a materially different number.
- **Prep and wrap days**, and how many are paid. Under-paying prep is the
  single most common crew-deal dispute.
- **Kit / box rental**, a separate weekly payment for the crew member's own
  equipment; genuinely significant income for sound mixers, hair and makeup
  artists, and DPs with their own kit.
- **Overtime structure**, guaranteed hours, and whether the rate is an
  all-in "flat" that absorbs overtime (much disliked, and non-compliant under
  most union agreements).
- **Turnaround waivers**, which are asked for and paid for.
- **Housing, per diem, and travel** on a distant location, which frequently
  determines whether a job is worth taking at all.
- **Credit** — position in the crawl, and for HODs, a card.

## 7. Guild minimums and protections

See `01-industry-structure.md` §6 for the bodies. The protections that most
shape day-to-day work, with the mechanics that make them bite:

### 7.1 Minimums and tiers

Minimums ("scale") are set **by budget tier and by job**, which is why the
budget number itself is a negotiating instrument — a film budgeted a dollar
under a threshold pays a different rate card for everyone.

- **WGA** minimums are split by budget tier (a "low budget" tier below roughly
  the mid-single-digit millions, and a high tier above), and by whether the
  work is a treatment, a first draft, a rewrite, or a polish. Each is a
  separately payable **step** (`02-development.md` §4).
- **DGA** minimums cover the director, UPM, and 1st/2nd ADs, on a weekly basis
  with guaranteed prep and post periods scaled to budget.
- **SAG-AFTRA** has day-player, weekly, and background rates, plus a family of
  low-budget agreements (below).
- **IATSE** rates are set **local by local and classification by
  classification**, which is why the same job pays differently in Los Angeles,
  New York, Atlanta, and New Mexico.

### 7.2 The fringes

Every union labour dollar carries **fringes** on top — pension, health, and
welfare contributions, plus payroll taxes and payroll-company handling. Budget
**20–35%** on top of the rate as a rule of thumb (`05-…` §17). Two consequences
that matter more than the number:

- Overtime is charged fringes too, so an expensive day is more expensive than
  the multiplier suggests.
- **Hours worked qualify people for health coverage.** Working enough days in a
  qualifying period to keep health insurance is a real, concrete career pressure
  for crew and working actors, and it changes which jobs people accept — a
  short, badly paid union job can be worth more than a long non-union one.

### 7.3 Hours, penalties, and turnaround

The rules that actually shape a shooting day (`06-principal-photography.md` §7):

- **Overtime** — typically time-and-a-half after 8 hours, double time after 12,
  with higher multipliers ("golden time") beyond that on some agreements.
- **Meal penalties** — a meal must be broken at roughly 6-hour intervals;
  running past it charges escalating per-person penalties that compound with
  every additional increment, so the second and third penalties cost more than
  the first.
- **Turnaround** — the minimum rest between wrap and the next call, commonly
  **10 hours** (11 or 12 for some categories and territories), longer over a
  weekend. Breaching it costs a penalty and is the structural reason a schedule
  cannot simply push later each day: a late wrap forces a late call, which
  pushes the whole week.
- **Forced call** — the specific penalty for calling someone back inside their
  turnaround.
- **Sixth and seventh day** premiums, and night premiums.

### 7.4 Getting in, and staying in

- **Taft-Hartley** — a producer may employ a non-union performer on a union
  production by filing a Taft-Hartley report justifying it. After qualifying
  work, the performer becomes **must-join** for subsequent union jobs.
- **SAG-AFTRA eligibility** — broadly, work as a principal performer under a
  union contract, accumulate background days on a union production, or hold
  qualifying membership in an affiliated performers' union.
- **Station 12** — the clearance check a producer runs before engaging a
  performer, confirming they are in good standing (dues paid). A performer who
  has let dues lapse can be blocked from a job at the last minute.
- **Initiation fees and dues** — joining costs a substantial one-off fee (four
  figures for performers, up to five for some IATSE locals) plus ongoing dues
  as a percentage of earnings. For someone at the bottom this is a real barrier.

### 7.5 Other protections

- **Residuals** (`11-money-accounting-and-participations.md` §4) — the reason a
  guild card is a long-term asset and not just a work permit.
- **Credit determination** — WGA arbitration (`02-…` §7), DGA credit rules, and
  the "one director" rule, which is why a replaced director is a formal
  procedure and not just a personnel change.
- **Director's cut** — the DGA-guaranteed period for the director to assemble
  their own version before the studio recuts (`07-postproduction.md` §1).
- **Safety and consent protocols** — safety meetings, firearms procedures,
  stunt and intimacy protocols, and the right to refuse unsafe work
  (`06-…` §8).

### 7.6 Low-budget agreements

**Non-union / low-budget agreements** exist at every guild (ultra-low-budget,
modified low-budget, moderate low-budget, and their equivalents), trading
reduced minimums for budget caps, day caps, and deferred payments. Broadly:
the lower the budget cap, the deeper the rate discount and the more the
deferral. This is how most independent films are legally made with union
talent, and it is why a well-known actor can appear in a $400K film without
anybody breaking a quote (§2.3).

The trap is that these agreements are **budget-capped**: a production that
raises more money, or overspends, can find itself outside the tier it signed
under and owing full scale retroactively.

## 8. Below-the-line careers

Crew careers work differently from talent careers. There is no breakout, no
quote in the star sense, and no public. There is a rate, a phone, and a
reputation that is entirely word-of-mouth.

### 8.1 The ladder, with real timescales

Progression is a **ladder within a department**, and the rungs are specific:

| Department | Ladder | Typical years per rung |
|---|---|---|
| Camera | Trainee → 2nd AC (clapper/loader) → 1st AC (focus puller) → operator → DP | 2–4 per rung; 10–20 years trainee to DP is normal |
| Directing/AD | Set PA → 3rd AD → 2nd AD → 1st AD → UPM/line producer/producer | 8–15 years PA to 1st AD |
| Grip/Electric | Day-player → best boy → key grip / gaffer | 5–12 years |
| Art | Draughtsperson/art dept assistant → set designer → art director → supervising art director → production designer | 12–20 years |
| Editorial | Post PA → assistant editor → additional editor → editor | 6–15 years, and the assistant-to-editor jump is the hardest in the industry |
| Costume | Trainee → set costumer → supervisor → assistant designer → costume designer | 10–18 years |
| Sound | Utility/2nd → boom operator → production sound mixer | 5–12 years |

**How a promotion actually happens** is rarely a formal event. The three real
mechanisms:

1. **The gap.** Someone above leaves, gets sick, gets a better job, or is
   fired mid-show, and the next person down steps up for a day, a week, or the
   rest of the picture. If it goes well, they never step back down.
2. **The sponsor.** A department head takes their number two up with them onto
   a bigger show, or recommends them into a job the sponsor turned down. Most
   careers are a chain of these.
3. **The smaller show.** Taking a step up on a much smaller production —
   operating on a music video, gaffing a short — to acquire the credit, then
   converting it. The pay is worse and it is the standard route.

The second-hardest thing about the ladder is that **going up means going
backwards in employability**: a brilliant, constantly employed 1st AC who
starts calling themselves an operator is now an unproven operator competing
with proven ones, and may work far less for two years. Many people never make
the jump for exactly this reason, and it is a rational choice.

### 8.2 How union entry actually works

The gate, and the paradox that defines it.

- **Days-bank / roster systems.** US IATSE locals typically require a candidate
  to accumulate a specified number of days in a classification on qualifying
  productions before they can join or be placed on the roster — in Los Angeles
  this is administered as an industry experience roster with a days threshold
  (commonly on the order of 30–60 days for entry classifications), plus safety
  training and, in some locals, a skills examination.
- **The paradox.** Qualifying days come from union productions, and union
  productions hire from the roster. The gap is bridged by non-union work: low
  budget features, commercials, music videos, documentary, corporate, and
  work outside the local's zone — which is why the first three to five years of
  a crew career are spent on productions nobody will ever see.
- **Organising.** The other route in is a non-union show being organised
  mid-production, at which point the working crew is brought in as a group.
  This is a real and significant path.
- **Cost.** Initiation fees run from around a thousand dollars to well into
  five figures for the most restricted classifications, plus quarterly dues and
  working dues as a percentage of earnings.
- **UK and much of Europe differ fundamentally.** There is no closed shop;
  BECTU is a union but not a gatekeeper, so entry is purely relational —
  runner to trainee to assistant through personal recommendation, plus formal
  trainee and apprenticeship schemes run by broadcasters, the national film
  bodies, and the larger productions. The consequence is that UK entry is
  easier and progression is *slower and less formalised*, and networks matter
  even more than in the US.

### 8.3 What reputation actually consists of for crew

It is not a rating. It is a small set of specific claims that circulate
verbally, and they are startlingly consistent across departments:

**Positive:**

- *Fast* — gets the shot before the light goes.
- *Never blows a day* — has never been the reason a day was lost.
- *Good on nights* — functions at 4am on the sixth night.
- *Great with actors* — the makeup artist or focus puller a nervous lead trusts.
- *Keeps their department calm* — the head whose team does not generate drama.
- *Brings a good crew* — the recommendations they make are reliable.
- *Answers the phone* — available, decisive, gives a straight yes or no.
- *Solves it* — presents the UPM with a solution and a price, not a problem.
- *Safe* — nothing bad has ever happened on their watch.

**Negative, and equally specific:**

- *Slow.* *Precious.* *Argues with the 1st.* *Complains on the radio.*
- *Costs me money* — over-orders, over-crews, over-builds.
- *Won't work with X.* *Drinks.* *Difficult with young crew.*
- *Someone got hurt.*

The asymmetry matters: a good reputation accumulates slowly across many jobs;
a bad one attaches from a single named incident and is close to permanent.

### 8.4 How reputation travels

The mechanism is a **three-minute phone call**, and understanding it explains
almost everything about below-the-line hiring:

1. A UPM or line producer needs a gaffer for a show starting in five weeks.
2. They ask the DP, who names two or three people they've worked with.
3. The UPM calls a UPM who has used that gaffer, and asks three questions: did
   they hold the schedule, did they hold the budget, and would you use them
   again. Nobody asks about artistry; that is the DP's problem.
4. One equivocal answer removes the candidate. There is no appeal and the
   candidate never learns it happened.

Supporting infrastructure: crew lists from previous shows circulate and are
effectively a hiring database; call sheets and the crawl are the permanent
record of who was on what; "who brought you in" is a durable tag that follows
people for years. Credits databases matter far less below the line than above
it, because the industry's own memory is better than any database and much more
opinionated.

### 8.5 The economics of a crew career

- **Income = day rate × days worked**, and days worked is the variable that
  actually determines a year. A well-employed crew member targets roughly
  **150–220 paid days a year**; below about 100 the career is in trouble
  regardless of rate.
- **Kit rental** is a real component — a sound mixer, a hair and makeup artist,
  or a DP with their own package can add 20–50% to their effective income
  through equipment rental billed to the production.
- **The rate/days trade-off is constant.** A lower rate on a 16-week show beats
  a higher rate on a 3-week one, and a lower rate on a show that will lead to
  the next one beats both.
- **Distant location work** pays per diem and housing on top, so it banks
  better — at the cost of being away, which is the primary attrition mechanism
  for crew with families.
- **The employment is intermittent by design.** A working crew member is
  looking for the next job while finishing this one, and rate, location, and
  length of engagement compete with each other. Turning down a long job for a
  possible better one is the recurring gamble of the career.
- **Geography follows incentives** (`03-…` §6). When a state or country's
  rebate changes, the work moves, and crews either move with it, commute, or
  lose the year. This has ended more careers than any individual failure.

### 8.6 The head-of-department transition

The hardest transition in the below-the-line world is not technical. Becoming
an HOD means the job changes from *doing the craft* to:

- **Budgeting** a department and defending the number to the UPM.
- **Hiring** — the department's output is now the quality of the people picked.
- **Personnel** — managing 5–60 people, their morale, their hours, and their
  conflicts.
- **Politics** — negotiating with other departments for time and space on the
  floor, and with the director for what is actually wanted.
- **Owning the failure** — the HOD is who gets the call when it goes wrong,
  including when it was someone else's mistake.

Many superb technicians are poor department heads, and the industry knows it;
this is why "great operator, shouldn't be a DP" is a thing people say.

### 8.7 What ends a crew career

| Cause | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **The body** | Grips, camera crew, and costume standers-by do heavy physical work in 12-hour days for decades. Backs, knees, and shoulders end careers in the fifties |
| **A safety incident** | Being named as the cause of an injury is close to terminal, whatever the finding |
| **A run of no work** | Two thin years, savings gone, and a job outside the industry that becomes permanent. The most common ending by far, and entirely undramatic |
| **Geography** | The work moves to another state or country and the person cannot follow |
| **Technology** | Whole classifications have been obsoleted — film loaders, negative cutters, several photochemical and optical grades — and the transition window is short |
| **Reputation** | One well-circulated story; see §8.3 |
| **Addiction** | An occupational hazard of a job with extreme hours and long idle periods, and a known and frequently fatal career ender |
| **Family** | The travel and the hours are incompatible with many lives, and the exit is usually voluntary and permanent |
| **Promotion into producing** | The happy version: 1st ADs become UPMs and producers, and leave the floor deliberately |

**Departmental identity is strong** and crossing between departments is rare
after the first few years — the ladders are separate, the union
classifications are separate, and a person's reputation is held by their own
department's grapevine, which does not transfer.

## 9. Producers, executives, agents, and writers

Four careers with completely different currencies, all of them longer than an
actor's and none of them public.

### 9.1 Producers

**The ladder.** Two routes that rarely cross. The **creative** route: agency or
studio assistant → creative executive at a production company → director of
development → VP → producer with a shingle. The **physical** route: production
assistant → production coordinator/POC → production manager → UPM → line
producer → producer. Creative producers find and shape material; physical
producers deliver films. The industry needs both, pays them differently, and
credits them confusingly (`05-…` §18).

**The economics.** A producer fee on a studio film runs roughly **2–5% of the
budget**, usually capped, and is paid across prep, shoot, and delivery.
Backend is a net or adjusted-gross participation that frequently pays nothing
(`11-…` §2). A producer with an **overall deal** gets an office, a development
executive or two, and an annual fund, in exchange for first look — and lives in
constant awareness that the deal is renewed on the strength of what got made.

**The currency:** material and relationships. Specifically — the ability to
attract a writer's next spec, the ability to get a director on the phone, the
ability to make a studio believe a package is real, and a track record of
films that were actually delivered.

**What separates a good producer from a bad one:**

1. **Taste in material**, judged years before anyone else can check it.
2. **Casting the film's key personnel** — director, writer, DP, editor. A
   producer's real signature is who they hire.
3. **Closing.** Turning "everyone likes it" into a signed, financed, dated
   film. Most projects die here, and the difference between producers is almost
   entirely their conversion rate at this step.
4. **Two-way protection** — shielding the director from the studio and the
   studio from the director, which requires being trusted by both and is why
   producers who are purely one side's advocate are ineffective.
5. **Telling the truth about money.** A producer who reports an overage early
   keeps working; one who conceals it until it's unrecoverable does not.
6. **Being in the room when it matters** — the note session, the recut, the
   date conversation.
7. **Knowing which fight to lose.**

**How producers fail:** a slate that never converts; one film and then nothing;
a project taken away in turnaround (`02-…` §9); losing the overall deal when
the executive who signed it leaves; being blamed for an overrun; and the most
common — being a producer of record with no actual function, a position the
credit system generates in quantity.

### 9.2 Studio executives

**The ladder.** Assistant → creative executive/story editor → director of
development → VP → SVP → EVP → President of Production → Chairman. Progress is
fast at the bottom and murderous at the top; the pyramid narrows to a handful
of jobs per studio.

**The week.** Reading (scripts, coverage, books, articles), taking pitches,
giving notes (`02-…` §6), tracking what every rival is developing, shepherding
projects through internal approval, sitting in on casting and dailies, and
writing the greenlight memo that puts their name on a $100M decision.

**The currency:** relationships plus a hit they can plausibly claim. Executives
are hired for taste and relationships and fired on regime change. A new head of
production frequently abandons the predecessor's slate, because inheriting
someone else's films means owning their failures and sharing their successes.

**What separates a good executive from a bad one:**

1. **Notes that make the film better** rather than notes that demonstrate
   engagement. The industry can tell the difference immediately.
2. **Saying no quickly and clearly.** A fast pass is a gift; a slow maybe
   costs a filmmaker a year.
3. **Championing internally** — the executive's actual product is the argument
   that gets a film through the greenlight committee.
4. **Knowing what their boss will approve**, which is a distinct skill from
   knowing what is good and is at least as career-determining.
5. **Protecting a filmmaker from the process** without lying to either side.
6. **Being right in public and wrong in private** — building a record of
   advocated films that worked.

**The exit.** The structural feature outsiders find hardest to believe: a
departing senior executive is frequently given a **producing deal** at the same
studio, with an office and a fund. This is not sentiment; it retains their
relationships and buys their silence and goodwill. It is why the executive and
producer populations are the same people at different stages.

### 9.3 Agents

**The ladder.** Mailroom → agent's desk (assistant) → agent trainee/floater →
junior agent → agent → department head → partner. The mailroom-and-desk system
is deliberately brutal, poorly paid, and 2–4 years long; its function is to
teach the business by exposure and to filter for tolerance.

**The economics.** An agent's value to the agency is the commission their
clients generate. A desk (agent plus assistant plus overhead) has to be covered
by a book of business, which is why client lists are pruned and why an agent
whose top client leaves is immediately in danger.

**The job:** covering (owning the relationship with a set of buyers),
servicing, signing, and packaging (`01-…` §5.1). The skill axis is in §1.3.

**How agents fail:** a static list — nobody signed in two years; losing an
anchor client, which can end a career at that level outright; moving agencies
and discovering the clients don't follow; being wrong publicly about a client's
value; and burnout, which is endemic.

### 9.4 Writers

**The ladder.** Spec sale, contest, fellowship, or assistant job → first
assignment → open writing assignments (OWAs) → rewrites and polishes →
producing or showrunning. Notably, this is the one above-the-line career where
a person can move *up* in income while moving *down* in credit: uncredited
rewrite and polish work is lucrative, invisible, and a large share of many
working writers' income.

**The economics.** WGA minimums by budget tier set the floor; quotes work the
same way as an actor's (§2). Assignments are **step deals** — a payment per
step, with the studio holding a cut-off after each (`02-…` §4) — which means a
writer's contracted number and their realised income diverge sharply. Residuals
accumulate over decades and function as an annuity, which is why writers who
have not had a produced credit in years are still receiving cheques.

**The career shape.** Writers are the most durable and the most replaceable at
once. Being fired from your own script is normal, common, and not a career
event; large films routinely pass through 5–15 writers, with credit finally
determined by WGA arbitration (`02-…` §7) rather than by chronology or volume.
A writer with one produced hit can work on assignments for decades.

**What separates a good screenwriter from a bad one — professionally, not
artistically:**

1. **Structure.** The ability to make a story work at the level of what happens
   and in what order, which is the thing rewrites are hired to fix.
2. **Speed and reliability.** Delivering on the date is worth an extraordinary
   amount, because a script date gates a start date.
3. **Taking notes** — extracting the real problem from a badly expressed note
   and solving it without capitulating on everything else (`02-…` §6).
4. **Pitching.** A large share of assignments are won in a room, verbally.
5. **Being good in the room** — collaborative, specific, not defensive. This
   determines re-hiring more than the pages do.
6. **Range in register** — the writer who can do the action beats *and* the
   emotional scene gets the whole assignment.

**How writers fail:** development purgatory (years of paid work, nothing
produced, no credits accumulating); a reputation for lateness, which is fatal;
being known for one voice in a market that has moved; and the structural
attrition of a career with no visible face and no direct relationship with an
audience.

### 9.5 The other above-the-line careers, briefly

- **Casting directors** — hired per film on a flat fee (`04-…` §8), build
  careers on discoveries and on relationships with agents, and have the
  narrowest ladder in the business: associate → CD, with no further rung.
  Increasingly the route into producing for some.
- **Composers** — represented, quoted, and hired on relationships with
  directors above all; a composer's career is a handful of director
  relationships plus a library of prior work that gets temp-tracked into other
  people's films.
- **Editors** — a career built almost entirely on repeat collaboration (§5),
  with the assistant-to-editor jump as the bottleneck, and a late-career
  advantage because the job is not physical.
- **Cinematographers** — the one below-the-line craft with something
  approaching star value: a DP's name attracts crew, reassures financiers on a
  first-time director's film, and appears in the trades.

## 10. What a simulation most often gets wrong

1. **Treating a talent's value as a single scalar.** Real bankability is
   genre-, territory-, and role-specific (§3.2).
2. **Ignoring availability.** The best person for the job is usually working.
3. **Ignoring the ratchet.** Quotes go up easily and come down slowly, and
   talent will restructure a deal rather than break a quote.
4. **Ignoring relationships.** Who someone will work with, and for whom, is not
   a function of money.
5. **Making reputation instantaneous.** It lags performance by a film or two,
   because deals are signed long before results are known.
6. **Forgetting the crew.** Below-the-line availability and quality is a real
   constraint that determines what a production can attempt.
7. **Ignoring attribution.** A failure only damages the people it is blamed
   on, and blame is assigned by narrative rather than by evidence (§4.4).
8. **Treating heat as a permanent stat rather than a depleting asset** that is
   spent whether or not it is used (§4.2).
9. **Modelling representation as a fee rather than as an actor in the system.**
   Agents create opportunities, hold calendars, and refuse deals.
10. **Assuming behaviour is cosmetic.** "Good on set" is priced by producers
    and bond companies and materially changes who gets hired (§11.6).

## 11. What actors actually do — the craft

Everything above is about the market. This section is about the job, which the
market only imperfectly rewards.

**Who** — the performer, working with the **director** (the only person whose
notes they must take), the **DP** (frame and lens, which dictate scale), the
**1st AD** (whose clock they are inside), **hair, makeup and costume** (who
have them for hours a day and often know them best), the **script supervisor**
(who holds continuity of action and eyeline), and **dialect, movement, fight,
intimacy, and dialogue coaches** as the role requires.

**When** — preparation begins on signature, intensifies 4–12 weeks out,
peaks in the two weeks before the first day, and then the actual work is
distributed across a shoot in which the actor is on set for perhaps 40–70% of
the days and working for a fraction of the hours they are present.

### 11.1 Preparation

What the weeks before a shoot actually contain:

| Work | Typical timing | What it involves |
|---|---|---|
| **Script analysis** | From signature | Breaking the script into scenes and beats and answering, per scene: what does the character want, what is in the way, what am I *doing* to get it. The last question is the operative one — playable actions, not adjectives |
| **The through-line** | 4–8 weeks out | Mapping the character's state at every point in the story, because the film will be shot out of order and nobody else will track it for them |
| **Research** | 4–12 weeks out | Reading, interviews with people who do the job, spending time in the world. Its function is to generate specific behaviour, not information |
| **Physical transformation** | 8–24 weeks out | Weight gain or loss, training. Requires production scheduling support, a trainer, and sometimes medical supervision; a schedule change can invalidate months of it |
| **Skills** | 4–12 weeks out | Riding, driving, fighting, dancing, instruments, weapons handling, surgery, sign language. Usually 3–8 weeks of several sessions a week, and the production pays |
| **Dialect** | 6–12 weeks out, continuing on set | With a coach, who then stays through the shoot to catch drift in the moment |
| **Rehearsal** | 1–3 weeks before, if at all | Increasingly rare on studio films; more common in the UK and on character-driven work. Often replaced by a single sit-down with the director |
| **Fittings and camera tests** | 2–6 weeks out | Costume, hair, makeup, prosthetics. Where the character's exterior gets decided — and often where the actor first physically finds them |
| **Lines** | Continuously, and the night before | Memorisation is assumed, unremarked, and entirely the actor's own problem |

The governing rule is **the homework is done at home**. Set time is the most
expensive time in the industry (`06-…` §1); an actor arriving without their
choices made is spending it.

### 11.2 The shooting day, from the actor's side

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Call** | Often 5–7am. Pick-up, then straight into the trailer |
| **Hair, makeup, costume** | 45 minutes to 4+ hours depending on prosthetics. The most time the actor spends with anyone all day |
| **Blocking rehearsal** | The first rehearsal on set, with the director, DP, 1st AD, script supervisor and key crew watching. Positions and moves are agreed; the crew watches to learn what they must light and shoot |
| **Marks** | Camera and floor mark positions taped. From here the actor's movement is precise, repeatable, and non-negotiable |
| **Stand-ins** | The actor leaves; stand-ins hold positions for 30–120 minutes of lighting |
| **First team** | The actor returns, warm, and is expected to be *at the same place* they were at blocking, emotionally |
| **Rehearsal for camera** | One or two, for focus, camera moves, and sound |
| **Takes** | 2–15 per setup. The take that works may be the second or the eleventh |
| **Coverage** | The same scene again from each new angle (`06-…` §4), 3–8 times, over hours. The performance must match on action and eyeline while staying alive |
| **Off-camera** | The actor plays the scene again, out of frame, for the other actor's coverage. Nothing they do is recorded and it matters enormously |
| **Wrap** | Costume out, makeup off. Then lines for tomorrow |

The structural fact that defines screen acting: **the performance is delivered
in fragments of 20–90 seconds, out of order, across weeks, with an hour of
technical work between each, and assembled later by someone else.**

### 11.3 What separates a great screen performance from a bad one

The skill axis. Almost none of it is about emotional intensity.

1. **Behaving rather than indicating.** The camera reads thought. An actor who
   *shows* the emotion — a face arranged into sadness — reads as false at any
   distance under a medium shot. The great ones do the thing and let the lens
   find it. This is the single most common failure and the hardest to fix.
2. **Listening.** Most of screen acting is being acted upon. The reaction shot
   is where a film's emotion actually lives, and an actor who is waiting for
   their line rather than hearing the other one is visibly dead in the cut.
3. **Scale calibration.** The correct size of a performance is a function of
   lens and frame. What plays in a wide two-shot is grotesque in a 100mm
   close-up. Great screen actors adjust without being told, having clocked the
   lens on the way in.
4. **Repeatability with life.** Hitting the same mark, the same gesture on the
   same word, the same eyeline, take after take — so the material cuts — while
   the performance stays spontaneous. Doing either alone is easy; both is the
   job.
5. **Specificity.** A specific choice reads and a general one does not. "Angry"
   is unplayable; "trying to leave the room without letting him see my hands
   shake" is a performance.
6. **Taking an adjustment.** The director asks for something different — faster,
   colder, as if you already know — and the actor plays it *immediately*, fully,
   without arguing and without abandoning what was working. Casting directors
   test for exactly this in the room (`04-…` §8.1) because it predicts whether
   the shooting day will function.
7. **Holding the through-line unaided.** On day 31 of an out-of-order schedule,
   knowing precisely how much the character knows in this scene, and playing
   the right amount of it. The script supervisor tracks continuity of
   *action*; nobody but the actor tracks continuity of *interior state*.
8. **Giving the editor options.** Playing the beat two or three legitimately
   different ways across takes, so the cut has choices. Actors who deliver one
   fixed reading are harder to edit and are quietly less valued in post.
9. **Stamina and consistency.** The fourteenth hour of the sixth night. The
   ability to deliver the same quality at 4am that was delivered at 9am is not
   a temperament trait; it is the difference between a usable day and a lost one.
10. **Generosity off camera.** Giving a full performance for someone else's
    coverage. It improves the film, it is noticed by everyone on the floor, and
    it is the single most reliable driver of an actor's reputation among
    directors.
11. **Technical literacy.** Knowing where the light is, not crossing an eyeline,
    not shadowing another actor, knowing when the camera is moving and how fast.
    A performance that cannot be shot is not a performance.

### 11.4 Screen versus stage

They are different crafts with a shared vocabulary, and the transition in
either direction is genuinely hard.

| | **Stage** | **Screen** |
|---|---|---|
| **Size** | Calibrated to the back row | Calibrated to the lens; often almost nothing |
| **Chronology** | The story in order, every night | Out of order, weeks apart |
| **Continuity** | Whole arc in one sitting | Fragments; the arc exists only in the actor's head and the edit |
| **Rehearsal** | 3–6 weeks, standard | Often none |
| **Feedback** | A live audience, immediately | A monitor, a director, and silence |
| **Ownership** | The actor controls the performance nightly | The editor controls it; the actor supplies material |
| **Repetition** | Same performance, 8 shows a week, improving | Same 40 seconds, 12 times in a row, then never again |
| **Second chance** | Tomorrow night | The cut is permanent |
| **Physical demand** | Vocal and sustained | Waiting, then precision under time pressure |

Stage actors moving to screen typically over-play and struggle with the
stop-start rhythm. Screen actors moving to stage typically under-project and
are unprepared for the sheer sustained physical demand of eight shows a week.

### 11.5 How an actor works with a director

The relationship is not what it looks like from outside. On a studio film,
most direction of actors is **thirty seconds, quietly, between takes**, because
the director has a crew of 150 and a clock.

**Before the shoot:** one or more conversations about who the character is and
what the film is doing with them. This is where the real directing of the
performance happens, and it is the conversation an actor should insist on.

**On the floor:** the vocabulary that works is **actions and adjustments** —
"this time you're trying to reassure her", "she already knows, play it after
that" — because they are playable. The vocabulary that does not work is
**result direction** — "be sadder", "more energy", "funnier" — which tells the
actor the destination and nothing about the route, and which reliably produces
indicating (§11.3).

Other mechanics that matter:

- **Private notes.** A note given in front of sixty crew is a different note.
  Good directors walk the actor away. Bad ones give the note across the set,
  over the radio, or through the 1st AD.
- **Protection.** The director's most valuable gift is a room where the actor
  can be bad for a take. That means managing the crew's impatience, the clock,
  and the video village audience — including, sometimes, clearing it.
- **Knowing when they have it.** An actor doing take 14 of something that
  worked on take 3 loses confidence in the director and then in the scene. The
  reverse — moving on before it is there — is worse and is unfixable in post.
- **Asking.** A professional actor asks precisely: "where am I coming from?",
  "how much does she know?", "do you want the whole thing or just the end of
  it?" — and can work without an answer if the director doesn't have one.
- **Lines that shouldn't be crossed.** An actor does not direct other actors,
  does not give notes on someone else's performance, and takes disagreements to
  the director rather than to the studio. Breaching any of these is a
  reputational event.

### 11.6 What "good on set" actually means

This is behavioural, it is priced, and it is reported. The 1st AD, the UPM, and
the line producer all form a view within a week, and they talk to their peers
(§8.4). Concretely, a "good" actor:

- Knows the lines, including the rewrite that arrived last night.
- Is on time and out of makeup on schedule — because the whole unit's day is
  built around it.
- Hits marks, so the focus puller can do their job.
- Doesn't hold the crew — is at the door when first team is called.
- Doesn't renegotiate on the day.
- Is the same person in hour twelve as in hour one.
- Treats the crew as colleagues and learns their names.
- Is honest about what they can't do — the stunt, the accent, the horse — early
  enough for the production to solve it.
- Gives the off-camera performance (§11.3).
- Turns up for ADR, reshoots, and the contracted press days without a fight.

The commercial consequence is direct: reliability is a component of bankability
(§3.3), it is priced by completion bond companies (`03-…` §7), and among
producers it outweighs a surprising amount of talent. The industry's phrase for
the alternative — "not worth the trouble" — is a hiring decision, not a
complaint.

### 11.7 What a bad performance costs

| Failure | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **Miscast lead** | Unfixable. No editing, scoring, or reshooting repairs it (`04-…` §8.3) |
| **Indicating** | Every close-up is unusable; the film retreats to wides and the intimacy is lost |
| **No continuity of state** | The character's arc is incoherent in the cut, and post spends weeks papering over it |
| **Poor matching across coverage** | Whole angles are unusable; the editor is locked into one master and the scene is flat |
| **One reading only** | No options in the edit; a scene that could be tuned is fixed |
| **Line trouble** | Takes multiply; a day's page count is missed and the schedule pays for it (`06-…` §5) |
| **Can't take an adjustment** | The director stops directing them, which is the quietest and most damaging outcome of all |
| **Behavioural problems** | Days lost, crew morale, a report to the bond company, and no re-hire |
| **Refusing ADR or press** | The film is delivered late or marketed without its lead (`09-…` §12.2) |

## 12. Directors as a career and a craft

The director is the only person present for the whole film, and the job is
different in each of its three phases.

**Who they work with** — in development, the writer, producer, and executives;
in prep, every HOD plus the CD, 1st AD, and UPM; on the floor, the DP, 1st AD,
and cast above all; in post, the editor, then sound, music, and VFX
supervisors; at release, marketing and publicity. **Who they answer to** — the
producer and the financier, with the extent of that authority set entirely by
the deal (§12.7).

### 12.1 What a director actually does, phase by phase

| Phase | Duration | What the job is |
|---|---|---|
| **Development** | 6 months–5 years | Finding or being offered material; developing a **take** (a specific, arguable interpretation) and selling it in a room; working with the writer through drafts; assembling attachments to make the film financeable (`02-…`, `03-…` §2) |
| **Prep** | 8–16 weeks (studio), 4–8 (low budget) | Hiring HODs; casting (`04-…` §8); location scouting and the tech scout (`04-…` §5.2); design approvals — sets, costumes, palette, props; the shot plan, storyboards, previs; agreeing the schedule with the 1st AD; a rolling stream of several hundred decisions a week, most of them small and unglamorous |
| **Photography** | 25–100+ days | Per day: block the scene, agree the coverage, direct the performances, decide when it's there, decide what to cut when the day runs short, watch dailies (`06-…` §9), and answer questions continuously |
| **Post** | 6–18 months | The assembly; the **director's cut** (DGA-guaranteed, commonly around 10 weeks on a feature); the studio cut; test screenings (`07-…` §9); reshoots; sound, music, VFX and the DI; delivery (`07-…`) |
| **Release** | 2–6 months | Press, festivals, awards campaigning (`13-…` §5), and the long public argument about what the film is |

### 12.2 What the job actually is

Two things, under everything else:

1. **Answering questions.** Several hundred a day in prep and on the floor:
   which watch, which door, how fast does she walk in, do we see the body, is
   the wall blue. Most are individually trivial; collectively they *are* the
   film. The director's throughput on this is a real constraint and is the main
   thing a regular team relieves (§5.2).
2. **Holding the intent stable** across 18 months, 300 people, and thousands of
   compromises, so that the thing delivered is recognisably the thing intended.
   Nobody else in the process has this job, and no document can do it.

### 12.3 What separates a great director from a competent one

1. **Knowing what the film is** — in one sentence, and being able to apply that
   sentence to a costume swatch, a lens choice, and a line reading. A director
   who cannot answer "what is this film" produces a set where 300 people each
   make a reasonable guess.
2. **Casting.** The single largest lever available, and mostly what a
   director's "taste" turns out to be in practice (`04-…` §8). A great cast
   forgives an enormous amount; a miscast lead forgives nothing.
3. **Reading a performance in the moment** — knowing when they have it. This
   is what keeps the day and is the difference between finishing at 7pm and
   losing the last two setups.
4. **Coverage judgement.** Deciding what to shoot and, crucially, what not to.
   Over-covering is how a schedule is lost; under-covering is how a scene
   becomes uncuttable. Both errors are made in the same five-minute decision.
5. **Shooting for the cut.** Visualising the assembled scene while standing on
   a set and shooting only what it needs. Directors who cannot do this shoot
   beautiful, unusable material.
6. **Treating the clock as a creative instrument.** The day has ten hours in
   it; choosing to spend four on the scene that matters and forty minutes on
   the one that doesn't is a taste decision disguised as a logistics decision.
7. **Leading.** The tone of a set comes from the director and nowhere else. A
   calm set is faster, safer, and more inventive; a frightened one produces
   people who stop volunteering solutions, which is a measurable loss.
8. **Working with actors** — objectives and adjustments rather than results,
   and protection (§11.5).
9. **Collaborating without abdicating.** Getting the best out of a great DP or
   designer while still making the final call, and doing it without the
   department feeling overruled.
10. **Surviving post.** The self-honesty to recut their own work, the
    resilience to sit through a bad test screening (`07-…` §9) and separate the
    useful signal from the noise, and the stamina for a year of it.
11. **Choosing which battles to fight** with the studio, and losing the others
    gracefully enough to still be there for the ones that matter.
12. **Choosing the next project.** Over a career this outweighs everything
    else on this list.

### 12.4 How directorial reputation is built

The ladder is real and quite rigid, because financiers are underwriting risk:

```
Shorts / commercials / music videos / TV episodes
   └─► first feature (micro-budget or festival-financed)
         └─► festival premiere = the calling card
               └─► second feature ($5–20M, specialty or studio-adjacent)
                     └─► $30–80M studio film
                           └─► $100M+ tentpole
```

Mechanics worth naming:

- **The calling card.** A first feature's function is not to make money; it is
  to prove a director can finish something with a point of view. A festival
  premiere puts it in front of exactly the people who decide.
- **The second-film problem.** The hardest film in a career. The first was made
  on nothing with total freedom; the second has money, notes, and expectations,
  and the failure rate is high and well known.
- **The take.** The literal mechanism by which directors are hired for material
  they didn't originate: a meeting, and often a written or visual document — a
  **take document** or **lookbook** — describing what *this* director's version
  of the film would be. Studios meet 5–15 directors this way for a big
  assignment.
- **The commercials and episodic route.** Directing commercials or streaming
  episodes is a paid apprenticeship in scale and speed, and studios read it as
  evidence a director can handle a unit — increasingly the standard bridge to a
  big budget.
- **Parallel ladders.** The commercial ladder buys budget. The **prestige**
  ladder — awards standing, critical reputation, festival standing — buys
  *control*: final cut, casting approval, and the freedom to make an odd film.
  They are only loosely correlated, and directors choose which to climb.
- **The step-down.** A director who has made a $150M film can usually still get
  a $10M one made. The ladder is easier to descend than to ascend, and doing so
  deliberately after a failure is a standard recovery move.

### 12.5 How directorial reputation is lost

The order here is not what outsiders expect. **Cost, not quality, is the
reliable career killer.**

| Cause | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Overrun blamed on the director** | The bond company and the studio both keep records. A director who has cost a picture days is a specific, quantified risk on the next finance plan (`03-…` §7). This ends more careers than any flop |
| **Two attributable flops** | The financing conversation stops. "Development" becomes indefinite — a decade is not unusual |
| **Being fired** | Rare, and near-permanent in effect. The DGA one-director rule makes it a formal, public event |
| **The film taken away and recut** | Damaging even when the recut version fails, because the perception is that the director couldn't deliver |
| **A safety incident** | The most serious of all, with legal as well as career consequences |
| **Undeliverable rating** | A director who cannot or will not deliver the contracted rating (`07-…` §7) breaches the deal outright |
| **Uninsurable** | Health, conduct, or claims history; ends the career at the level where bonds are required |
| **A reputation for slowness** | Whispered, unprovable, permanent. Line producers are asked about it on every hiring call |
| **Nothing at all** | The most common: projects fall apart repeatedly, no film gets made, and the career ends without an event |

### 12.6 What good and bad look like on the floor

**Good:** the day's work is finished; the crew knows what the film is and
volunteers ideas that are actually useful; the actors are protected and are
doing their best work; the coverage cuts; the department heads are arguing
about the right things; and when something goes wrong the director changes the
plan without changing the intent.

**Bad:**

| Failure | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **No clear intent** | Every department guesses. The film is internally inconsistent and no cut fixes it |
| **Indecision on the floor** | The unit waits. 150 people at full rate, doing nothing (`06-…` §5) |
| **Over-covering** | Setups multiply, the day is lost, and the last scenes of the day are shot badly |
| **Under-covering** | The scene cannot be cut; reshoots or a compromised edit |
| **Directing by monitor only** | The actors are alone. Performances flatten and the set's centre of gravity moves to video village |
| **Result direction** | Indicated performances (§11.3) |
| **Losing the crew** | Ideas stop coming; people do exactly what is asked and nothing more, which on a film is a serious loss |
| **Fighting every note** | The studio stops arguing and starts planning the recut |
| **Not watching the clock** | Overtime, turnaround breaches, penalties, and a schedule that eats the third act (`06-…` §7) |

### 12.7 Authority is contractual, not natural

"The director's vision" is a term of art whose real content is written into the
deal, and the spectrum is wide:

- **Final cut** — a small number of filmmakers. The studio may argue; the
  director decides.
- **Director's cut, then studio cut** — the DGA baseline and by far the most
  common arrangement (`07-…` §1).
- **Approval rights** — over cast, key crew, marketing materials, the title.
  Negotiated individually and frequently traded away by a first-timer.
- **Preview and consultation only** — the weakest position: the director is
  shown things and heard.
- **Replaced** — the studio's ultimate remedy, constrained by the DGA's
  procedures and the one-director credit rule.

Which of these a director has is a function of their standing at the moment the
deal was signed — which, given §4.4's lag, is a function of results from two
films ago.

## 13. The working year

Careers are lived as calendars, and the calendar is the thing most often
omitted from an account of the industry.

### 13.1 An actor's year

- **Availability is the asset.** A lead on a studio film is committed for
  roughly: 4–8 weeks of prep and training, 8–16 weeks of photography, then a
  scattering of reshoot days 6–12 months later, ADR, and 5–15 contracted press
  days around release — a commitment spanning **18–24 months** from a shoot
  that lasted three.
- **Awards season eats a winter.** A lead on a contending film is effectively
  employed from October to March on campaigning (`13-…` §3), largely unpaid.
- **Casting seasons.** US television's pilot season historically concentrated
  hiring into January–April; streaming has spread it across the year without
  entirely dissolving it. Features cast continuously, gated by financing.
- **Gaps are the norm, not the exception.** Even busy actors work a few months
  a year. The unstructured time is the hard part of the job, and it is when
  the bad decisions get made.

### 13.2 A crew year

- **Seasonality.** Shooting concentrates by climate and by studio slate
  planning; in most markets there is a pronounced lull in the weeks after the
  new year and a crunch in spring and late summer.
- **The overlap problem.** Jobs are offered 2–8 weeks out and shows run 8–20
  weeks, so a crew member is almost always choosing between a firm offer now
  and a better possible offer later, with imperfect information about both.
- **Geography.** The work is where the incentives are (`03-…` §6), which means
  a career decision about whether to be local to one market, travel
  continuously, or relocate as the map changes.
- **The counted year.** 150–220 paid days is a good year; the number of days
  also determines health coverage qualification (§7.2), which makes the last
  few weeks of a qualifying period disproportionately important.

### 13.3 What everyone is doing when not working

The industry's invisible labour: reading, taking generals (meetings with no
specific job attached, which is how most future work is actually seeded),
maintaining the network, developing material, training, and — for a large
fraction of working actors and crew — doing something else for money. This is
not a footnote to the careers described above. For most of the people in them,
it is the majority of the year.
