# 12 — Talent, Labour & Careers

*How careers actually work.* Reputation, availability, quotes, and relationships
are the persistent state of the industry — the films are temporary, the people
are not.

> **Domain reference.** Real industry, not this game. See `README.md`.

---

## 1. The representation stack

| Party | Function | Typical fee |
|---|---|---|
| **Agent** | Procures employment, negotiates deals, works the market | 10% |
| **Manager** | Career strategy, development, producing, day-to-day counsel | 10–15% |
| **Entertainment lawyer** | Papers the deal, negotiates long-form | 5% or hourly |
| **Publicist** | Press, image, crisis, awards campaigning | $5K–$25K/month |
| **Business manager** | Money, tax, investments | ~5% |

A fully-represented star pays 25–30% off the top. This is why gross quotes and
net take-home differ so sharply, and why buyouts and box-office bonuses are
negotiated so hard.

---

## 2. The quote system

A talent's **quote** is what they were last paid for a comparable job. It is
the single most important number in any negotiation because it sets the floor.

- Quotes are **tracked informally across the industry** — business affairs
  departments and agencies all have a working knowledge of them.
- A quote **ratchets up** with success and is very hard to lower publicly.
  Rather than break a quote, talent will:
  - take a **favoured nations** deal (everyone in the film on the same terms);
  - take **scale plus backend** for a prestige project;
  - accept a **deferred** fee;
  - or take a "**passion project discount**" that is contractually structured
    to not count as a quote.
- Quotes are **role- and format-specific**: a streaming buyout quote and a
  theatrical quote-plus-points are different numbers.

Directional acting fees (studio feature, US):

| Tier | Fee |
|---|---|
| SAG scale day player | ~$1,200/day |
| Working actor, supporting role | $50K–$500K |
| Recognisable lead, mid-budget | $1M–$3M |
| A-list star | $10M–$20M+ |
| A-list with backend/bonuses | $20M–$100M+ realised on a hit |
| Streaming buyout for a star | Often above the theatrical quote, as compensation for no backend |

## 3. What actually makes a star bankable

Bankability is a market judgment, not a talent judgment. It is composed of:

1. **Opening record** — has their name previously converted awareness into
   opening weekend, *distinct* from the IP they were attached to.
2. **International value** — foreign distributors' estimates by territory.
   Some stars are worth far more abroad than at home.
3. **Genre fit** — bankability is genre-bound; an action star's name is worth
   little on a romantic comedy.
4. **Recency** — the market discounts fast. Two flops materially reduce a quote.
5. **Reliability** — insurable, professional, shows up, does the press.
6. **Awards standing** — raises prestige-project value and directorial access
   more than it raises commercial value.

The honest industry consensus of the last two decades: **IP outperforms stars**
for opening weekend on tentpoles, while stars still matter enormously for
mid-budget films, for international pre-sales, and for getting a film financed
at all.

## 4. The career arc

A rough but recognisable shape:

```
Unknown ─► Working actor ─► Breakout ─► Heat ─► Established ─► Plateau
                                          │                       │
                                          └─► Overexposure ──────►┘
                                                       │
                                          Reinvention ─┴─► Character actor /
                                                            elder statesman
```

Mechanics that drive it:

- **Heat** is temporary, roughly 12–36 months after a breakout, and it is
  when a career's leverage is highest — the moment to convert into a quote,
  a franchise, or a passion project.
- **Overexposure** — too many films, too similar, too fast.
- **Typecasting** — commercially useful and creatively fatal; deliberate
  counter-casting is the standard escape.
- **Reinvention** — a prestige director taking a chance on a fading star is one
  of the industry's most reliable career-restoring moves.
- **Ageing** — historically brutal and highly asymmetric by gender, a
  well-documented feature of role availability and pay.
- **Scandal and behaviour** — insurability, studio risk appetite, and public
  reaction; consequences vary enormously and unpredictably.

The same arc applies to **directors**, where the currency is not opening
weekend but *the ability to get the next film financed*: a critical success
buys a bigger budget, a commercial failure buys a smaller one, and two failures
in a row can mean a decade in development.

## 5. Relationships and repeat collaboration

The industry runs on repeat collaboration far more than outsiders expect:

- Directors bring **regular DPs, editors, composers, and production
  designers** — often for decades.
- Directors have **stock companies** of actors.
- Producers and studios sustain **filmmaker relationships** over multiple
  films, sometimes at a loss, to retain access.
- HODs bring their own **crews** (`05-departments-and-crew.md` §1).

Practically, this means **availability is relational**: a filmmaker can get a
crew that a first-timer with more money cannot, and a start-date slip loses a
whole trusted team to another film.

## 6. Contracts: what is actually negotiated

Beyond fee and dates:

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Pay-or-play** | Paid whether or not the film is made (`03-…` §2.1) |
| **Exclusivity / holds** | First and second position on the talent's calendar |
| **Start date / stop date** | Firm outer limits, often driven by another commitment |
| **Overage** | What happens if the shoot runs past the contracted days |
| **Approvals** | Director, co-star, cut, photograph, likeness, marketing materials, still approval (a genuine bottleneck on key art) |
| **Credit** | Position, size relative to title, paid ad requirements, shared cards |
| **Perks** | Travel class, entourage, security, accommodation, per diem, trailer specification, gym/trainer, chef |
| **Nudity/intimacy rider** | Specific, scene-by-scene consent, with an intimacy coordinator |
| **Publicity obligation** | Number of days for junkets, premieres, and press tours |
| **Sequel/option terms** | Escalating fees for pictures 2 and 3, negotiated up front |
| **Digital replica / AI consent** | Post-2023 SAG-AFTRA terms: informed consent and compensation for scanning and synthetic performance |

## 7. Guild minimums and protections

See `01-industry-structure.md` §6 for the bodies. The protections that most
shape day-to-day work:

- **Minimums (scale)** by budget tier and job.
- **Overtime, meal penalties, and turnaround** (`06-principal-photography.md` §7).
- **Health and pension contributions** — the fringes on every labour dollar, and
  the reason "working enough days to qualify for health insurance" is a real
  career pressure for crew and working actors.
- **Residuals** (`11-money-accounting-and-participations.md` §4).
- **Credit determination** — WGA arbitration, DGA credit rules, and the "one
  director" rule.
- **Safety and consent** protocols.

**Non-union / low-budget agreements** exist at every guild (ultra-low-budget,
modified low-budget, etc.), trading reduced minimums for budget caps and
deferred payments. This is how most independent films are legally made with
union talent.

## 8. Below-the-line careers

Crew careers work differently from talent careers:

- **Progression is a ladder within a department** — 2nd AC → 1st AC → operator
  → DP; trainee → 3rd AD → 2nd AD → 1st AD → UPM/producer; art department
  draughtsperson → art director → production designer.
- **Union entry** is gated by accumulated days on qualifying productions
  (a roster or days-bank system), which makes the first jobs the hardest.
- **Reputation is local and specific**: "good on nights", "great with actors",
  "fast", "never blows a day". Word travels fast in a small market.
- **Employment is intermittent** — a working crew member is looking for the
  next job while finishing this one, and rate, location, and length of
  engagement compete with each other.
- **Departmental identity is strong** and crossing between departments is rare.

---

## 9. Producers, executives, and the other careers

- **Producers** live or die on their **slate and relationships**; the currency
  is a track record of delivered films and the ability to attract material.
- **Executives** are hired for taste and relationships and fired on regime
  change; a new head of production frequently abandons the predecessor's slate
  (`02-development.md` §9).
- **Agents** are measured on client list and packaging; agencies poach clients
  and agents constantly.
- **Writers** have the least visible career and the most durable one: credits
  and residuals accumulate, and a writer with a produced hit can work for
  decades on assignments.

---

## 10. What a simulation most often gets wrong

1. **Treating a talent's value as a single scalar.** Real bankability is
   genre-, territory-, and role-specific.
2. **Ignoring availability.** The best person for the job is usually working.
3. **Ignoring the ratchet.** Quotes go up easily and come down slowly, and
   talent will restructure a deal rather than break a quote.
4. **Ignoring relationships.** Who someone will work with, and for whom, is not
   a function of money.
5. **Making reputation instantaneous.** It lags performance by a film or two,
   because deals are signed long before results are known.
6. **Forgetting the crew.** Below-the-line availability and quality is a real
   constraint that determines what a production can attempt.
