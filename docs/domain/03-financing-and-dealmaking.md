# 03 — Financing & Dealmaking

*Where the money comes from, what it costs, and what it buys.* The financing
structure of a film determines who controls the cut, who gets paid first, and
what the film must earn to be considered a success.

> **Domain reference.** Real industry, not this game. See `README.md`.

---

## 1. The two financing worlds

**Studio-financed.** The studio pays the negative cost from its own balance
sheet (possibly with a co-financier), distributes worldwide, and owns the film.
Simple, expensive, and the studio bears everything.

**Independently financed.** No single party has the money, so the producer
assembles it from four or five sources, each with its own security and
recoupment position. Every element must be locked before the first dollar is
drawn. This is where most of the complexity lives.

A typical independent capital stack:

```
┌─ Pre-sales (foreign territory MGs)          — 30–50%
├─ Tax incentive / rebate (soft money)        — 15–35%
├─ Gap or super-gap loan (against unsold)     — 10–20%
├─ Equity (private, fund, or company)         — 10–30%
└─ Deferrals (talent/crew paid later)         — 0–10%
```

All of it is usually **cash-flowed by a bank** against those contracts, and
the whole thing is guaranteed by a **completion bond** (§7).

---

## 2. Attachments and the package

An "attachment" is a commitment by a talent to the project. Attachments create
value; they also create constraints.

- **Director attachment** is the single biggest value step for a spec or IP
  project. A director with heat makes the package financeable.
- **Cast attachment** determines pre-sale value directly: foreign buyers bid
  against a **star's value in their territory**, tracked as an internal
  estimate per market.
- **Soft attachment** — interested, no deal. **Hard attachment** — a signed
  deal, possibly pay-or-play.
- **Elements** — the collective term for director + principal cast + writer.

**Letters of intent (LOIs)** are the intermediate instrument: non-binding but
sufficient to shop the package.

### 2.1 Pay-or-play

The talent is owed their fee whether the film happens or not, contingent only
on their own availability and non-breach. It is what talent demands to hold a
window in their schedule. Once a studio signs pay-or-play deals with a star and
director, it has converted development risk into a hard liability, and the
project acquires momentum that is more financial than creative.

**Holds and windows.** Talent availability is a calendar problem: a star will
"hold" dates (first position, second position). Losing a hold usually costs the
production a start date and can trigger cascading crew and location losses.

## 3. Talent deal structures

| Element | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Fixed fee / guarantee** | Cash compensation, usually paid over the shoot |
| **Quote** | The talent's most recent fee, which sets the floor for the next |
| **Backend / points** | Participation in the film's revenue — see `11-…` |
| **First-dollar gross** | Points from the first dollar of studio receipts; extremely rare now, historically the top-tier star deal |
| **Cash break / breakeven definition** | The negotiated point after which participations begin |
| **Box office bonus** | Fixed payments at defined gross thresholds — the modern replacement for first-dollar gross |
| **Buyout** | A lump sum replacing residuals/backend; standard on streaming films |
| **Perks / riders** | Travel, accommodation, security, trailer size, per diem, assistants, gym, chef — real money, negotiated in detail |
| **Approvals** | Cast, director, cut, still photo, likeness, marketing materials |
| **Credit** | Position, size, paid ad requirements — heavily negotiated |

Director deals additionally involve the **DGA director's cut** (a guaranteed
period to produce the director's own version before the studio recuts) and
frequently **final cut** for a small number of filmmakers.

## 4. Pre-sales and territory rights

The independent world's engine.

- A **sales agent** takes the package (script, director, cast, budget, start
  date) to **AFM / EFM / Marché du Film**.
- Foreign distributors buy the rights for their territory, committing to a
  **minimum guarantee (MG)** payable on delivery.
- The MG contract is then **discounted by a bank** — the producer borrows
  against it, at interest, to fund production.
- Buyers evaluate on **cast value in that territory**, genre, and budget. Action
  and horror travel; comedy notoriously does not.

**Estimates** are the sales agent's published low/high value per territory.
The whole system runs on them; a package is financeable when estimates cover
the budget with margin.

---

## 5. Negative pickup and acquisitions

- **Negative pickup** — a distributor contracts *in advance* to buy the finished
  film for a set price on delivery to spec. The producer borrows against that
  contract to make it. The distributor takes no production risk but must accept
  what is delivered (subject to a detailed delivery schedule).
- **Acquisition** — a completed film is bought after the fact, usually after a
  festival premiere (Sundance, Toronto, Cannes, Berlin, SXSW). Prices swing
  from nothing to eight figures on the strength of one screening.
- **Split rights** — separate deals for domestic vs international, or
  theatrical vs streaming.

## 6. Soft money: incentives and rebates

Government subsidy is now a primary driver of *where films shoot* and
frequently of *whether they get made*.

Common mechanisms:

- **Refundable tax credit** — a percentage of qualifying local spend returned
  in cash (or as a credit that can be sold to a local taxpayer at a discount).
- **Cash rebate** — paid on audited local spend.
- **Uplift** — additional percentage for shooting outside a capital, hiring
  local heads of department, or post-producing locally.
- **Co-production treaty** — the film qualifies as national in two countries,
  accessing both subsidy systems, in exchange for spend/personnel quotas.

Directional rates: **20–40%** of qualifying spend is the usual band, with
specific jurisdictions higher. Caps, per-project ceilings, annual pools, and
"cultural test" qualification (as in the UK) all apply.

Consequences to model:

- A production will relocate its story to chase a rebate, then dress the
  location to match the script.
- Incentives are usually paid **after** the spend and audit, so they must be
  **cash-flowed** (borrowed against) — costing interest.
- A jurisdiction's crew depth constrains how much can actually be spent there.

## 7. Completion bonds

A **completion guarantor** (a specialist insurer) guarantees to the financiers
that the film will be completed and delivered on schedule and to specification,
or that their money will be returned.

- Fee: roughly **1.5–3%** of the budget above-the-line + below-the-line, often
  with a rebate if not called upon.
- The bond company **approves the budget, schedule, key crew, and the
  contingency** before closing, and monitors daily cost reports and progress.
- If the production overruns badly, the bond company can **take over the film**,
  replace the director or producer, and cut the film to a deliverable state.
  This right is rarely exercised but its existence disciplines the production.
- Strike-out clauses: bonds typically exclude force majeure and certain
  cast-illness risks, which are covered separately.

Studio films are usually **self-bonded** — the studio's balance sheet is the
guarantee.

## 8. Insurance

Mandatory and non-trivial. Typical package:

- **Cast insurance** — covers loss from a principal's death/illness/injury.
  Requires medicals for key cast; a star with a health history may be
  uninsurable or excluded, which can kill a film.
- **Negative/faulty stock, equipment, props/sets/wardrobe, third-party
  property damage, general liability, workers' compensation.**
- **Errors & omissions (E&O)** — protects against copyright, defamation, and
  right-of-publicity claims. Distributors require it; it requires clean chain
  of title and clearances.
- **Weather / cast interruption / civil authority** cover.

Combined production insurance package: broadly **1–3%** of budget, rising with
stunt, water, aerial, animal, and pyrotechnic content. Higher risk content also
attracts higher workers' comp classifications.

## 9. Budget anatomy: above and below the line

The **line** is a real line on the budget top sheet, historically dividing
creative elements negotiated individually from technical costs quoted by rate.

**Above the line (ATL)** — story & rights, producers, director, principal cast,
and their fringes. Typically **20–40%** of a studio budget; can be far more when
a star has a big quote.

**Below the line (BTL)** — everything else in production: crew, equipment,
locations, sets, transportation, travel/living, extras, stunts, VFX unit
photography, catering.

**Post & other** — editorial, VFX (frequently the largest single line on a
tentpole), music, sound, DI, titles, deliverables.

**Other charges** — insurance, completion bond, legal, financing costs,
contingency, studio overhead.

A conventional top sheet:

```
ABOVE THE LINE
  Story & Rights ................  1–3%
  Producers .....................  3–7%
  Director ......................  3–6%
  Cast ..........................  10–25%
BELOW THE LINE (production)
  Production staff, camera, grip/electric, art,
  set construction, set dressing, props, costume,
  hair/makeup, sound, locations, transport,
  extras, stunts, SFX, catering ..  30–45%
POST & VFX
  Editorial, VFX, music, sound, DI  15–50%  (VFX-driven)
OTHER
  Insurance, bond, legal, fringes,
  contingency (~10%), overhead ...  10–20%
```

**Fringes** — payroll taxes plus guild/union health & pension contributions
loaded onto every labour line. Commonly **20–35%** on top of gross wages.
Routinely forgotten by outsiders and never by line producers.

## 10. Contingency, overages, and cost reporting

- **Contingency** — typically **10%** of the budget, held for the unforeseen.
  Bond companies require it and control access to it.
- **Cost report** — issued weekly by the production accountant showing
  *actual + committed + estimate to complete* against budget, line by line.
  This is the film's real-time P&L and the document everyone above the
  production reads.
- **Overage** — spending above budget. Who pays depends on the deal: studio
  absorbs it; on an independent, the bond company steps in once contingency is
  exhausted.
- **Amortisation of overhead** — studios charge their own films an overhead
  fee (historically **10–15%** of the budget) and interest on the money
  advanced. This is a major driver of "why did that hit never break even" —
  see `11-money-accounting-and-participations.md`.

## 11. Numbers to anchor on

| Item | Directional figure |
|---|---|
| Micro-budget / no-star indie | under $2M |
| Independent drama with recognisable cast | $5M–$20M |
| Studio mid-budget (drama, comedy, thriller) | $20M–$60M |
| Studio genre / horror | $5M–$40M (Blumhouse model ~$5–15M) |
| Studio franchise / tentpole | $150M–$300M+ |
| Animation (studio CG feature) | $100M–$200M |
| Contingency | 10% |
| Completion bond fee | 1.5–3% |
| Insurance package | 1–3% |
| Fringes on labour | 20–35% |
| Studio overhead charge | 10–15% of budget |
| Typical tax incentive | 20–40% of qualifying local spend |
| Agency commission / manager / lawyer | 10% / 10–15% / 5% |
