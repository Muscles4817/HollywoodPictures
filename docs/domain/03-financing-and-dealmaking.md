# 03 — Financing & Dealmaking

*Where the money comes from, what it costs, and what it buys.* The financing
structure of a film determines who controls the cut, who gets paid first, and
what the film must earn to be considered a success.

> **Domain reference.** Real industry, not this game. See `README.md`.

---

## 1. The two financing worlds

**Studio-financed.** The studio pays the negative cost from its own balance
sheet (possibly with a co-financier), distributes worldwide, and owns the film.
Simple, expensive, and the studio bears everything.

**Independently financed.** No single party has the money, so the producer
assembles it from four or five sources, each with its own security and
recoupment position. Every element must be locked before the first dollar is
drawn. This is where most of the complexity lives.

A typical independent capital stack:

```
┌─ Pre-sales (foreign territory MGs)          — 30–50%
├─ Tax incentive / rebate (soft money)        — 15–35%
├─ Gap or super-gap loan (against unsold)     — 10–20%
├─ Equity (private, fund, or company)         — 10–30%
└─ Deferrals (talent/crew paid later)         — 0–10%
```

All of it is usually **cash-flowed by a bank** against those contracts, and
the whole thing is guaranteed by a **completion bond** (§7).

### 1.1 Who is actually in the room

Financing is done by a small, specialised set of people, most of whom never
visit the set.

| Role | What they do | Who they answer to |
|---|---|---|
| **Producer** | Owns the project, assembles the stack, signs almost everything, and is personally on the hook for the promises made to get it closed | The financiers, and nobody |
| **Executive producer (financing EP)** | Frequently the person who introduced the money; the credit is often payment for that introduction | Themselves |
| **Film finance lawyer** | Runs the close: drafts and negotiates the loan, security, interparty and collection documents, and maintains the **conditions-precedent checklist** | The producer, who is paying them |
| **Production counsel** | Chain of title, talent contracts, clearances, guild paperwork, E&O prerequisites | The producer |
| **Sales agent** | Estimates, foreign licence deals, market presence (§4) | Commission; aligned but not identical to the producer |
| **Bank / lender** | Discounts contracts into cash; wants belt, braces, and a bond | Its own credit committee |
| **Equity investor** | The only money with no contract to be repaid from — takes the real risk, sits last in the waterfall | Their own LPs, or their own instincts |
| **Completion guarantor** | Guarantees delivery; approves the budget, schedule and key crew (§7) | Its underwriters |
| **Collection account manager (CAM)** | An independent third party who receives all revenue post-delivery and pays it out in the contracted order (`11-money-accounting-and-participations.md` §1) | The interparty agreement |
| **Line producer / UPM** | Builds the budget the whole structure is sized against (§9) | The producer |
| **Studio business affairs** | On a studio picture, replaces most of the above (§3.3) | The studio |

On a studio film the equivalent list collapses to: the production executive,
business affairs, legal, finance, and the physical production department. There
is no bank, no bond, no CAM, and no interparty agreement, because there is only
one party.

### 1.2 The studio path, end to end

1. **Greenlight** (`02-development.md` §8) sets an approved budget and a
   release date at the same meeting. The date is often the harder constraint.
2. **Business affairs** closes the ATL deals — director, cast, producers —
   converting the greenlight into pay-or-play liability (§2.1).
3. **A production number and account codes** are issued; the film becomes a
   cost centre in the studio's accounting system.
4. **Cash flow** is drawn from the studio's treasury against a weekly forecast
   prepared by the production accountant. The studio charges the film interest
   on that money and an overhead fee (§10).
5. **Co-financing**, if any, is papered in parallel — a slate partner taking a
   fixed percentage of the negative cost and the same percentage of receipts,
   or a single-picture partner taking specified territories.
6. **Delivery** to the studio's own distribution arm is an internal transfer,
   not a sale, which is why studio delivery is less brutal than independent
   delivery (§5.1).

The entire structure can be closed in **days** once greenlit, because the only
signature that matters is the studio's. That speed is what the studio system
is actually selling.

### 1.3 Walking an independent close, step by step

This is the sequence that people mean by "we're closing". It runs roughly
**6–16 weeks** from "we have the money in principle" to first drawdown, and it
is the stage at which most independent films die.

| # | Step | What is produced | Who signs / acts | Typical duration |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Chain of title assembled** | Option/purchase agreement, all writer agreements, copyright registration and search, title report, life-rights agreements | Production counsel; sellers and writers | 2–8 weeks, longer if broken |
| 2 | **SPV formed** | A single-purpose company per film, sometimes one per jurisdiction, into which rights are assigned | Producer, corporate counsel | 1–2 weeks |
| 3 | **Budget and schedule locked** | Movie Magic budget + strip board, cash-flow schedule (§9) | Line producer, 1st AD, producer | 3–6 weeks, iterative |
| 4 | **Talent deal memos signed** | Short-form deals for director and essential cast (§3.4) | Business affairs / producer's lawyer, agents | Runs in parallel |
| 5 | **Sales agency agreement** | Territories, term, commission (10–25%), expense cap, estimates schedule | Producer, sales agent | 1–3 weeks |
| 6 | **Foreign licence deals papered** | Long-form international distribution agreements (IDAs) per territory, with payment schedule and **essential elements** clause | Sales agent, buyers | 4–12 weeks after the market |
| 7 | **Incentive qualification confirmed** | Interim certification, opinion letter from an approved accountant estimating the credit (§6) | Production accountant, incentive counsel | 2–8 weeks |
| 8 | **Completion bond issued** | Completion guaranty (to financiers) + completion agreement (with producer), after script/schedule/budget/crew review (§7) | Guarantor's rep, producer | 3–6 weeks |
| 9 | **Insurance bound** | Production package + E&O, with financiers named as loss payee/additional insured (§8) | Broker, underwriter | 2–4 weeks |
| 10 | **Loan and security documents** | Facility agreement, debenture/charge, copyright mortgage and assignment, UCC-1 filings, lab access letter, notices of assignment to each buyer | Bank's counsel vs producer's counsel | 4–8 weeks, the long pole |
| 11 | **CAMA** | Collection account management agreement naming the collection agent and the payout order | All beneficiaries | 2–4 weeks |
| 12 | **Interparty agreement** | The single document reconciling bank, bond, sales agent, equity and CAM into one recoupment order and one set of cure rights | Everyone, simultaneously | The last thing signed |
| 13 | **Conditions precedent satisfied** | The CP checklist — typically 40–120 numbered items | Producer's lawyer, chasing everyone | 1–3 weeks of pure attrition |
| 14 | **Closing and first drawdown** | Signature pages released from escrow; money hits the production account | Bank | 1 day, after all of the above |

Two things about this list surprise outsiders. First, **the money arrives
last** — crew have often been working for weeks on the producer's own credit,
a deferred fee, or a bridge loan. Second, **step 13 is where films die**: not
because anyone refuses, but because item 74 on the checklist is a signature
from a co-writer nobody can find, and the start date passes.

### 1.4 The recoupment order the interparty agreement creates

Everything above exists to establish who gets paid, in what order, out of the
collection account. A standard independent order:

```
1. Collection agent's fee
2. Sales agent's commission and recoupable market expenses
3. Senior bank debt (pre-sale and incentive facilities) + interest + fees
4. Gap/mezzanine debt + its (much higher) interest and fee
5. Deferments (talent and crew who agreed to be paid later)
6. Equity, to 100% of investment — often with a premium of 10–20% first
7. Net profits split — commonly 50/50 producer side / equity side
```

The producer's own fee is usually somewhere between 5 and 6, and a producer
who has been forced to defer it sits behind every bank in the deal. See
`11-money-accounting-and-participations.md` §1 for how the waterfall behaves
once revenue actually arrives.

### 1.5 What the money costs

Independent money is expensive, and the cost is a real line in the budget
(§9, "Other charges").

| Source | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| **Pre-sale discounting** | Bank advances **80–90%** of contracted MG face for approved buyers; interest at a floating base + **3–5%**; arrangement fee **1–2%** |
| **Incentive lending** | **85–92%** of the estimated credit; interest **8–12%**; requires an accountant's opinion letter |
| **Gap loan** | Capped at **10–15%** of budget, requires unsold territory estimates covering **2–2.5×** the gap; effective cost **12–20%** including fees |
| **Equity** | No interest, but a **10–20% premium** ahead of profit split, and usually approval rights |
| **Completion bond** | **1.5–3%** of the strike price (§7) |
| **Legal (both sides)** | **$150K–$500K** on a $10–30M independent; the producer usually pays the bank's costs too |

Finance and legal costs together commonly consume **6–12%** of an independent
budget before a single foot is shot. This is the structural reason independents
have less money on screen than a studio film of nominally the same budget.

### 1.6 How a close collapses

| Failure | Mechanism | Typical timing |
|---|---|---|
| **Cast falls off** | The star's dates move or they take another film; the IDAs contain an essential-elements clause and the buyers are released | Anytime up to the first day |
| **Estimates don't cover** | The market comes and goes and the sold territories fall short; the gap needed exceeds what any lender will take | 4–8 weeks after the market |
| **Buyer can't pay** | An approved-list buyer is downgraded, or a smaller buyer can't open the letter of credit the bank requires | At loan documentation |
| **Chain of title defect** | An unsigned co-writer, an expired option, an uncleared underlying work, a life-rights holder who changes their mind | Discovered in due diligence, always late |
| **Bond refuses the schedule** | The guarantor will not bond the number of days for the pages, or will not bond this director without a stronger 1st AD | Weeks 3–6 |
| **Incentive slips** | Annual pool exhausted, rules change, or the start date moves outside the certified window | Anytime |
| **Equity doesn't fund** | The soft commitment was always soft; the investor asks for one more piece of information every week | At the drawdown, catastrophically |
| **The date passes** | Location seasons, a star's next commitment, or the incentive window all have hard edges | Cumulative effect of everything above |

The pattern to internalise: **an independent financing is a set of
interdependent conditions, each of which expires.** The producer is not
raising money so much as holding a dozen decaying commitments in alignment
long enough to sign them all on the same day.

### 1.7 What separates a good producer at this from a bad one

1. **Sequencing.** Knowing which piece must be locked first because everything
   else is conditional on it — usually the director, then one cast name, then
   the jurisdiction.
2. **Budgeting to the money, not the wish.** Sizing the film to a stack that
   plausibly exists, rather than closing a $14M stack for an $18M film and
   hoping.
3. **Honesty with the bond and the bank.** The producer who reports a problem
   early keeps control; the one who conceals it loses the film (§7.3).
4. **Knowing what a commitment is worth.** Distinguishing an agent's
   enthusiasm from an availability, and an LOI from a signature (§2.4).
5. **Relationships with actual capital.** Equity that has closed a film before
   behaves completely differently from equity that has not.
6. **Chain of title hygiene from day one.** The cheapest fix for a rights
   defect is made in development; the most expensive is made in week 13 of a
   close.
7. **Tolerance for attrition.** Most closes take two or three attempts across
   two or three years, and the skill is being able to restart without burning
   the elements.

## 2. Attachments and the package

An "attachment" is a commitment by a talent to the project. Attachments create
value; they also create constraints.

- **Director attachment** is the single biggest value step for a spec or IP
  project. A director with heat makes the package financeable.
- **Cast attachment** determines pre-sale value directly: foreign buyers bid
  against a **star's value in their territory**, tracked as an internal
  estimate per market.
- **Soft attachment** — interested, no deal. **Hard attachment** — a signed
  deal, possibly pay-or-play.
- **Elements** — the collective term for director + principal cast + writer.

**Letters of intent (LOIs)** are the intermediate instrument: non-binding but
sufficient to shop the package.

### 2.1 Pay-or-play

The talent is owed their fee whether the film happens or not, contingent only
on their own availability and non-breach. It is what talent demands to hold a
window in their schedule. Once a studio signs pay-or-play deals with a star and
director, it has converted development risk into a hard liability, and the
project acquires momentum that is more financial than creative.

**Holds and windows.** Talent availability is a calendar problem: a star will
"hold" dates (first position, second position). Losing a hold usually costs the
production a start date and can trigger cascading crew and location losses.

The mechanics are worth stating precisely, because "pay-or-play" is used
loosely:

- It attaches on a **trigger** — most often the studio exercising a start-date
  option, or a fixed calendar date in the deal ("PoP on 1 March or on
  commencement of principal photography, whichever is earlier").
- It is **conditional on the talent's own performance**: they must remain
  available, pass the medical (§8.2), obtain a visa, and not breach.
- It usually carries a **first-position obligation** — the talent may not take
  conflicting work in the guaranteed period without consent.
- It does **not** oblige the studio to make the film. A studio can and does pay
  a star $10M not to appear in a film that is never shot; this is a normal, if
  painful, line in a write-off.
- On the independent side, pay-or-play is often **conditional on financing
  closing**, which is precisely the thing agents fight to remove and producers
  fight to keep.

### 2.2 How an attachment is actually secured

There is no single route, but the sequence below is the common one, and it is
mostly conducted by phone between four or five people.

| Stage | What happens | Who |
|---|---|---|
| **Target list** | Producer, director and (if engaged) casting director build a ranked list per role, annotated with availability, quote, and territory value | Producer, director, CD, sales agent |
| **Temperature check** | An informal call to the agent: is the client free, would they read it. Deliberately not an offer, so a pass costs nothing | Producer or CD to agent |
| **Material out** | Script plus a **director's letter**, and often a lookbook or director's treatment (§2.3) | Producer's office |
| **The read window** | Agents sit on scripts. A week is normal; three weeks is normal for a busy star. Chasing too hard reads as desperation | Agent, assistant |
| **The meeting** | Director and actor meet, in person or on video. Most star casting is decided here, and it is a mutual audition (`04-preproduction.md` §8.1) | Director, actor, sometimes producer |
| **Interest signalled** | "They're interested subject to…" — the conditions are always dates, director, and money, in that order | Agent to producer |
| **LOI or deal memo** | Either a non-binding letter for shopping, or straight to a short-form deal if the money is real | Lawyers, business affairs |
| **Announcement** | A trade announcement is itself a financing act — it makes the attachment public and harder to walk away from | Publicist, trades |

Three details practitioners know:

- **The agent is not the only door.** Managers, lawyers, a producing partner,
  a previous collaborator, or the actor's own production company can all be a
  faster route, and on independents usually are.
- **A "hip-pocket" pass** — the agent declining to pass the material to the
  client at all — is the most common failure, and it is invisible. The
  producer is told "they passed" when the client never saw it.
- **Offers are made in writing and become quotes.** An offer that is accepted
  and then falls apart can still anchor the actor's quote (§3.2), which is why
  business affairs is careful about what number goes out.

### 2.3 The director's letter

For any wanted actor the director's letter is the load-bearing document, and
it is genuinely a craft object. What a good one contains, roughly in order:

1. **What the film is about** in a paragraph — not the plot, the argument.
2. **Why this actor, specifically.** Referencing a performance the director
   actually admires, in a way that could not have been written about anyone
   else. Generic flattery is instantly detectable and fatal.
3. **What the part is for.** How the role functions in the film, including the
   uncomfortable parts, and what the actor will get to do that they have not
   been asked to do before.
4. **Tone and reference.** Two or three films, a visual approach, sometimes
   images. This is where the lookbook attaches.
5. **The practical truth.** Where and when it shoots, for how long, in what
   conditions, and what the budget actually is. Understating a hard shoot is
   the most reliable way to lose an actor later.
6. **A specific promise the director intends to keep** — rehearsal time, a
   collaborator the actor wants, protection of a scene.

Length is one to three pages. It goes with the script, not instead of it. The
equivalent instrument aimed at financiers rather than talent is the
**lookbook** or **pitch deck**: tone imagery, comparable titles with their
actual grosses, budget top line, incentive jurisdiction, and the elements to
date.

### 2.4 What elements are actually worth

The blunt version: an element is worth the difference it makes to the
financeable number, minus what it costs. That is an arithmetic question, and
sales agents answer it explicitly.

| Element | What it typically moves | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| **Bankable director** | Makes the package readable at all; can lift estimates 10–30% and is a precondition for cast | "Bankable" here means "buyers know the name", which is a smaller list than critical reputation suggests |
| **Star lead with foreign value** | The largest single lever on pre-sale estimates — can double them in genre | Territory-specific; a star huge in Japan may be worth nothing in Germany |
| **Second-billed name** | Diminishing; usually worth far less than the first name | Buyers price the poster, not the ensemble |
| **Genre fit** | Action, horror, thriller and sci-fi travel; comedy, most drama and anything dialogue-dependent do not | The single most reliable rule in foreign sales |
| **Writer** | Almost nothing to foreign buyers; real value to equity, awards positioning and other talent | |
| **Awards pedigree** | Moves specialist buyers and domestic acquisition, not volume buyers | |
| **A finished, well-received festival cut** | Worth more than any attachment — it removes execution risk entirely (§5.2) | Which is why some films skip pre-sales and gamble on the festival |

The arithmetic that kills packages: a name who adds **$2M** to worldwide
estimates but costs **$1.5M** plus perks, fringes and a bigger insurance
loading is roughly **net zero** — and has also consumed the schedule
flexibility, since they are now available for six weeks in one specific
window. Experienced producers run this calculation before they chase.

### 2.5 How packages fall apart

Attachments decay. A soft attachment has a practical half-life of about
**six months**; a signed pay-or-play deal with a start date has a hard edge.

| Mechanism | What actually happens |
|---|---|
| **Better offer** | The actor takes a studio film for four times the money in the same window. Nothing in an LOI prevents this |
| **Director drift** | The director develops something else while waiting; when the money arrives their availability is gone |
| **Rewrite drift** | The script the actor said yes to is rewritten to satisfy a financier, and the actor's part is no longer the part they read |
| **Date slippage** | The financing takes four months longer than promised and walks straight into the actor's next commitment |
| **The domino** | Cast B was only ever attached because of Cast A; A leaving releases B, and often the director too |
| **Financier veto** | A buyer or equity investor refuses a specific name, and the producer must choose between the money and the element |
| **Announcement fatigue** | A project that has been announced twice with different casts is read by the market as a project that does not happen |
| **Agency politics** | An agency will not let a client attach for a fee below their quote if it would damage the quote across the roster (§3.2) |

The producer's counter-moves are limited and well known: pay for a short
**exclusive hold** (real money, weeks not months), keep the rewrite away from
the attached actor's role, put the start date in the LOI, and close the
smallest credible stack fast rather than the ideal stack slowly.

### 2.6 What separates good packaging from bad

1. **Casting for the money and the film at once.** The failure mode is a name
   who finances the film and is wrong in it (`04-preproduction.md` §8.3) — the
   damage lands in the cut, long after the financing succeeded.
2. **Knowing the territory map.** Which names actually move which markets, from
   the sales agent's estimates rather than from domestic fame.
3. **Speed after a yes.** The window between an actor's verbal yes and a signed
   deal memo is when packages are lost. Good producers have the deal memo
   drafted before the meeting.
4. **Not over-announcing.** Announcing an attachment that isn't papered burns
   credibility with buyers who track exactly this.
5. **Protecting the director's relationship with the talent.** The director's
   letter, the meeting, and the promise kept in prep are worth more than any
   fee increment.
6. **Having a real second choice.** A list that stops at three names is why
   films end up with their fifth choice on a bad list.

## 3. Talent deal structures

| Element | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Fixed fee / guarantee** | Cash compensation, usually paid over the shoot |
| **Quote** | The talent's most recent fee, which sets the floor for the next |
| **Backend / points** | Participation in the film's revenue — see `11-…` |
| **First-dollar gross** | Points from the first dollar of studio receipts; extremely rare now, historically the top-tier star deal |
| **Cash break / breakeven definition** | The negotiated point after which participations begin |
| **Box office bonus** | Fixed payments at defined gross thresholds — the modern replacement for first-dollar gross |
| **Buyout** | A lump sum replacing residuals/backend; standard on streaming films |
| **Perks / riders** | Travel, accommodation, security, trailer size, per diem, assistants, gym, chef — real money, negotiated in detail |
| **Approvals** | Cast, director, cut, still photo, likeness, marketing materials |
| **Credit** | Position, size, paid ad requirements — heavily negotiated |

Director deals additionally involve the **DGA director's cut** (a guaranteed
period to produce the director's own version before the studio recuts) and
frequently **final cut** for a small number of filmmakers.

### 3.1 Who talks to whom

A talent negotiation is conducted between two professionals who negotiate
against each other several times a year and will do so for decades. Neither is
the person whose money or career is at stake.

```
  TALENT SIDE                          BUYER SIDE
  ───────────                          ──────────
  Actor / Director
      │
      ├── Agent (10%)          ◄────►  Business Affairs executive
      │     the dealmaker               (studio) or the producer's
      │                                 entertainment lawyer (indie)
      ├── Manager (10–15%)                    │
      │     strategy, taste, saying no        │
      │                                       │
      └── Entertainment lawyer (5%)  ◄────►  Production counsel /
            papers it, fights the                studio legal
            long-form                              │
                                            Production executive
                                            (creative sign-off)
```

- **The agent** makes the deal: fee, dates, credit, bonuses. The agent talks to
  business affairs, not to the director or the studio head.
- **The manager** is more often the one who decides *whether* to do it, and
  handles the relationship with the filmmaker. Managers cannot legally procure
  employment in California, a distinction observed loosely.
- **The lawyer** takes over at long-form and fights the sixty pages of standard
  terms and the participation definition.
- **The producer or director never negotiates money directly** with the talent
  if it can be avoided — the entire point of the structure is that the creative
  relationship survives whatever happens on the money.
- On a small independent the producer *is* business affairs, which is why
  independent deals close slowly and badly-drafted long-forms are common.

### 3.2 The order in which things are conceded

Negotiations proceed in a fairly fixed order, and both sides know it. Roughly:

| Order | Point | Why it comes here |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Availability and dates** | If the dates don't work nothing else matters. Start date, guaranteed period (e.g. "10 weeks guaranteed"), stop date, overage rate per week |
| 2 | **Fee** | The headline. Anchored on the **quote** — the talent's last comparable fee — which the agent protects across the roster because it sets the floor for everyone |
| 3 | **Credit** | Position (first, last, "and", "with"), size relative to title, paid ad requirements, single card. Costs nothing in cash and is fought over hardest |
| 4 | **Bonuses and backend** | Box office bonuses at gross thresholds, award bonuses, streaming performance bonuses, and the backend definition by reference |
| 5 | **Approvals and consultation** | Cast, director, cut, likeness, stills (kill rights over a percentage of images), marketing materials |
| 6 | **Perks** | Travel class, companion travel, housing, per diem, security, trainer, assistant, trailer spec |
| 7 | **Long-form boilerplate** | Everything else, over the following months |

The trades that actually happen:

- **Cash down, backend up.** The standard budget-constrained move: a reduced
  fee against a better breakeven definition or a lower bonus threshold. Agents
  accept it more readily when the *quote is protected* — the deal memo records
  the notional full fee with a stated deferral, so the quote survives.
- **MFN ("most favoured nation").** Two comparable actors both take the same
  fee, credit, or perk, and neither has to be told they came second. It is how
  ensembles are closed quickly and how a single concession cascades across a
  cast.
- **Credit for money.** A "and Name as Character" end-of-cast card is a real,
  routine substitute for cash.
- **Approvals are conceded last and regretted first.** A cast approval given to
  a lead in week one of a package constrains casting for the next six months
  (`04-preproduction.md` §8.4).

### 3.3 What business affairs actually does

Business affairs is the least visible and most consequential department in a
studio. Its work is not "negotiating" so much as **maintaining a consistent
price structure across an entire slate**.

1. **Precedent.** Every deal is checked against what the studio paid comparable
   talent on comparable films. Business affairs exists partly to stop one
   over-eager production executive from resetting a quote the studio will pay
   on ten future films.
2. **Structure design.** Choosing between fee, bonus, and backend to get the
   talent to yes at the lowest *cash* cost and the lowest *risk-weighted* cost
   — a bonus at $500M domestic is nearly free; one at $150M is not.
3. **Drafting.** The deal memo, then the long-form, then the amendments.
4. **Guild compliance.** Minimums, credits rules, residual obligations,
   pension-and-health contributions, and the specific requirements of DGA, WGA
   and SAG-AFTRA agreements (`12-talent-labor-and-careers.md` §7).
5. **Clearance gating.** No deal closes without medical clearance for
   insurance (§8.2), immigration/visa feasibility, and confirmation the talent
   is not conflicted by a first-position hold elsewhere.
6. **Chain of title.** Making sure the studio actually owns what it thinks it
   owns before any money moves.
7. **Saying no in a way the agent can live with.** The relationship is
   repeated; a business affairs executive who wins every point stops getting
   calls returned.

The equivalent function on an independent is a single entertainment lawyer
billing hourly, which is why independent talent deals are frequently signed
late, sometimes after the actor has already worked.

### 3.4 Deal memo vs long-form

Almost all films are shot on **deal memos**. The long-form is often signed
after the picture wraps, and occasionally never.

**The deal memo** (short-form, typically 2–8 pages) contains only what must be
agreed before work starts:

- Parties, the lending company (talent are usually engaged through their own
  loan-out company), and the role or services
- Guaranteed compensation and payment schedule
- Start date, guaranteed period, free weeks (post-shoot obligations included in
  the fee: ADR, reshoots, publicity days), and the weekly overage rate
- Pay-or-play trigger and conditions (§2.1)
- Credit — position, size, paid ads
- Approvals list
- Backend, usually by reference: "5% of Net Proceeds as defined in Company's
  standard definition, subject to good-faith negotiation"
- Perks, often as an attached schedule
- Conditions precedent: medical, visa, guild signatory status, financing close
- The catch-all: "subject to negotiation and execution of a long-form agreement
  containing Company's standard terms and conditions"

**The long-form** (40–120 pages, plus exhibits) contains everything the deal
memo deferred: the full **participation definition** (which alone can run 30+
pages and is where net profits are engineered out of existence —
`11-money-accounting-and-participations.md` §2), the standard terms and
conditions, representations and warranties, force majeure and suspension
rights, default and cure, moral clauses, name-and-likeness, merchandising,
sequel and remake obligations, arbitration, and the perks rider.

The practical consequence: **the fight over the long-form is a fight over
money that mostly does not exist yet**, conducted with no leverage, because the
talent has already worked. This is a structural reason participations pay out
so rarely.

### 3.5 Director deals in particular

- **The DGA director's cut** is a guaranteed period — 10 weeks on a first film
  under the DGA Basic Agreement, six weeks otherwise, running from the last day
  of principal photography — during which the director produces their own
  version before anyone else can recut (`07-postproduction.md` §2).
- **Final cut** is contractual, not a right, and is held by a small number of
  filmmakers. What is far more common is a **preview obligation** (the studio
  may recut after a defined number of test screenings) or **cut approval within
  a rating and running time** — deliver an R-rated cut under 120 minutes and it
  is yours.
- **Delivery obligations** — running time, rating, and specification. A
  director who cannot deliver the contracted rating has breached, and this is
  the lever studios actually use (`07-postproduction.md` §8).
- **Pay** is typically a DGA-minimum-plus structure: a fee for prep, shoot and
  post (often split 20/60/20 or similar), plus backend, plus box office
  bonuses.
- **Overage**. Directors are frequently on a "no additional compensation for
  overschedule" basis while the crew is on overtime — the director is one of
  the few people who costs the same on day 60 as on day 40.

### 3.6 How talent deals go wrong

| Failure | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **Shooting without a signed deal memo** | The talent has leverage and no obligation; renegotiation mid-shoot is a real and expensive phenomenon |
| **Dates not locked precisely** | The "guaranteed period" expires mid-schedule and the film pays weekly overage at 1/10th of the fee per week, or loses the actor |
| **Approvals granted too broadly** | Cast, cut, or marketing approvals that make the film unmakeable or unsellable — an approval is a veto |
| **Free weeks not specified** | Reshoots, ADR and press become chargeable extras, negotiated when the film needs them most |
| **MFN cascade** | One concession to one actor propagates to five, multiplying the cost of a small win |
| **Quote inflation** | Overpaying resets the talent's floor and the studio pays it again on the next three films |
| **Perks unbudgeted** | Riders are real money: private travel, security details and housing can add 10–20% to a star's cost and are frequently missing from the top sheet |
| **Long-form never signed** | Disputes about backend, sequel obligations and credit are litigated years later against a two-page memo |
| **Medical failed after announcement** | The deal was conditional; the film has lost weeks and a public attachment (§8.3) |

## 4. Pre-sales and territory rights

The independent world's engine.

- A **sales agent** takes the package (script, director, cast, budget, start
  date) to **AFM / EFM / Marché du Film**.
- Foreign distributors buy the rights for their territory, committing to a
  **minimum guarantee (MG)** payable on delivery.
- The MG contract is then **discounted by a bank** — the producer borrows
  against it, at interest, to fund production.
- Buyers evaluate on **cast value in that territory**, genre, and budget. Action
  and horror travel; comedy notoriously does not.

**Estimates** are the sales agent's published low/high value per territory.
The whole system runs on them; a package is financeable when estimates cover
the budget with margin.

### 4.1 How an estimate is actually built

An estimate is not a guess. It is a comparables exercise, done territory by
territory, on a spreadsheet the sales agent maintains across their whole slate.

The inputs, in the order they matter:

1. **Comparable licence fees.** What did three or four genuinely similar films
   — same genre, similar budget band, similar cast tier — actually licence for
   in that territory in the last 2–4 years? Sales agents know these numbers
   because they made the deals, and this is the real asset of a sales company.
2. **Cast value in that market.** Tracked per actor per territory. A name that
   sells in Germany and Italy may be worth nothing in Korea, and vice versa.
3. **Genre travel.** Action, horror, thriller, sci-fi and animation travel.
   Comedy travels very badly (language, reference, timing). Dialogue-driven
   drama travels only with awards or a director's name.
4. **Budget as a credibility signal.** Buyers price the film they think will
   arrive; a $3M budget with a $20M ambition reads as undeliverable.
5. **Market conditions in that territory.** Whether the local pay-TV buyer is
   still buying, whether a local streamer has an output deal, whether the
   currency has moved, and whether the buyer already has too much unreleased
   product on the shelf.

The output is two numbers per territory, and the difference between them is
where the negotiation lives:

- The **high** ("ask") — what the agent opens at, used in the announcement and
  in optimistic financing conversations.
- The **low** ("take") — what the agent believes is genuinely achievable.

**The bank only counts the lows**, and then applies its own haircut. For gap
lending, banks typically count only the low estimates of *unsold* territories,
require them to cover the gap **2–2.5×**, and exclude the most volatile
territories (China above all) entirely. An estimates sheet that "covers the
budget" on the high column is not a financing.

A rough shape of foreign value for a mainstream genre title, as a share of
total non-US licence income:

| Territory group | Typical share of foreign |
|---|---|
| Germany / Austria | 8–12% |
| France | 7–10% |
| UK / Ireland | 8–12% |
| Italy | 5–8% |
| Spain | 4–7% |
| Japan | 6–12% (genre-dependent, unpredictable) |
| Korea | 3–6% |
| Australia / NZ | 4–6% |
| Latin America | 5–8% |
| Scandinavia, Benelux, E. Europe, Middle East | 10–15% combined |
| China | 0–20%, and excluded from every financing model |
| Airlines / ancillary | 1–3% |

### 4.2 A sales agent's market, day by day

The four fixtures: **EFM** (February, Berlin), **Marché du Film** (May,
Cannes), **AFM** (November, historically Santa Monica), and **TIFF**
(September, Toronto — not a formal market but where a great deal of business is
done). **Sundance** in January is an acquisitions market, not a pre-sales one
(§5.2).

| When | What happens | Who |
|---|---|---|
| **6–8 weeks before** | The slate is set. Which titles are "ready" — meaning castable claims can be made. Artwork commissioned | Sales company head, marketing |
| **4–6 weeks before** | The **heads-up email** goes to every buyer on the list with the line-up and one-line descriptions. Buyers start requesting scripts and meetings | Sales team |
| **2–3 weeks before** | Trade announcements timed to land into the market: the cast attachment, the director. This *is* the marketing | Publicist, trades |
| **Market days 1–2** | 20–40 meetings a day in a hotel suite or booth, 20–30 minutes each, back to back. One-sheets on the wall; a promo reel or sizzle if there is footage | Sales agent, buyers |
| **Days 2–4** | Screenings of completed titles; the pre-sale titles live entirely on the pitch. Offers begin arriving as emails with a number and conditions | Both |
| **Days 3–5** | Negotiation on the numbers: MG, payment terms, holdbacks, delivery date. Deals are "closed" as one-page deal memos or emails | Sales agent, producer by phone |
| **Days 4–6** | The trades are fed closed deals. A title that announces four or five territories reads as hot and the remaining territories re-price upward | Sales agent, publicist |
| **Weeks after** | Long-form IDAs drafted and negotiated; letters of credit opened; notices of assignment served on the bank's behalf | Lawyers |

The unglamorous truth is that **most of a market is meetings that produce
nothing**, and the deals that close were substantially agreed by email before
anyone flew. The market's real function is the deadline: it forces buyers to
decide in the same week as their competitors.

### 4.3 How a buyer actually decides

A foreign distributor is not betting on the film's quality. They are asking a
narrow commercial question: **can I recoup this MG from my own pipeline?**

1. **Do I have an output obligation to fill?** Many buyers have contracts with
   local pay-TV or streamers requiring a number of titles a year with defined
   minimum specs (budget, cast tier, English-language). A slot that must be
   filled is the single biggest driver of a pre-sale.
2. **What did the comparable do here?** Their own numbers, not the film's
   worldwide ones.
3. **Is the cast recognisable *here*?** Local recognition, from their own TV
   and streaming data.
4. **Does it dub or subtitle well?** A dialogue comedy is nearly unsellable;
   an action film with 300 lines is easy.
5. **What is my downside?** Most buyers structure so the MG is recoverable from
   TV/VOD alone, treating theatrical as upside. This is why MGs are far smaller
   than casual observers expect.
6. **Delivery risk.** Will this film exist, on time, at this spec? A completion
   bond and a credible producer are worth real money here.
7. **Shelf pressure.** A buyer with twelve unreleased acquisitions is not
   buying, regardless of the film.

Payment structure is the buyer's other lever: typically **10–20% on signature**
and the balance **on delivery**, often via a letter of credit from a bank the
lender will accept. A buyer who cannot open an acceptable LC is, from the
financing's perspective, not a buyer at all.

### 4.4 What is actually in the licence agreement

- **Territory and media** — theatrical, home video, pay-TV, free-TV, SVOD,
  transactional, non-theatrical (airlines, ships, hotels).
- **Term** — commonly 12–25 years from delivery.
- **MG and payment schedule**, plus an **overage** formula: the buyer recoups
  the MG plus a distribution fee and expenses, then shares back. Overages are
  paid on a minority of deals — a working assumption of **10–20%** of licences
  ever paying an overage is not cynical.
- **Essential elements** — named director and cast. A change releases the buyer
  or triggers renegotiation. This is the clause that makes a package fragile.
- **Delivery date and delivery schedule** — the technical and legal materials
  list (§5.1). Non-compliance is the buyer's escape hatch.
- **Specification** — running time band, rating/certificate, language,
  final-cut constraints, sometimes a required music track or a promise about
  the ending.
- **Holdbacks** — the dates before which the buyer may not release in each
  medium, protecting the worldwide window pattern
  (`09-marketing-and-distribution.md` §7).

### 4.5 When a film underdelivers on its estimates

This is the ordinary case, not the exception, and the system has well-worn
responses.

| Situation | What happens |
|---|---|
| **A territory goes unsold at the market** | It sits in the gap. If the gap exceeds what the lender will take, the budget must come down or the film does not close |
| **The film is finished and is weaker than promised** | Buyers who signed early are contractually bound. Buyers who signed on a handshake vanish |
| **The buyer wants out** | They almost never refuse outright — they **refuse delivery on technical grounds**. The delivery schedule has 100+ items and something is always missing or late |
| **The buyer asks for a discount** | Common. The sales agent's choice is to hold the contract and litigate a buyer they need next year, or take 70–80 cents and keep the relationship |
| **Estimates were simply wrong** | Unsold territories re-price downward; the gap lender is now under-secured, and the equity — sitting last — absorbs the loss |
| **The film underperforms in release** | Overages never arrive. The film "sold out" its value at the pre-sale stage and the producer sees nothing beyond their fee |
| **A pre-sold film becomes a hit** | The producer captures very little of it. Territories were sold flat, years earlier, at a fraction of what the film turned out to be worth |

That last row is the fundamental trade of the pre-sale model: **the producer
sells the upside to buy the certainty of getting made.** It is why the same
producers keep making $8M genre films and never get rich from the one that
worked.

### 4.6 What separates a good sales agent from a bad one

1. **Honest estimates.** An agent who inflates estimates to win the film gets
   the producer a financing that later collapses, or a film that closes and
   never repays. Producers who have been burned once check estimates against
   the agent's own past deals.
2. **Buyer knowledge.** Knowing which buyer in Italy is actually paying this
   year, which one is over-stocked, and which one's parent company has changed
   strategy.
3. **Package construction.** Telling a producer, before the money is spent,
   that this cast does not travel and that a different name at the same price
   doubles the estimates.
4. **Market choreography.** Timing announcements, screenings and the first
   closed deals so the title accumulates momentum across a five-day market.
5. **Collecting.** Chasing delivery payments, LCs and overages years after
   everyone else has moved on. A large fraction of an independent film's
   theoretical revenue is lost simply to nobody chasing it.
6. **Not over-selling the film into the ground.** Agreeing terms and holdbacks
   that keep the film's later windows worth something.
7. **Delivery competence.** A sales company with a real delivery department
   turns technical rejections into cured items instead of lost MGs.

## 5. Negative pickup and acquisitions

- **Negative pickup** — a distributor contracts *in advance* to buy the finished
  film for a set price on delivery to spec. The producer borrows against that
  contract to make it. The distributor takes no production risk but must accept
  what is delivered (subject to a detailed delivery schedule).
- **Acquisition** — a completed film is bought after the fact, usually after a
  festival premiere (Sundance, Toronto, Cannes, Berlin, SXSW). Prices swing
  from nothing to eight figures on the strength of one screening.
- **Split rights** — separate deals for domestic vs international, or
  theatrical vs streaming.

### 5.1 The delivery schedule

The most underestimated document in independent film. It is an exhibit to
every licence and pickup agreement, typically **80–150 numbered items**, and
until every item is accepted, **nobody pays**.

Broad contents:

- **Picture and sound masters** — the graded master at the contracted
  specification, textless backgrounds, all versions (theatrical, airline,
  broadcast), and the **M&E** (music and effects) track without which the film
  cannot be dubbed. Missing or defective M&E is the single most common delivery
  failure (`07-postproduction.md` §7).
- **Audio stems**, dialogue/music/effects split, plus the 5.1 and stereo
  mixes.
- **Textless and localisation materials**, subtitle files, dubbing scripts,
  continuity script (a shot-by-shot transcript, produced by a specialist).
- **Marketing materials** — key art, unit stills (a contracted minimum number,
  approved by talent per their kill rights), EPK, trailer, behind-the-scenes.
- **Legal** — chain of title, copyright registration, all talent and crew
  agreements, music licences (sync **and** master, with the territory and term
  matching the licence), clearances, releases, the E&O policy with the
  distributor named, MPA rating certificate or local certificate.
- **Technical QC report** from an approved facility.

Delivery costs real money — commonly **$50K–$250K** on an independent — and
is routinely under-budgeted because it happens after everyone has left. It is
also the producer's last exposure: the bank is repaid from delivery payments,
so a delivery that stalls turns into default interest.

### 5.2 How an acquisition actually happens

The festival acquisition is the film industry's one genuine auction, and it
runs on a compressed clock.

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Pre-festival** | The sales agent sends a private screening link to a short list of buyers under embargo, or refuses to, betting that the room will be better than the link |
| **The premiere** | Acquisitions executives sit in the room reading the audience as much as the film. Walkouts, laughs and the applause at the end are the data |
| **The lobby** | Interest is signalled within minutes. An executive calling their studio head from the lobby is the mechanism by which eight-figure deals begin |
| **The night** | Offers arrive between midnight and 4am. The sales agent runs an informal auction by phone, playing buyers against each other |
| **The terms** | Not just price: P&P commitment (a guaranteed minimum marketing spend and screen count), release window, awards commitment, and whether it is theatrical at all |
| **Signature** | A deal memo signed within 24–72 hours; the long-form months later |
| **The cold room** | The far more common outcome — nobody bids, the film screens to polite applause, and the sales agent spends a year selling territories individually |

What buyers are actually assessing: whether the film has a **hook that can be
marketed** (`09-marketing-and-distribution.md` §12.1), whether the audience
reaction was real, whether reviews will be good enough to matter
(`16-critics-reviews-and-word-of-mouth.md`), and what the P&A commitment would
have to be. A film with no marketable hook is not bought at any price.

The seller's dilemma is that **the acquisition model is all-or-nothing**: a
completed film with no distributor has cost its investors everything, while a
pre-sold film with the same content would have been safely, unexcitingly
financed. Which route a producer takes is a statement about how good they
think the film will be.

## 6. Soft money: incentives and rebates

Government subsidy is now a primary driver of *where films shoot* and
frequently of *whether they get made*.

Common mechanisms:

- **Refundable tax credit** — a percentage of qualifying local spend returned
  in cash (or as a credit that can be sold to a local taxpayer at a discount).
- **Cash rebate** — paid on audited local spend.
- **Uplift** — additional percentage for shooting outside a capital, hiring
  local heads of department, or post-producing locally.
- **Co-production treaty** — the film qualifies as national in two countries,
  accessing both subsidy systems, in exchange for spend/personnel quotas.

Directional rates: **20–40%** of qualifying spend is the usual band, with
specific jurisdictions higher. Caps, per-project ceilings, annual pools, and
"cultural test" qualification (as in the UK) all apply.

Consequences to model:

- A production will relocate its story to chase a rebate, then dress the
  location to match the script.
- Incentives are usually paid **after** the spend and audit, so they must be
  **cash-flowed** (borrowed against) — costing interest.
- A jurisdiction's crew depth constrains how much can actually be spent there.

### 6.1 What actually qualifies

"Qualifying spend" is a defined term and it is narrower than "money we spent
there". The rules differ by jurisdiction, but the shape is consistent:

| Usually qualifies | Usually does not |
|---|---|
| Wages of **local residents**, at all levels | Wages of non-residents, or only up to a cap |
| Goods and services bought **from local vendors** | Anything invoiced from outside the jurisdiction |
| Equipment and stage rental in-territory | Equipment shipped in from elsewhere |
| Local accommodation, per diems, ground transport | International airfares (frequently excluded outright) |
| Post and VFX performed in-territory | Post performed elsewhere, unless a separate uplift applies |
| Local location fees, permits, construction | Financing costs, completion bond, insurance premiums (usually) |
| Some ATL, capped | ATL over the cap — per-person caps of **$500K–$1M** are common, and some regimes exclude ATL entirely |

Two structural variants matter:

- **Spend-based (the UK model).** The UK tests whether the spend is on goods or
  services **used or consumed in the UK**, largely regardless of the supplier's
  nationality — so a US crew member working in the UK generates qualifying
  spend on their UK days.
- **Residency-based (many US states).** The test is who was paid, not where the
  work happened, so importing a department head is expensive twice: their wage
  does not qualify and it displaces a local who would have.

Certification adds a second gate. The UK requires the film to pass a
points-based **cultural test** administered by the BFI (a pass mark out of a
fixed total, scored on setting, characters, language, and where the work is
done), with **interim certification** obtained before the credit can be
cash-flowed and **final certification** on completion.

### 6.2 What a production does structurally to qualify

This is corporate plumbing, and getting it wrong is one of the few unforced
errors that can cost a production 25% of its budget.

1. **A qualifying entity is formed** in the jurisdiction — in the UK a **Film
   Production Company (FPC)**, which must be within the corporate chain and
   responsible for the film from pre-production onward. In US states, an
   in-state LLC registered with the film office.
2. **The application is filed before the start date.** Almost every regime
   requires pre-approval, and many allocate from an annual pool on a
   first-come basis. Filing late can simply mean the money is gone.
3. **A production services agreement (PSA)** is signed between the offshore
   owner of the film and the local entity, so the local company incurs the
   spend and generates the credit.
4. **Every vendor contract and every crew deal memo is signed in the name of
   the qualifying entity.** This sounds trivial and is the classic disaster:
   contracts signed in the wrong company's name produce spend that does not
   qualify, discovered at audit, a year later.
5. **The accounting system is configured on day one** with a qualifying /
   non-qualifying flag on every cost code, so the claim is a report rather than
   a forensic reconstruction (§10.1).
6. **Residency evidence is collected as people are hired** — proof of address,
   tax registration — not chased after wrap when half the crew has moved on.
7. **The schedule is built to the rules.** Shooting days inside the territory,
   post booked locally to trigger an uplift, and a start date inside the
   certified window.

### 6.3 How the claim is made and audited

| Stage | What happens | Who | When |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Application / pre-approval** | Budget, schedule, financing plan, estimated qualifying spend submitted to the film office | Producer, incentive counsel, production accountant | Before principal photography |
| **Interim certification** | Where available, confirms the film will qualify; required by lenders before they will advance against the credit | Certifying body | During prep |
| **Ongoing tagging** | Every invoice, timecard and PO coded Q / non-Q as it is entered | Production accountant, assistant accountants | Weekly, throughout |
| **Wrap reconciliation** | The final cost report is mapped to the claim schedule; residency evidence and vendor invoices assembled | Production accountant | 4–12 weeks after wrap |
| **Independent audit** | An accounting firm on the jurisdiction's approved list tests a sample (often 100% of large items), verifies residency, vendor location, and category, and issues a report | Approved auditor | 4–10 weeks |
| **Submission** | Audited claim plus statutory accounts for the entity, final certification, and the delivery evidence | Producer's accountant | After audit |
| **Assessment** | The authority reviews and queries. Disallowances are argued line by line | Film office / revenue authority | 4–20 weeks |
| **Payment** | Cash, or a credit certificate to be sold | Treasury | **3–12 months after wrap**, sometimes longer |

**Transferable credits** (Georgia is the best-known example) add a step: the
credit certificate is sold to a local taxpayer through a broker, typically at
**88–93 cents on the dollar**, with the broker taking a fee. That discount is a
real cost and belongs in the budget, not in the optimism.

### 6.4 Bridging the cash-flow gap

The production spends the money over 3–6 months; the incentive arrives 6–18
months after that. Somebody has to fund the interval.

- A **tax credit lender** (a specialist fund or a bank) advances **85–92%** of
  the estimated net credit, secured on the credit itself and on the production
  entity.
- The advance requires an **opinion letter** from an approved accountant or
  law firm estimating the qualifying spend and the credit — and lenders
  typically insist the audit firm is one they recognise.
- Interest runs at **8–12%** for the whole period from drawdown to receipt,
  plus arrangement and legal fees. On a 25% incentive against a $20M budget,
  the interest alone can be **$300K–$600K**.
- Lenders discount for **shortfall risk**: they lend against a conservative
  estimate of qualifying spend, not the producer's.
- On smaller films where no lender will engage, the producer either **defers**
  the equivalent amount (paying crew and vendors late) or treats the incentive
  as **producer's profit** received after the fact — which means the film was
  actually financed at a smaller budget than its top sheet claims.

### 6.5 How incentives fail

| Failure | Mechanism | Typical size |
|---|---|---|
| **Spend disallowed at audit** | Non-local vendor, ATL over cap, airfares, a category the production assumed qualified | 2–15% of the claim |
| **Wrong contracting entity** | Deals signed in the parent's name, not the qualifying entity's | Can void a whole category |
| **Annual cap exhausted** | The jurisdiction's pool ran out before the application landed | 100% |
| **Rules changed mid-production** | A legislature amends or suspends the programme; grandfathering is not guaranteed | Variable, occasionally total |
| **Cultural test failed** | Points miscounted, or a late change (a location, a language, a post vendor) drops the score | 100% |
| **Start date moved outside the window** | Certification lapses and must be re-applied for, at the back of the queue | Delay, sometimes total |
| **Crew depth overestimated** | Not enough local crew exists, so people are imported and their spend does not qualify | 3–10% of the claim |
| **Audit late** | Payment slips a further six months; the bridge loan accrues interest the budget did not carry | Interest only, but it comes out of contingency |

The honest summary: **the incentive is not free money, it is a receivable with
a haircut, a delay, an interest cost, and an audit risk** — and productions
that treat it as a discount on the budget rather than as a financing
instrument are the ones that run out of cash in post.

## 7. Completion bonds

A **completion guarantor** (a specialist insurer) guarantees to the financiers
that the film will be completed and delivered on schedule and to specification,
or that their money will be returned.

- Fee: roughly **1.5–3%** of the budget above-the-line + below-the-line, often
  with a rebate if not called upon.
- The bond company **approves the budget, schedule, key crew, and the
  contingency** before closing, and monitors daily cost reports and progress.
- If the production overruns badly, the bond company can **take over the film**,
  replace the director or producer, and cut the film to a deliverable state.
  This right is rarely exercised but its existence disciplines the production.
- Strike-out clauses: bonds typically exclude force majeure and certain
  cast-illness risks, which are covered separately.

Studio films are usually **self-bonded** — the studio's balance sheet is the
guarantee.

### 7.1 What the guarantor reviews before it will bond

The guarantor's representative is almost always a former line producer or UPM,
and they read the package the way a line producer would, looking for the gap
between what the script demands and what the schedule and budget provide.

| Reviewed | What they are looking for |
|---|---|
| **Script** | Page count, scene count, night exteriors, water, children, animals, crowd, stunts, vehicle work, and the number of VFX shots implied but not budgeted |
| **Schedule** | Pages per day, company moves, days per location, whether the 1st AD has built recovery days, and whether the post schedule is real |
| **Budget** | Whether the rates are current, whether fringes are right, whether contingency is intact, and what has been quietly under-provided |
| **Director's record** | Has this director finished on schedule before? A first-time director is bondable, but usually only with a strong 1st AD and line producer attached |
| **Line producer and 1st AD** | The single most reactive item. Some directors are only bondable with specific people beside them |
| **Cast deals** | Availability, overage rates, medicals, and what happens if a date moves |
| **Post and VFX plan** | Vendor, shot count, delivery dates; VFX overruns are the modern bond's biggest exposure |
| **Insurance** | That the package exists, the deductibles are affordable, and cast cover is in place |
| **Locations and weather** | Historical weather for the shoot window; a monsoon season in the schedule is a priced risk |

The output of this review is not just yes or no. The guarantor routinely
**imposes conditions**: add two days to the schedule, replace the 1st AD,
increase the contingency, cut a sequence, restructure the post schedule, or
place a cap on VFX shot count. These conditions are the bond's real influence,
and they land before a foot is shot.

### 7.2 What the guarantor monitors, and how often

| Frequency | Document | What it reveals |
|---|---|---|
| **Daily** | **Daily Production Report (DPR)** — call and wrap times, scenes/pages/setups completed, overtime, meal penalties, absences | Whether the unit is making its day, and at what human cost |
| **Daily** | Daily cost summary | Immediate variance on labour and overtime |
| **Weekly** | **Cost report** — actuals, committed, estimate to complete, estimated final cost, variance (§10.1) | The trend, and the honesty of the estimate to complete |
| **Weekly** | Revised schedule / one-liner, and the "days ahead/behind" count | The leading indicator; everything else follows it |
| **As issued** | Script revisions (coloured pages) | Scope creep — the root cause of most overruns (§13) |
| **Weekly** | Cash-flow forecast against drawdown schedule | Whether the film runs out of money before it runs out of days |
| **Periodically** | Dailies access, production meeting attendance, set visits by the guarantor's rep | Judgement about whether the footage is usable and the director is in control |

A bond rep on a well-run film is a phone call a week. On a troubled one they
are on set.

### 7.3 What triggers intervention

The thresholds are contractual and specific. Common ones:

- **Cumulative days behind schedule** — often a formal notice at **2 days**
  behind, escalating sharply beyond that.
- **Contingency consumption** — e.g. more than **50%** of contingency drawn
  before **50%** of principal photography is complete.
- **Projected final cost above the strike price** — the moment the estimated
  final cost exceeds budget plus contingency, the guarantor is on risk.
- **Change of key personnel** without consent.
- **Material script change** that increases scope.
- **Failure to deliver required reports** — which the guarantor reads,
  correctly, as concealment.

The escalation ladder:

```
1. The phone call        — informal, to the line producer. Most problems end here.
2. Letter of concern     — copied to the producer and financiers. Formal record.
3. Imposed remedies      — schedule cut, scenes dropped, unit days reduced,
                           second unit removed, VFX shot count capped, a
                           guarantor-appointed production supervisor placed on set.
4. Co-signature control  — the guarantor becomes a signatory on the production
                           account; no cheque goes out without them.
5. Takeover notice       — formal assumption of control under the completion
                           agreement.
```

### 7.4 What a takeover actually looks like

Full takeovers are rare — a handful worldwide in a year — because everyone
loses. But the mechanics are precise, and the threat only works because
producers know them.

1. **Notice is served** on the producer and the financiers under the completion
   agreement. The producer's authority over the production account ends
   immediately.
2. **The guarantor's own line producer takes over physical production.** In
   practice this is often someone already embedded as a "supervisor" during the
   remedies phase.
3. **The producer can be removed** from decision-making, though rarely from
   credit. The director can be replaced; more commonly, the director stays but
   loses control over scope.
4. **The film is cut to the minimum deliverable.** This is the important part:
   the bond guarantees *delivery to specification*, not the film anyone
   intended. Scenes are dropped, a sequence is rewritten to be shot in a day,
   an ending is simplified, second unit does what first unit was going to do.
5. **The guarantor funds the overage** from its own balance sheet and steps into
   a recoupment position **ahead of everyone else** in the waterfall (§1.4) —
   so the overage is repaid before the equity sees a cent.
6. **Delivery is completed** by the guarantor's team, including post and the
   delivery schedule (§5.1).

Consequences beyond the film: a producer who has been taken over is
substantially harder to bond next time, and a director with a takeover on their
record loses access to the budget tier above them. The bond market is small and
has a long memory.

### 7.5 What makes a production bondable

Effectively the skill axis for a producer and line producer, seen from the
guarantor's side:

1. **A schedule that matches the script.** Pages per day consistent with the
   material, night work honestly counted, and recovery days built in rather
   than hoped for.
2. **A budget with no hidden holes.** Correct fringes, current rates, real
   VFX numbers, and a post schedule that is not three weeks of magic.
3. **The right 1st AD and line producer for this director.** The guarantor is
   underwriting a pairing, not a person.
4. **A locked script.** An unlocked script at the start of photography is the
   most reliable predictor of an overrun (§13).
5. **Transparent reporting.** Reports on time, bad news early. Concealment
   converts a schedule problem into a control problem.
6. **A track record of finishing.** Both the producer's and the director's.
7. **Contingency left intact.** Spending contingency in prep — the classic
   move to solve a budget the film was never going to make — leaves nothing
   for the shoot and is visible immediately.

## 8. Insurance

Mandatory and non-trivial. Typical package:

- **Cast insurance** — covers loss from a principal's death/illness/injury.
  Requires medicals for key cast; a star with a health history may be
  uninsurable or excluded, which can kill a film.
- **Negative/faulty stock, equipment, props/sets/wardrobe, third-party
  property damage, general liability, workers' compensation.**
- **Errors & omissions (E&O)** — protects against copyright, defamation, and
  right-of-publicity claims. Distributors require it; it requires clean chain
  of title and clearances.
- **Weather / cast interruption / civil authority** cover.

Combined production insurance package: broadly **1–3%** of budget, rising with
stunt, water, aerial, animal, and pyrotechnic content. Higher risk content also
attracts higher workers' comp classifications.

### 8.1 The package, line by line

Placed by a **specialist entertainment insurance broker**, not a general
broker, against an application prepared by the line producer and production
accountant.

| Cover | What it pays for | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Cast / essential element** | Extra expense caused by death, injury or illness of a named person — usually 2–5 people, sometimes including the director | The expensive, medically-gated line |
| **Negative / faulty stock, camera & processing** | Loss or corruption of exposed footage, including data loss from cards and drives | Modern claims are DIT and workflow failures, not lab accidents |
| **Props, sets, wardrobe** | Damage, loss, theft of production property | |
| **Miscellaneous equipment** | Rented and owned camera, lighting, grip | Rental houses require certificates before releasing kit |
| **Third-party property damage (TPPD)** | Damage to property in the production's care while filming | Very frequently claimed; a low deductible is worth paying for |
| **General liability** | Injury to the public and third parties; typically $1M per occurrence / $2M aggregate, higher on studio pictures | Locations and permits demand certificates naming them additional insured |
| **Workers' compensation / employers' liability** | Crew injury | Rate varies enormously by job classification; stunt performers are the highest, and one stunt-heavy sequence moves the whole premium |
| **Auto / picture vehicles** | Fleet and on-camera vehicles | |
| **Umbrella / excess** | Sits above the primary limits | Required by most studios and networks |
| **Errors & omissions (E&O)** | Copyright, trademark, defamation, privacy, right of publicity | $1M/$3M limits and a 3-year term are the common ask; the distributor is named |
| **Weather** | A specific named day or period lost to weather | Expensive, day-specific, and rarely bought except for a single irreplaceable exterior |
| **Civil authority / political risk** | Government action, unrest, evacuation | Standard for foreign shoots |
| **Aircraft, watercraft, animal mortality, drone endorsements** | Named-peril extensions | Each is separately underwritten and separately excluded from the base policy |

**E&O is gated on paperwork, not risk appetite.** Before it is bound, the
insurer wants a **chain of title opinion**, a **title report**, and a **script
clearance report** — produced by a specialist clearance company that reads the
script and flags every character name, business name, address, phone number,
licence plate, song reference, trademark, and any real person who might be
identifiable. Then: music licences (sync **and** master, matching the
territories and term of the distribution deals), appearance releases from
everyone on camera, location releases, and life-rights agreements. Missing E&O
prerequisites is a routine cause of delivery failure (§5.1).

### 8.2 The medical, and what it is actually for

Cast cover is underwritten person by person. For each **essential element**:

- A **medical examination by an approved physician**, usually 4–8 weeks before
  the start date and always before the deal goes unconditional. It is a
  standard insurance physical: history, physical exam, blood work, blood
  pressure, sometimes an ECG, and specific follow-ups for anything in the
  history.
- A **written questionnaire** covering prior conditions, surgeries,
  medications, substance-use treatment, and mental-health treatment history.
- **Age loading** — premiums rise steeply with age, and above roughly 70–75 a
  lead may only be insurable with a large deductible or a partial exclusion.
- The insurer may then issue an **exclusion endorsement**: cover for everything
  except the named condition. This is by far the most common outcome for talent
  with any medical history, and it is workable — the film is covered for a car
  accident but not for a recurrence of the condition.
- **Activity restrictions** flow back into the talent's contract: no private
  aircraft, no motorcycles, no skiing, no scuba, and often a requirement to
  use a double for anything above a defined risk level
  (`08-vfx-and-specialty.md` §3).

The medical is also a scheduling item. It requires the talent's time in a
specific city, and a star on location elsewhere can delay a financing close by
weeks purely because they cannot get to the doctor.

### 8.3 What makes talent uninsurable

Rarely a flat refusal; almost always a price or an exclusion. The cases that
genuinely stop a film:

| Situation | Underwriter's response |
|---|---|
| **Recent substance-use treatment or overdose** | Exclusion for anything related, sometimes a flat decline within 1–3 years; a producer can proceed only if someone eats the risk |
| **Serious chronic or progressive illness** | Exclusion, or decline for a lead whose absence would stop the film |
| **Recent major surgery or an active injury** | Deferral until cleared, which moves the start date |
| **Advanced age in a physically demanding lead** | Very high premium, big deductible, or an exclusion that makes the cover nearly worthless |
| **A history of claims on prior productions** | Loading, and reputational effects far beyond the premium |
| **Undisclosed history discovered later** | The claim is denied and the production carries the entire loss — which is why business affairs treats the questionnaire as a contractual representation |
| **Refusal to take the medical** | Effectively a decline; the deal cannot go unconditional |

Where cover is unobtainable and the film must proceed, the options are: the
producer **self-insures** the exposure, an equity investor accepts it, the
**completion bond carves it out** (leaving the financiers exposed), or the
role is recast. On studio films the studio simply absorbs it — another quiet
advantage of a balance sheet.

### 8.4 How a claim actually works

Using the most common significant claim — a lead actor falls ill mid-shoot:

| Step | What happens | Who | Timing |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The 1st AD and UPM record the lost time precisely on the DPR; the actor is seen by a doctor | 1st AD, UPM, production medic | Same day |
| 2 | **Notice to the insurer within 24–48 hours.** Late notice is a standard reason for denial | Production accountant / broker | Immediately |
| 3 | The unit reschedules around the absence — shooting other scenes, second unit, or "shooting around" the actor | 1st AD, director, line producer | Same day |
| 4 | The insurer appoints an **adjuster**, and frequently its own physician to examine the actor | Insurer | 1–5 days |
| 5 | The production documents the loss: schedule as it was, schedule as executed, the specific days lost, and the costs incurred to recover them | Production accountant, UPM | Ongoing |
| 6 | **Quantum** is computed as **extra expense** — the cost of the lost days plus the cost of the recovery (reshoot days, re-hiring crew, holding cast, additional location fees), less what would have been spent anyway | Adjuster with the accountant | 2–8 weeks |
| 7 | The **deductible** applies — typically the first day or two of production cost, or a fixed sum, and it is not small | | |
| 8 | Payment | Insurer | **Weeks to months** later |
| 9 | The completion bond watches all of it, because an uninsured or under-recovered loss lands on them | Guarantor | Throughout |

Two consequences practitioners live with. First, **the production funds the
loss before the insurer pays it**, so a large claim is a cash-flow event even
when it is fully covered — usually met out of contingency. Second, **claims
follow you**: a production company with a claims record pays more on its next
film, and the actor at the centre of a claim may find the next medical harder.

## 9. Budget anatomy: above and below the line

The **line** is a real line on the budget top sheet, historically dividing
creative elements negotiated individually from technical costs quoted by rate.

**Above the line (ATL)** — story & rights, producers, director, principal cast,
and their fringes. Typically **20–40%** of a studio budget; can be far more when
a star has a big quote.

**Below the line (BTL)** — everything else in production: crew, equipment,
locations, sets, transportation, travel/living, extras, stunts, VFX unit
photography, catering.

**Post & other** — editorial, VFX (frequently the largest single line on a
tentpole), music, sound, DI, titles, deliverables.

**Other charges** — insurance, completion bond, legal, financing costs,
contingency, studio overhead.

A conventional top sheet:

```
ABOVE THE LINE
  Story & Rights ................  1–3%
  Producers .....................  3–7%
  Director ......................  3–6%
  Cast ..........................  10–25%
BELOW THE LINE (production)
  Production staff, camera, grip/electric, art,
  set construction, set dressing, props, costume,
  hair/makeup, sound, locations, transport,
  extras, stunts, SFX, catering ..  30–45%
POST & VFX
  Editorial, VFX, music, sound, DI  15–50%  (VFX-driven)
OTHER
  Insurance, bond, legal, fringes,
  contingency (~10%), overhead ...  10–20%
```

**Fringes** — payroll taxes plus guild/union health & pension contributions
loaded onto every labour line. Commonly **20–35%** on top of gross wages.
Routinely forgotten by outsiders and never by line producers.

### 9.1 How a budget is physically structured

Film budgets are built in dedicated software — **Movie Magic Budgeting**,
Showbiz, or Hot Budget — and every budget in the industry has the same
three-level structure:

```
TOP SHEET          one line per account, plus the four subtotals and the total
   │
   ├── ACCOUNT      e.g. 3200 CAMERA — a page of detail lines
   │      │
   │      └── DETAIL LINE
   │             3201  Director of Photography   1  Flat   14 Weeks  9,500  = 133,000
   │             3202  Camera Operator           2  Flat   12 Weeks  4,200  = 100,800
   │             3210  Camera package rental     1  Allow  13 Weeks 18,000  = 234,000
   │
   └── GLOBALS      shooting days, prep weeks, wrap weeks, crew size,
                    fringe rates, exchange rate, contingency %
```

Every detail line is **amount × units × rate**, where the units are days,
weeks, or an allowance. The **globals** are the powerful part: changing
"shooting days" from 38 to 34 ripples through every weekly and daily line in
the file at once. This is why the schedule is the budget's real control, and
why the answer to "can we save $2M" is nearly always "cut four days".

Account numbering is conventional and largely shared across the industry, which
is what allows a studio executive to compare two budgets at a glance:

| Range | Contents |
|---|---|
| **1100–1500** | Story & rights, writers, producers, director, principal cast, ATL travel & living |
| **2000–2100** | Production staff (UPM, ADs, coordinator), extra talent |
| **2200–2400** | Art department, set design, set construction |
| **2500–2800** | Set operations (grip), special effects, set dressing, property |
| **2900–3000** | Wardrobe, makeup & hair |
| **3100–3300** | Electrical, camera, production sound |
| **3400–3600** | Transportation, location, production film & lab / digital media |
| **3700–3900** | Second unit, tests, facility/stage rentals |
| **4000–4900** | Post: editorial, music, sound post, VFX, DI, titles, deliverables |
| **6000–7000** | Insurance, legal, publicity, fringes, contingency, completion bond, overhead, financing costs |

The **cover sheet** carries the assumptions the numbers depend on — shooting
days, locations, unions, format, exchange rate, and the contingency rate — and
a budget circulated without its cover sheet is close to meaningless.

### 9.2 Who builds it, when, and how many times

**Who** — the **line producer** (or on a studio film, the UPM, sometimes with
a dedicated estimator), working from the **1st AD's schedule** and the script
breakdown (`04-preproduction.md` §§1–3). The production accountant takes over
the number once it is locked and never lets it change without paperwork.

**When** — a rough budget exists at packaging, a real one during financing, and
a **locked budget** at close. It is a contractual document: the bond is written
against it, the bank sizes the facility to it, and the studio's greenlight
approves it specifically.

**How many times** — a working film goes through five to fifteen budget
versions:

1. **Schematic** — a top sheet built from comparables and a page count, before
   there is a schedule. Accurate to ±25%.
2. **First real pass** — from the first schedule. It is always over, usually by
   20–40%, and an experienced line producer delivers it over on purpose,
   because the honest number is the only useful starting point.
3. **The cuts** — the painful phase. Days out of the schedule, locations
   consolidated, a unit removed, a sequence rewritten, a stage build converted
   to a location, VFX shot count capped.
4. **The approved budget** — signed off by the producer, financiers, studio
   physical production, and the bond.
5. **Revised budgets** — after any material change, formally issued so that the
   cost report has something to measure against (§10).

The four levers that actually move a budget, in descending order of power:

| Lever | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Shooting days** | The most powerful single lever; a day costs the entire crew, equipment, and location for a day. Cutting days cuts everything at once |
| **Cast cost** | One quote can consume the entire supporting ensemble and half the schedule flexibility |
| **Locations vs stage, and number of locations** | Company moves, travel and living, and build costs all key off this (`04-preproduction.md` §5.5) |
| **VFX shot count** | The only line that can grow after photography ends (`08-vfx-and-specialty.md` §1) |

### 9.3 Fringes, properly

Fringes are payroll costs loaded on top of gross wages, and they are not
optional. Directional US figures:

| Component | Typical |
|---|---|
| Employer payroll taxes (FICA, FUTA, SUI) | **8–11%** of wages, with wage caps on some elements |
| Workers' compensation | **2–6%** typical crew classifications; far higher for stunts and aerial |
| Guild pension & health — SAG-AFTRA | around **20%** of covered earnings, subject to contract caps |
| Guild pension & health — DGA | high-teens percentage of covered salary |
| Guild pension & health — WGA | low-to-mid teens percentage |
| IATSE / Teamsters | a fixed hourly contribution plus a percentage, varying by local and agreement |
| Payroll service handling fee | **0.3–0.75%** |
| Vacation and holiday pay | contractual, several percent |

Blended, this is the **20–35%** on labour that the top sheet carries as a
separate account. A budget quoted "before fringes" is understating a
labour-heavy film by roughly a quarter, and the mistake is common enough that
studio executives ask the question reflexively.

Non-US practice differs: UK productions carry **employer's National Insurance**
plus holiday pay and pension auto-enrolment, and the equivalent load lands
around **15–20%**, with different treatment for loan-out companies and for
crew engaged as self-employed under HMRC's film industry arrangements.

### 9.4 What a budget conceals, and where to look

Reading a budget is a skill. The places an experienced reader checks first:

1. **Shooting days against page count and scene count.** Three pages a day on a
   dialogue drama is comfortable; three pages a day on an action film with
   night exteriors is fiction.
2. **Contingency.** Is it 10%, and is it on the whole budget or only part of
   it? A contingency already spent in prep is the classic tell.
3. **Fringe rates.** Under-set fringes are the easiest way to make a budget
   land on a target number.
4. **Post and VFX.** A tentpole with a suspiciously round VFX number and no
   shot-count breakdown is carrying an unpriced liability.
5. **Overtime assumptions.** A budget built on a 12-hour day for a director who
   shoots 14 is short by several percent of the entire BTL.
6. **Travel and living.** Headcount × weeks × per diem is arithmetic; getting
   it wrong is one of the most common straightforward errors.
7. **The stunt, water, aerial, animal and child content**, which drive
   insurance, workers' comp, and schedule simultaneously.
8. **Currency.** A film budgeted in dollars and spent in another currency has
   an unhedged exposure that no line on the top sheet describes.

## 10. Contingency, overages, and cost reporting

- **Contingency** — typically **10%** of the budget, held for the unforeseen.
  Bond companies require it and control access to it.
- **Cost report** — issued weekly by the production accountant showing
  *actual + committed + estimate to complete* against budget, line by line.
  This is the film's real-time P&L and the document everyone above the
  production reads.
- **Overage** — spending above budget. Who pays depends on the deal: studio
  absorbs it; on an independent, the bond company steps in once contingency is
  exhausted.
- **Amortisation of overhead** — studios charge their own films an overhead
  fee (historically **10–15%** of the budget) and interest on the money
  advanced. This is a major driver of "why did that hit never break even" —
  see `11-money-accounting-and-participations.md`.

### 10.1 How the cost report is actually built, week by week

The **production accountant** runs a department of two to eight people
(assistant accountant, payroll accountant, accounts payable clerk, and on a
large film a first assistant accountant and a payroll assistant). The weekly
cycle is fixed and the whole production is organised around it.

| Day | What happens | Who |
|---|---|---|
| **Throughout** | Every commitment is raised as a **purchase order** by the department, approved by the UPM/line producer, and entered before the goods arrive. A PO raised late is a cost the report cannot see | HODs, coordinator, accounts |
| **Sat/Sun** | Timecards from the week close; the 2nd AD's cast time sheets and the department heads' crew timecards are collected and checked for overtime, turnaround violations and meal penalties | 2nd AD, HODs, payroll accountant |
| **Monday** | Payroll is transmitted to the payroll service; invoices received are coded to accounts and entered | Payroll accountant, AP clerk |
| **Tuesday** | The week is closed in the accounting system. Actuals, commitments and payroll are reconciled | Production accountant |
| **Tue/Wed** | **The estimate to complete is rebuilt** — line by line, against the current schedule, with the UPM and line producer walking the accountant through what has changed | Accountant, line producer, UPM |
| **Wed/Thu** | The cost report is drafted, the variances explained in a written commentary, and the producer reviews it before it goes out | Producer, line producer, accountant |
| **Thu/Fri** | Issued to producer, studio production executive or financiers, completion bond, and bank | Accountant |

The report's columns, and what each one means:

| Column | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Original budget** | The locked, approved number. Never changes |
| **Approved changes** | Formal amendments — script changes, added days, approved contingency draws — each with a change-order number |
| **Revised budget** | Original + approved changes. What performance is measured against |
| **Cost to date (actuals)** | Money actually spent and recorded |
| **Committed** | POs raised and contracts signed but not yet invoiced. The column outsiders forget, and the reason a "healthy" actuals number can be an illusion |
| **Estimate to complete (ETC)** | What the rest of the film will cost from here. **All the judgement lives here** |
| **Estimated final cost (EFC)** | Actuals + committed + ETC |
| **Variance** | EFC minus revised budget, per line and in total |

**The ETC is the entire skill.** Actuals are bookkeeping and committed is
clerical; the ETC is a forecast made by a person who has to decide whether the
back half of the schedule will go like the front half. An accountant who leaves
the ETC at budget while the unit runs two hours over every day is producing a
report that says everything is fine until the week it says the film is $3M
over. The characteristic pathology of a troubled production is not overspending
— it is **an ETC that only catches up with reality in the last three weeks**.

### 10.2 Who reads it, and what they do about it

| Reader | What they are looking for | What they can do |
|---|---|---|
| **Line producer / UPM** | Line-level variance and the trend in labour hours | Reallocate between accounts, cut scope, renegotiate vendors, change the schedule |
| **Producer** | Total EFC and the trajectory | Approve contingency draws, go to the financiers, cut a sequence, talk to the director |
| **Studio production executive** | EFC vs approved budget, and whether they will have to tell their boss | Approve an increase, impose cuts, send their own person to set |
| **Completion guarantor** | Days behind, contingency burn, ETC credibility (§7.3) | Letter of concern, imposed remedies, ultimately takeover |
| **Bank** | Cash-flow forecast vs drawdown schedule | Withhold a drawdown, require additional security |
| **Equity investor** | Whether their money is still last in line and getting further away | Very little, which is the nature of equity |
| **Director** | Usually does not read it | The director hears about it as "we are losing the Tuesday" |

The interventions available, in escalating order of pain: **reallocate** (move
money from an underspent account to an overspent one — "the art department is
paying for the crane"), **descope** (cut shots, scenes, a unit day, VFX shots,
a second unit), **draw contingency** (with the bond's or studio's consent),
**extend the schedule and fund it** (the expensive option), and finally
**increase the budget formally**, which on an independent means finding new
money and on a studio film means a difficult meeting.

### 10.3 The daily documents that precede the cost report

The cost report is a **trailing** indicator by one to two weeks. What people
actually watch every morning:

- **The daily production report (DPR)** — call, first shot, lunch, wrap;
  scenes, pages and setups completed; scenes still owed; overtime hours; meal
  penalties; cast worked; accidents. Circulated by 9am the following morning
  (`06-principal-photography.md` §2).
- **The daily cost** — a one-page summary of the day's labour and overtime.
- **The days ahead/behind count** and the **pages-per-day average** — the
  genuinely leading indicators. A unit that averaged 2.1 pages a day for the
  first two weeks against a schedule built on 3.0 is already over, and
  everybody knows it two weeks before the cost report says so.
- **The one-liner** — the current schedule in a page, reissued whenever it
  changes, which is often.

The rule of thumb that runs the whole system: **the first week of photography
predicts the shoot.** A unit that is a day behind at the end of week one is
almost never a day ahead at the end.

## 12. The line producer and the production accountant

Two roles hold the money side of a production together, and both are judged on
things that are invisible when done well.

### 12.1 The line producer / UPM

**Who** — on a studio film the **Unit Production Manager (UPM)** is a DGA
position reporting to the producer and the studio's physical production
department; on an independent the **line producer** is the same person with
more authority and a producer credit. Below them: the production coordinator,
the production accountant, the assistant directors on schedule, and every
department head on money.

**When** — engaged before financing closes (they build the budget the whole
structure is sized against), through prep, the shoot, and wrap. Frequently the
first person hired and the last to leave.

**What separates a good one from a bad one:**

1. **Budget honesty at the start.** Producing a number the film can actually be
   made for, rather than the number required to get it approved. A budget that
   is approved and cannot be met is a failure disguised as a success, and it is
   the most common one in the job.
2. **Schedule literacy.** Being able to convert a creative want into days
   instantly — "that's a two-day sequence if we lose the crane, four if we
   don't" — in the room, while the decision is still free.
3. **Knowing where the fat is.** Every budget has slack and every budget has
   lines that are already at the bone. A mediocre line producer cuts evenly
   across departments; a good one knows that cutting the art department by 10%
   costs more than cutting two days.
4. **Reading the director.** Knowing which director shoots twelve setups a day
   and which shoots forty, and building a schedule for the actual person rather
   than for an average one. This is why line producers and directors form
   long-term pairs.
5. **Saying no early rather than late.** A no in prep is free; the same no on
   the shooting day costs a morning and a relationship.
6. **Vendor and crew relationships.** Rates, deals, favours and availability at
   short notice. A line producer who has worked a region for fifteen years can
   assemble a crew in a week that a stranger cannot assemble in a month.
7. **No surprises.** The cardinal sin is a cost report the producer, studio or
   bond had not been warned about. Bad news delivered early is a problem; the
   same news delivered late is a competence question.
8. **Not being purely the enemy.** A line producer who is only ever "no" gets
   routed around — departments stop asking and start spending — and the first
   the money hears of it is the invoice.

**What good looks like:** the film is made for its number, the crew is not
destroyed, the departments got enough to do the work, and nobody above the
production was ever surprised. **What bad looks like:** a budget that was
never achievable, a schedule the unit fell behind on in week one, departments
that hoard because they don't trust the process, and a cost report that turns
bad all at once.

### 12.2 The production accountant

**Who** — hired by the line producer, but with a dotted line to the studio,
the completion bond and the bank, all of whom read their work. On a mid-size
film they run a department of three to five.

**When** — starts in prep, before most of the crew, and is one of the last
people working, often for months after wrap on the incentive claim and the
final cost report.

**What separates a good one from a bad one:**

1. **The estimate to complete.** Forecasting, not bookkeeping (§10.1). A good
   accountant's EFC in week three of a nine-week shoot is within a few percent
   of the final number; a bad one's is at budget until week eight.
2. **Coding discipline.** Every cost correctly assigned to account and — where
   an incentive is in play — correctly flagged qualifying or not, from the
   first invoice (§6.2). Reconstructing this at wrap costs a fortune and loses
   money at audit.
3. **Speed of close.** Getting the week closed and the report out on time,
   every week, without exception. A late cost report is read by financiers as
   a symptom.
4. **Cash-flow management.** Matching the bank drawdown schedule to actual
   requirements, so the production neither runs dry mid-week nor pays interest
   on money sitting idle.
5. **Payroll and guild compliance.** Correct rates, overtime, turnaround,
   meal penalties, and fringe contributions — errors here generate guild claims
   and back-payments long after wrap (`12-talent-labor-and-careers.md` §7).
6. **Audit-readiness from day one.** Filing as though an auditor will read
   everything, because on any incentivised film one will.
7. **Independence.** The accountant is quietly the financiers' person on the
   crew. An accountant who lets a producer soften a report has destroyed the
   only instrument the financiers have.

| Failure | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **ETC left at budget** | The overage appears all at once, too late to fix; the bond escalates immediately (§7.3) |
| **Commitments not captured** | POs raised and never entered; the report understates real exposure by hundreds of thousands |
| **Miscoded incentive spend** | Disallowed at audit, months after wrap, with no way to fix it |
| **Late or missing reports** | Read as concealment; triggers scrutiny disproportionate to the problem |
| **Payroll errors** | Guild claims, penalties, and a crew that stops trusting the office |
| **Cash-flow miss** | Vendors unpaid, crew payroll late — the fastest way to lose a crew mid-shoot |
| **No audit trail** | Delivery and incentive claims both stall; the bank's repayment slips and interest accrues |

## 13. How productions actually go over budget

Overruns are not random. They arrive through a small number of mechanisms, in a
fairly reliable order, and most of them are visible in advance to anyone who
knows what to look at.

### 13.1 The mechanisms

| # | Mechanism | Where it starts | Who sees it first | Typical size | Recoverable? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Script not locked at greenlight** | Development | The 1st AD, building a schedule against a moving target | 5–25% | No — it is the root cause of most of the rest |
| 2 | **Schedule built to the budget, not to the work** | Prep | Line producer, who often knew and was overruled | 5–20% | Only by adding days, which is the thing being avoided |
| 3 | **Prep too short** | Prep | Every HOD | 3–10% | No; it converts into shooting-day inefficiency |
| 4 | **Contingency spent in prep** | Prep | Production accountant | Up to the whole contingency | No |
| 5 | **A slow first week** | Week 1 | 1st AD, in the day count | Sets the pace for the whole shoot | Rarely |
| 6 | **Overtime and turnaround creep** | Week 1 onward | Payroll accountant | 3–12% of BTL labour | Only by changing how the day is run |
| 7 | **Setup count / director's coverage habits** | Week 1 onward | 1st AD | Compounds daily | Only by the director changing method |
| 8 | **Weather and location loss** | Shoot | Location manager, 1st AD | 1–8% | Partly, with cover sets and insurance |
| 9 | **Cast illness or unavailability** | Shoot | 2nd AD | 1–5% | Insurable, but with a deductible and a cash-flow gap (§8.4) |
| 10 | **Added scenes / reshoots during the shoot** | Shoot | Line producer | 2–10% | No |
| 11 | **Additional photography after wrap** | Post | Editor and director, then the studio | 2–15% | No; and the crew and cast have to be re-hired at new rates |
| 12 | **VFX shot count growth** | Post | VFX producer | 5–40% on a VFX-heavy film | No — the biggest post-side mechanism (`08-vfx-and-specialty.md` §1) |
| 13 | **Music clearance discovered late** | Post | Music supervisor | $50K–$2M+ | Partly, by replacing tracks |
| 14 | **Delivery items unbudgeted** | Delivery | Post supervisor | $50K–$250K | No (§5.1) |
| 15 | **Incentive shortfall at audit** | 6–18 months post-wrap | Production accountant | 2–15% of the claim | No (§6.5) |
| 16 | **Currency and interest** | Throughout | Producer | 1–5% | Only by hedging, which small films do not do |

### 13.2 The order in which it happens

```
DEVELOPMENT   The script isn't finished, but the date is set.
     │
PREP          The schedule is cut to fit the budget rather than the script.
     │        Prep is shortened to protect the start date.
     │        Contingency is quietly used to close the last gap.
     │
WEEK 1        The unit is a day behind. Everyone says it's a slow start.
     │
WEEKS 2–4     Days run 13–14 hours. Overtime and meal penalties become
     │        structural. The cost report shows a small variance and an
     │        ETC still at budget.
     │
MID-SHOOT     Two lost days to weather. Contingency is now half gone at
     │        the halfway point — the bond's trigger (§7.3). A letter arrives.
     │
WEEKS 5–7     Scenes are cut to make the schedule. The director shoots
     │        less coverage than they want. The film gets thinner.
     │
WRAP          Delivered on the revised schedule, over the original budget.
     │
POST          The edit reveals the scenes cut in week 6 were needed.
     │        Additional photography is scheduled. VFX shot count rises
     │        as fixes replace what could not be shot.
     │
DELIVERY      Music and delivery items nobody budgeted. The incentive
              claim comes back reduced.
```

The moral practitioners draw from this: **almost every overrun is paid for
twice** — once in money and once in the film, because the standard remedy for
being over is descoping, and descoping is how a film gets worse.

### 13.3 The leading indicators

If only four numbers can be watched, these are the ones:

1. **Days ahead/behind**, cumulative. The single best predictor.
2. **Average pages per day** against the schedule's assumption.
3. **Average shooting-day length** and the overtime percentage.
4. **Contingency consumed** as a fraction of schedule completed.

All four are available daily and all four move well before the cost report
does.

## 14. How a film gets shut down mid-production

Rare, but the mechanics matter because the *possibility* shapes every deal
above.

### 14.1 Suspension versus abandonment

- **Suspension (hiatus)** — the production stops, intending to resume. Crew are
  released or held on partial pay, sets are stored, cast deals are suspended
  under the force-majeure or suspension clauses in their contracts. Costs
  continue: storage, key personnel retained, insurance, interest.
- **Abandonment** — the film stops permanently. The negative, such as it is,
  goes to a vault; the rights revert to whoever the security documents say they
  do; and the sunk cost becomes a write-off.

### 14.2 What causes it

| Cause | How it presents | Typical outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Financing failure** | An equity investor misses a drawdown, or a buyer's LC fails; the bank stops funding | Immediate suspension; usually terminal on an independent |
| **Death or serious illness of a lead** | Mid-shoot | Insured (§8), then either recast and reshoot, rewrite around the absence, or abandon |
| **Scandal involving a principal** | A story breaks; distributors and financiers get nervous | Suspension, then recast-and-reshoot or shelving; morals clauses are the contractual mechanism |
| **Force majeure** | Pandemic, war, natural disaster, fire, a location made unusable | Suspension; insurance response varies enormously and pandemic cover largely disappeared after 2020 |
| **Industry-wide strike** | Guild action stops writers or performers working (`12-talent-labor-and-careers.md` §7) | Suspension for the duration; unstruck work sometimes continues |
| **Completion bond withdrawal or takeover** | Following the escalation in §7.3 | The film is finished, but not as intended |
| **Studio regime change** | New leadership kills an inherited project, occasionally after the start | Abandonment, written off in the quarter |
| **Legal injunction** | A rights dispute, an unsigned co-writer, a competing claim to underlying material | Suspension until resolved, which can take years |
| **Loss of the incentive** | Certification refused or the window missed, blowing a hole in the stack | Suspension while the producer looks for replacement money |

### 14.3 What a shutdown physically involves

Within 24–72 hours of the decision:

1. **The unit wraps.** The 1st AD calls it; the UPM issues notice. Crew notice
   periods are in the deal memos — commonly one to two weeks, sometimes a day.
2. **Equipment goes back** to the rental houses, and the rentals stop billing
   the moment it is returned, which is why it goes back fast.
3. **Sets are struck or stored.** Storage is a live cost, and the decision to
   strike a set is effectively a decision that the film will not resume in its
   present form.
4. **Locations are released**, contracts terminated, restoration completed.
5. **Cast are suspended** under their contracts; pay-or-play obligations may
   continue regardless (§2.1).
6. **The footage is secured** — drives, backups and the lab or post house are
   put on notice, and the lab access letter given to the bank becomes
   operative.
7. **The insurance claim is opened** immediately (§8.4), and an adjuster is
   appointed within days.
8. **The bank is notified**; drawdowns stop and the security documents govern
   what happens to the asset.

### 14.4 What happens afterwards

- **Insurance may cover abandonment** where the cause is an insured peril
  (death or illness of an essential element, most commonly). It covers the sunk
  cost, not the lost opportunity, and the insurer then owns a say in whether
  the film restarts.
- **Restart is expensive and rare.** Recasting a lead means reshooting
  everything they appear in; crew and cast must be re-hired at current rates
  and current availability; locations and sets must be rebuilt. Restarted films
  routinely cost 40–100% more than their original budget.
- **The shelf.** A completed-but-unreleased or half-shot film sitting in a
  vault is a real category. It stays there because releasing it costs more than
  writing it off, or because a legal position depends on it staying unseen.
- **The write-off** appears in the studio's quarter as an abandonment charge,
  or on an independent as the total loss of the equity and, depending on the
  security, of the lenders' position too.
- **Reputational cost** falls hardest on the producer and the completion bond.
  Both are asked about it on every subsequent film.

## 15. Three capital stacks, worked

Directional, and simplified, but the shapes are real.

### 15.1 A $3M independent drama, no pre-sales

```
Equity (private investors, 6 individuals)      $1,400,000   47%
Tax credit (30% state, discounted at 88%)        $700,000   23%
Producer/talent deferrals                        $500,000   17%
Distribution advance (a small domestic deal)     $250,000    8%
Director/producer fees deferred to profit        $150,000    5%
```

- No completion bond (too small; investors accept the risk), no sales agent
  until the film is finished, no bank.
- The whole plan is a **festival gamble** (§5.2). If it premieres well, an
  acquisition repays the equity; if it doesn't, foreign sells territory by
  territory over two years for a fraction of the estimates.
- **Where it fails:** the film is finished, is fine rather than remarkable, and
  no acquisition arrives. The equity is lost. This is the single most common
  outcome in independent film.

### 15.2 A $25M pre-sold genre film with one star

```
Foreign pre-sales (12 territories, MGs)       $11,000,000   44%
Tax incentive (25%, lent at 90%)               $5,600,000   22%
Gap loan (against unsold territories)          $3,000,000   12%
Equity                                         $4,000,000   16%
Deferrals (star's second-half fee, producers)  $1,400,000    6%
```

- Bank facility across the pre-sales and incentive; **gap capped at 12%** of
  budget and secured by unsold low estimates of roughly $7M.
- Completion bond at 2% (**$500K**), insurance at 1.5% (**$375K**), finance and
  legal about **$1.8M** all in — roughly 11% of the budget consumed before
  production.
- **Where it fails:** the star's dates move, three IDAs release under their
  essential-elements clause, the gap widens beyond what the lender will take,
  and the film is six weeks from a start date with no money.

### 15.3 A $180M studio tentpole

```
Studio balance sheet                         $126,000,000   70%
Co-financing partner (slate deal, 25%)        $45,000,000   25%
State/foreign incentives (net, retained)       $9,000,000    5%
```

- No bond (self-bonded), no bank, no CAM, no interparty agreement.
- The studio charges the film an **overhead fee** of 10–15% and **interest** on
  the advanced money, both of which sit in the negative cost the participations
  are computed against (`11-money-accounting-and-participations.md` §5).
- P&A of **$100M–$150M** on top, spent by a different department against a
  different budget (`09-marketing-and-distribution.md` §1).
- **Where it fails:** not in financing — the money is never in doubt. It fails
  in the cost report (§13) and at the box office
  (`10-theatrical-release-and-box-office.md` §11).

### 15.4 What the three have in common

1. **The last money in has the most risk and the least control** — equity in
   15.1 and 15.2, the studio's own capital in 15.3.
2. **Certainty is bought by selling upside.** The pre-sold film is the safest
   to make and the least rewarding to have made.
3. **The cost of money is a line in the budget**, and on independents it is a
   large one.
4. **Every structure is ultimately underwritten by the same thing** — a
   schedule that can be met and a script that is finished.
