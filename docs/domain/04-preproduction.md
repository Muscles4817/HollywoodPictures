# 04 — Pre-Production

*Turning a screenplay into a shootable plan.* Prep is where a film is actually
designed, costed, and de-risked. Almost every problem that appears on set was
either created or prevented here.

> **Domain reference.** Real industry, not this game. See `README.md`.

---

## 1. How long prep lasts

Prep begins when the film is greenlit (or soft-greenlit) and ends on the first
day of principal photography.

| Film type | Typical prep |
|---|---|
| Micro-budget indie | 3–5 weeks |
| Independent feature ($5–20M) | 6–10 weeks |
| Studio mid-budget | 8–12 weeks |
| Effects-led tentpole | 4–12 months (VFX and design start far earlier) |
| Period / heavy build / creature | 4–9 months |

Prep is not uniform: the director, producer, production designer, and 1st AD
start first; most crew are hired in a staggered ramp, and the largest
departments (art, construction, costume) arrive well before camera and grip.

**Prep drives quality more than shoot length does.** A shortened prep is the
most reliable predictor of an overrunning, compromised shoot, because
construction, costume manufacture, location securing, and rehearsal are all
serial processes that cannot be parallelised past a point.

### 1.1 The ramp — who starts when

The useful mental picture is not "a team preps the film" but **a staircase**:
a core of four or five people from day one, then departments switching on in
the order their lead times demand, then a very large number of people arriving
in the last fortnight. Below, **T–n** means *n weeks before day one of
photography*, sized for a mid-budget studio film with a ten-week prep. Scale
the whole staircase up or down with the schedule; the **order** is what holds.

| Starts | Who | Why then |
|---|---|---|
| Continuous | Producer, executive producer, writer, casting director | They never left development (`02-development.md` §7) |
| T–26 to T–52 | VFX supervisor, previs supervisor, creature/animatronic shop | On an effects-led film the design *is* the VFX design (`08-vfx-and-specialty.md` §1) |
| T–16 to T–24 | Production designer, construction coordinator, supervising art director | The longest serial chain on the picture |
| T–14 to T–16 | Line producer / UPM, production accountant, production coordinator | The office and the payroll have to exist before anyone can be hired into them |
| T–10 to T–16 | Director's contracted prep period begins | Guaranteed by the DGA agreement on a sliding scale by budget; on an indie it is whatever was negotiated |
| T–10 to T–14 | 1st AD, location manager, art directors, set designers | Breakdown, board, and location list all block each other |
| T–8 to T–12 | Costume designer, prop master, stunt coordinator, SFX supervisor, picture-vehicle coordinator | Manufacture, training, and rigging lead times |
| T–6 to T–10 | DP, set decorator, assistant location managers, additional ADs | The DP's prep is shorter than people expect and is mostly tests, scouts and the lighting plan |
| T–4 to T–8 | Makeup and hair department heads, transport captain, 2nd AD, greensman | |
| T–2 to T–4 | Gaffer, key grip, camera operator, 1st AC, rigging crews, standby carpenter/painter/props | Equipment orders, the pre-rig, and the tech scout |
| T–1 to T–2 | Sound mixer, boom operator, script supervisor, most of camera/grip/electric, set costumers, medics, catering | Paid prep is expensive; they arrive for the tech scout and the production meeting and no earlier |
| T–0 | Background performers, dailies crew, additional PAs | Booked days ahead, not weeks |

**Prep weeks are a negotiated line in every deal memo.** A HOD is hired for,
say, "8 prep + 45 shoot + 2 wrap", and each of those weeks is real money. The
single most common budget fight in prep is a HOD asking for two more prep
weeks and the UPM refusing, because the UPM is paying for them and the shoot
is the department that will pay for their absence.

### 1.2 What actually happens, week by week

A ten-week studio prep, department by department. Real preps overlap, slip,
and double back; the sequence is what survives.

| Week | The spine of it | True by the end of it |
|---|---|---|
| **T–10** | Production office opens; accounts and payroll set up; 1st AD breaks the script; PD delivers first visual response and the **set list**; LM takes the location brief | Everyone knows how many sets, how many locations, and how many shooting days are being aimed at |
| **T–9** | First stripboard; first scheduled budget; photo scouts return packages; art department starts concept art on the two or three sets that need selling; costume designer reads and starts research | There is a number and a board, both wrong, both arguable |
| **T–8** | Budget meeting with the studio; the "find $4M" conversation; script pass for budget begins; construction bids the first drawings; casting sessions for supporting roles | The gap between the budget and the number is named |
| **T–7** | Director's scouts; sets are chosen as build-or-location; long-lead materials ordered; costume fittings begin for cast already signed; stunt coordinator starts action design | Nothing further can change without costing money |
| **T–6** | Construction starts on the first-block sets; VFX methodology agreed shot by shot; prosthetics life-casts taken; picture vehicles sourced | The critical path is visible and someone owns each item on it |
| **T–5** | Board rebuilt around confirmed cast dates and confirmed locations; DOOD issued; permits filed; second wave of casting; DP joins and starts the lighting plan with the gaffer | The schedule stops being aspirational |
| **T–4** | Camera, lens, and format tests shot and screened; costume and makeup tests; rehearsal (if there is any) begins; catering, transport, and facilities booked | The film's look is agreed and recorded, not described |
| **T–3** | Tech scouts, location by location, with all HODs; equipment orders finalised; stunt rehearsals; previs sequences locked | Every department has stood in every location for the first block |
| **T–2** | Script locked and coloured revisions begin; final budget approved by studio and bond; the **production meeting**; safety meetings and risk assessments | Every disagreement that can be found in a room has been found |
| **T–1** | Sets handed over and pre-lit; dressing in; crew arrives en masse; first call sheet issued; day-one location prep-rigged | Day one is shootable, and the 1st AD says so out loud |

On a tentpole the same sequence runs at 3–4× the elapsed time, with the
front of it (design, VFX, previs, creature build) starting a year or more
out and the back of it — tech scouts, tests, production meetings — still
compressed into the final month.

### 1.3 The serial chains

Prep cannot be compressed by adding people, because the expensive parts of it
are chains where each link needs the previous one finished.

```
  DIRECTOR DECIDES ─→ drawing ─→ approval ─→ order ─→ build ─→ paint
       ▲                                                        │
       │                                                        ▼
  the pacing item                                    dress ─→ HAND OVER ─→ shoot
  for everything                                              (2–5 days
  downstream                                                   before)
```

| Chain | Steps | Irreducible elapsed |
|---|---|---|
| Stage set | Decision → drawing → costing → approval → materials → build → plaster/paint → age → dress → hand-over | 6–12 weeks (hero set: 3–6 months) |
| Bespoke costume | Design → **actor cast** → fitting → toile → make → fitting → break down → multiples | 6–10 weeks per costume |
| Prosthetic character | Life cast → sculpt → mould → run pieces → test on camera → refine | 10–20 weeks |
| Full creature suit / animatronic | Design → maquette → engineering → build → puppeteer rehearsal | 6–12 months |
| Picture vehicles | Source → acquire → mechanical prep → paint/dress → duplicates → camera rigging | 4–12 weeks |
| Permits | Application → review → community notice → issue | 3 days (routine city) to 6 months (landmark, rail, motorway, national park) |
| Animal work | Casting → training to the specific action → familiarisation with crew and set | 4–16 weeks |
| Previs sequence | Storyboard → blocking → iteration → techvis | 6–16 weeks |
| Armour and weapons | Design → pattern → fabrication → fitting → stunt-safe duplicates | 8–20 weeks |

**The actor-cast link is the one that catches people.** Nothing bespoke can be
made until the person who will wear it is signed, so a casting delay
propagates straight into costume, prosthetics, and stunt-double matching. A
lead cast four weeks before the shoot arrives with rental clothes and no
prosthetic, whatever the design said.

### 1.4 What "not enough prep" concretely costs

| Prep item short-changed | Where the bill actually arrives | Rough size |
|---|---|---|
| Breakdown rushed or not re-done after rewrites | Elements arrive on the day that nobody ordered — no picture car, no crowd, no hero prop | Half a day to a full day lost, each time |
| Locations not fully scouted | The unit discovers the power, parking, sound, or curfew problem at 7am | 1–3 hours a day, every day at that location |
| Tech scout skipped or attended by juniors | Departments turn up with different plans; the crane doesn't fit; the rig can't be hung | A day, and a very bad mood |
| Drawings late → construction late | Set handed over on the shooting morning, wet paint, no time to pre-light | 1–2 hours off the front of the day, plus painters on overtime |
| Insufficient costume multiples | The scene stops while a costume is washed and dried | Half a day, or a scene split across two days with continuity risk |
| No camera / makeup tests | The look is argued about in dailies during week two, and something gets reshot | A reshoot day and a permanently nervous DP |
| No rehearsal | Line changes and blocking discovered on the floor, with 90 people watching | 30–60 minutes per scene, compounding |
| Schedule built before cast was confirmed | Rebuild mid-shoot, actor holds and drop/pickups blow up | Six figures in cast cost with no change on screen |
| Production meeting cut short | Departmental misunderstandings surface on the day, in front of the director | Unquantifiable; this is where "the film went wrong" stories start |

**The arithmetic nobody disputes and everybody ignores.** A prep week for the
core prep team on a mid-budget studio film costs roughly **$50–150K**. A lost
shooting day on the same film costs **$150–400K**, and on a tentpole
**$500K–1M+**. Buying one extra prep week to avoid half a lost shooting day
pays for itself. Line producers make this argument on every picture and lose
it often, because prep money is spent before there is any footage to show for
it, and shoot money is spent when the studio is already committed.

## 2. Script breakdown

**Who** — the **1st AD** owns it, usually with the **UPM/line producer** doing
a parallel pass for money and the **2nd AD** absorbing it for the day-to-day.
On big films a dedicated **scheduler** or an outside breakdown service does the
first mechanical pass and the 1st AD corrects it. In the UK the same work sits
with the **1st AD** and the **production manager**. The **script supervisor**
does an independent breakdown for continuity and story days, and where the two
disagree, someone has misread the script.

**When** — the first pass is done in the first days of prep, before anything
else can be costed or scheduled, and it is redone after every meaningful
script revision for the rest of prep and most of the shoot. It blocks the
board, the budget, every department's order list, and clearance.

**How long** — a careful first pass on a 100-page contemporary drama is
**2–4 days**. A 130-page action or effects script is **1–2 weeks**, because
half the work is deciding what a line actually means. A re-break after a
substantial rewrite is 1–3 days. Nobody schedules enough time for this and it
is the cheapest work on the film.

Every scene is decomposed into **elements**, colour-coded by convention on the
breakdown sheet:

| Element | Examples |
|---|---|
| Cast (speaking) | Numbered; "day players" vs series regulars |
| Background / extras | Crowd sizes, special ability extras |
| Stunts | Falls, fights, driving, fire |
| Vehicles | Picture vehicles, action vehicles, process |
| Props | Anything an actor handles |
| Set dressing | Anything they don't |
| Wardrobe | Including doubles/multiples for damage or stunts |
| Hair & makeup | Prosthetics, blood, ageing |
| Special effects (practical) | Rain, wind, squibs, breakaways, pyro |
| VFX | Every shot requiring post work |
| Animals | Wrangler, humane oversight |
| Music/sound | Playback, source music |
| Special equipment | Crane, techno, drone, underwater, Steadicam |
| Security / medics / permits | |

**Page counts** are measured in **eighths of a page** — a scene is "2 4/8
pages." This is the unit in which a day's work is estimated and in which
progress is reported.

Scene headers give **INT/EXT** and **DAY/NIGHT**, which are the two variables
that dominate scheduling.

### 2.1 How a breakdown is actually performed

The mechanics are unglamorous and almost unchanged since the studio era, even
though the coloured pencils are now a database.

| # | Step | Who | Detail |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Number the scenes** | Production office / 1st AD | Every scene gets a permanent number. Scenes added later become **A12, B12**; scenes cut become **12 — OMITTED**. Numbers never change again, because the whole film's paperwork points at them |
| 2 | **Line the script** | 1st AD | Read scene by scene, marking every physical element — historically with a set of coloured pencils, now by highlighting in scheduling software |
| 3 | **Establish script days** | 1st AD with script supervisor | Assign every scene to a **story day** (D1, N1, D2 …). This is a reading of the script, not a lookup, and it is frequently ambiguous |
| 4 | **Measure page counts** | 1st AD | Each page is divided into eight vertical eighths; a scene is measured to the nearest eighth, and anything shorter counts as 1/8 |
| 5 | **Create a breakdown sheet per scene** | 1st AD | Header (scene no., INT/EXT, D/N, set, location, script day, page count, one-line synopsis) plus every element in its category |
| 6 | **Cost-flag the sheets** | UPM / line producer | Marking which scenes carry the money: stunts, crowd, VFX, animals, minors, SFX, company moves |
| 7 | **Sanity pass with the director** | 1st AD, director | "When you say the room is destroyed, do you mean we destroy it, or we shoot the aftermath?" This conversation is worth its weight in shooting days |
| 8 | **Distribute** | Production office | Every HOD receives the breakdown and builds their own department list from it |
| 9 | **Re-break on every revision** | 1st AD | Coloured revision pages (§11.3) are re-broken and the board and budget rippled |

### 2.2 The marking conventions

The traditional colour set is still the vocabulary, even where nobody holds a
pencil. Conventions vary between productions and software; the discipline
matters more than the colour.

| Mark | Element |
|---|---|
| Red | Cast (speaking) |
| Yellow | Background / atmosphere |
| Green | Silent bits / special-ability extras |
| Orange | Stunts |
| Blue | Special effects (practical) |
| Purple | Props |
| Pink | Vehicles and animals |
| Brown | Sound, music, playback |
| Circle | Wardrobe |
| Asterisk | Hair and makeup |
| Box | Special equipment |
| Underline | Production notes and everything else |

### 2.3 Script days — the sleeper item

A **script day** is a day in the *story*. The scenes making up story day 4 may
shoot in weeks two, five, and nine, at three locations, months apart. Every
continuity decision hangs off this number:

- **Costume** builds its costume plot as character × script day, and orders
  multiples per script day, not per scene.
- **Hair and makeup** track injury progression, beard growth, and hairstyle
  changes against script days.
- **Props** track which items a character is carrying on which day.
- **Script supervisor** polices it on the floor.

Scripts are routinely vague about the passage of time, and the breakdown is
where that vagueness has to be resolved into a number. An unresolved script
day means an actor turns up in the wrong shirt in week nine, and either the
scene is reshot or the audience sees it.

### 2.4 Eighths, and their limits

An eighth is roughly one inch of page. Its virtue is that it is objective and
comparable; its vice is that it measures typography, not work.

- A 3/8 dialogue scene between two people at a table is 40 minutes.
- A 3/8 scene reading "*They fight. He goes through the window.*" is two days.
- A 6/8 scene with 200 extras, rain, and a crane move is a day, plus a
  wet-down and a pre-rig.

Experienced 1st ADs therefore schedule on **setups**, not pages, and use page
counts to report progress rather than to plan it. A board that reads "3 1/8
pages" every day and never varies is the sign of a schedule built by
arithmetic rather than by reading.

### 2.5 What the breakdown feeds

This is the reason it comes first. Almost every ordering decision on the film
is derived from it, and an error here propagates into every department at once.

| Consumer | What they build from it | When they need it |
|---|---|---|
| 1st AD | Stripboard, DOOD, one-liner, shooting schedule (§3) | Immediately |
| Line producer / UPM | Scheduled budget (§4) | Week 1–2 of prep |
| Production designer | Set list — build / location / redress / VFX extension (§6) | Week 1 |
| Set decorator, prop master | Dressing and prop lists, hero-prop identification, multiples | Weeks 2–4 |
| Costume designer | Costume plot by character × script day; multiples count (§7) | Weeks 2–4 |
| Hair and makeup | Continuity plot, prosthetic list, application-time estimates | Weeks 3–6 |
| Casting | Role list by tier, day-player count, background counts (§8) | Week 1 |
| Stunt coordinator | Stunt list, double requirements, rehearsal and rigging days | Weeks 2–4 |
| SFX supervisor | Practical effects list, rain/wind/pyro rigs, permits | Weeks 2–4 |
| VFX supervisor | Shot list, methodology per shot, vendor bid (`08-…` §2) | As early as possible; the bid depends on it |
| Location manager | Location list, permit lead times, prep/wrap day counts (§5) | Week 1 |
| Transport | Picture vehicles, unit vehicles, move plan | Weeks 4–6 |
| Legal / clearance | Named brands, logos, signage, artwork, music, real persons | Weeks 2–6 (clearances are slow) |
| Insurance and bond | Hazardous sequence list, minors, animals, aerial, water | Before the budget is approved |

### 2.6 What gets missed, and what it costs

The failure mode of a breakdown is almost never a missed *stated* element. It
is the **implied** element — the thing the script assumes rather than names.
This table is the difference between a good 1st AD and an adequate one.

| Line in the script | What it actually implies | Cost if missed |
|---|---|---|
| "He pulls up outside." | Picture vehicle, an actor cleared and insured to drive (or a pod/process rig), road closure, traffic control, police, insurance rider | The shot becomes a static one, or the day is lost |
| "A crowd gathers." | 60–250 background, extras casting, costume for every one, holding area, catering, extra 2nd/3rd ADs, toilets, transport | Five figures appearing three days out, or a "crowd" of eleven |
| "She reads the letter." | Hero prop legible in a 4K close-up, period-correct hand, 6–20 identical copies for handling, and legal clearance of the text | The take stops while props writes a letter |
| "The TV is on." | Cleared footage or purpose-shot material, on-set playback, sync, and a licence | The screen is off in the finished film, or a fix in the DI |
| "His phone rings." | Practical handset, a designed fictional OS, screen content matching in every angle, and either live playback or a VFX comp | A VFX shot nobody budgeted, in every coverage angle |
| "A dog barks." | Trained animal, wrangler, welfare oversight, and the acceptance that the dog decides the pace | Half a day |
| "Rain." | SFX rain towers, water supply and drainage, street wet-down, costume multiples, hair/makeup reset, and a day that runs at half speed | A day becomes two |
| "They eat dinner." | Food styling, fresh multiples every take, dietary restrictions, and plate continuity | Slow, and continuity errors that show |
| "The window shatters." | Breakaway glass, SFX technician, safety brief, costume multiples, clean-up, and a location that will permit it | Deferred to a second unit day that doesn't exist |
| "A newspaper headline reads…" | Graphic design, period typesetting, masthead clearance, multiples | A blurred prop, or a legal note in post |
| "EXT. STREET — NIGHT", 1962 | Every car, sign, shopfront, aerial, and extra in shot; probably a VFX skyline; probably a full street lock-off | The single largest cost line on many period films |
| "Later that day." | A script day, a costume continuity obligation, and a lighting continuity obligation | Wrong shirt, wrong light, in week nine |
| "INT. HOSPITAL" (once, on page 88) | A whole set or location for one scene — the classic candidate for a budget pass (§4.5) | An entire location booked for two-thirds of a page |

### 2.7 What separates a good breakdown from an adequate one

1. **Reading for the implied element.** The good 1st AD breaks down what the
   scene *requires*, not what the writer typed. This is the whole skill.
2. **Knowing which elements are cost drivers.** Night exteriors, crowd,
   minors, animals, water, fire, and company moves are where the money is.
   Flagging them in week one changes the film; flagging them in week eight
   changes nothing.
3. **Pessimism about VFX.** Anything that could be a VFX shot is one until
   somebody senior says otherwise, in writing.
4. **Resolving ambiguity early, in a conversation.** The difference between
   "we destroy the room" and "we see the destroyed room" is a week of
   construction and an SFX package, and it is one question.
5. **Consistency of naming.** "The Diner", "Sal's", and "the coffee shop"
   being one set — or three — determines the entire board. Sloppy set naming
   silently produces phantom locations in the budget.
6. **Re-breaking rigorously.** A revision that adds two lines can add a
   location, a day player, and a stunt. Departments work from the last
   breakdown they were sent, not the last draft they read.
7. **Distributing it and then chasing it.** A breakdown that sits in a
   coordinator's outbox has done nothing.

## 3. Scheduling

**Who** — the **1st AD** builds the board, in continuous negotiation with the
**UPM/line producer** (who prices every version of it) and the **director**
(who has to be able to shoot it). The **2nd AD** maintains it day to day once
shooting starts.

**When** — a first pass exists as soon as there's a breakdown, and is rebuilt
repeatedly through prep as cast, locations, and budget move. It is never
finished: the board is revised nightly during the shoot as days are lost,
scenes are cut, and weather intervenes.

**What separates a good 1st AD** — the ability to hold the whole film's
dependency graph in mind and see, three weeks ahead, that a decision being made
today about a location will strand an actor's schedule. A good board looks
obvious in hindsight; a bad one is only discovered when it fails.

### 3.1 The board

Each scene becomes a **strip** on a **stripboard** (historically physical
cardboard strips in a board; now software — Movie Magic Scheduling, Gorilla,
StudioBinder). A strip carries scene number, INT/EXT, D/N, set, page count,
cast IDs, and a description.

Strips are then reordered. **Films are almost never shot in script order.**
Scheduling optimises for:

1. **Location** — shoot everything at a location while you have it. Company
   moves are the single biggest waste of a shooting day; a **company move**
   costs 1–3 hours.
2. **Cast availability and cost** — group a day player's scenes so they work
   the fewest days. Star availability is often a hard window.
3. **Day/night** — night shoots are grouped, and switching between day and
   night work requires a **split day** or a weekend to reset the crew's clock
   (a "**French hours**" or turnaround problem).
4. **Set construction readiness** — a set being built cannot be shot on.
5. **Weather and season** — exterior work needs **cover sets** (an interior
   ready to shoot if the weather turns).
6. **Actor continuity needs** — hair length, physical transformation, injury
   makeup, or a performance arc a director wants shot in order.
7. **Child labour rules** — minors have strict daily limits including schooling
   and rest; they are often the most constrained element on a family film.
8. **Animals, stunts, and SFX prep** — need dedicated rigging and rehearsal
   time.

### 3.2 Day out of days (DOOD)

A grid of every cast member against every shooting day, marking
**SW / W / WF / H / R** (Start Work, Work, Work Finish, Hold, Rehearsal). It
determines exactly how many days each actor is paid for, including **holds**
(paid, not working) and **drop/pickup** (a permitted gap of at least 10 days
before rehiring, avoiding continuous payment — with strict guild rules).

DOOD is where scheduling meets money: a badly shaped schedule can double an
actor's cost without changing a frame.

### 3.3 One-liner and shooting schedule

- **One-liner** — the condensed schedule: one line per scene, in shooting
  order, day by day. What everyone carries.
- **Full shooting schedule** — the same with all elements listed per day.

### 3.4 How much work is a day?

Directional norms for **pages per day**:

| Production type | Pages/day |
|---|---|
| Studio tentpole / heavy action | 1/8 – 1 |
| Studio drama | 2 – 3 |
| Independent feature | 4 – 6 |
| Low-budget / limited-location | 6 – 10+ |
| Multi-camera TV / soap | 20+ |

Also tracked: **setups per day** (a "setup" is a new camera position;
20–40 setups is a busy day) and **shots per day**.

### 3.5 Shoot length

| Film type | Shooting days |
|---|---|
| Micro-budget | 12–20 |
| Independent feature | 20–30 |
| Studio mid-budget | 35–50 |
| Tentpole | 60–120+ (plus 2nd unit and reshoots) |

Six-day weeks are possible but expensive (premium pay) and degrade quality
through fatigue. Standard is a **five-day week**, with a **6th day** as an
overage decision.

## 4. Budgeting

**Who** — the **line producer** or **UPM** builds it, in standard accounts
(Movie Magic Budgeting is the default tool; Showbiz and Hot Budget exist). The
**production accountant** takes it over once shooting starts and reports
against it weekly. On the other side of the table sit the **studio EVP/SVP of
physical production** — who has their own view of what the film should cost,
often built by a rival line producer — and, on an independent, the
**completion bond company**, whose approval is a financing condition
(`03-financing-and-dealmaking.md` §7).

**When** — a rough number exists at greenlight, a credible one within two
weeks of the first board, and a locked one two to three weeks before the
shoot. It is then re-forecast weekly for the rest of the film's life.

Structure follows the ATL/BTL split in `03-financing-and-dealmaking.md` §9.

Process:

1. **Rough budget** from the script alone, for greenlight modelling.
2. **Scheduled budget** once the board exists — the first credible number.
3. **Locked budget** approved by studio/bond, with contingency.
4. **Cost reporting** thereafter, weekly (`03-…` §10).

### 4.1 The account structure

A budget is a hierarchy: **top sheet** (one line per account) → **account
detail** (line items) → **line item** (units × rate, plus fringes). Account
numbering is conventional rather than universal, but a US studio budget is
recognisably this shape:

| Range | Block | Typical accounts |
|---|---|---|
| 1100–1500 | **Above the line** | Story and rights; producers unit; direction; cast; ATL travel and living |
| 2000–2900 | **Production, part 1** | Production staff; extra talent; art department; set construction; set striking; set operations (grip); special effects; set dressing; property |
| 2900–3300 | **Production, part 2** | Wardrobe; makeup and hair; electrical; camera; production sound |
| 3400–3900 | **Production, part 3** | Transportation; location expense; media/DIT and lab; tests; stage and facility rental; second unit |
| 4500–4900 | **Post-production** | Editorial; music; post sound; DI and finishing; VFX and titles |
| 6500–7500 | **Other** | Insurance; legal; publicity and unit stills; general expense; fringes; contingency; completion bond; financing costs |

Two mechanical facts about how the numbers are actually assembled:

- **Every line is units × rate.** "Gaffer — 8 prep + 45 shoot + 1 wrap weeks
  @ $4,200/wk." Nothing in a real budget is a round guess; a round guess is a
  sign the line has not been thought about.
- **Every labour line carries fringes.** Payroll taxes, guild pension and
  health contributions, holiday and vacation pay, and payroll-service fees.
  Fringes run roughly **18–35%** on union labour depending on the guild and the
  territory, and a budget that has forgotten to fringe is wrong by the price of
  the film's contingency.

### 4.2 How it is actually built, in order

The order matters, because it reveals which lever moves the number.

| # | Pass | What is being computed | Depends on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Labour** | Everyone × their prep/shoot/wrap weeks × rate, plus fringes | The crew list and the ramp (§1.1) |
| 2 | **Day-driven costs** | Anything priced per shooting day: catering, transport, facilities, equipment rental, stage rent, location fees, per diems | **The number of shooting days** |
| 3 | **Quantity-driven costs** | Sets built, costumes made, props bought, vehicles, VFX shots, extras man-days | The breakdown (§2) |
| 4 | **Fixed and negotiated** | Cast fees, director and producer fees, rights, music, post facility packages | Deals (`03-…` §3) |
| 5 | **Percentage lines** | Fringes, insurance (~1.5–2.5% of budget), completion bond (**1.5–3%**, often with a partial rebate), contingency (**5–10%**, and the bond will insist on 10%), studio overhead | Everything above |
| 6 | **Incentive modelling** | Which spend qualifies in which jurisdiction, and what comes back (`03-…` §6) | Where the film shoots |

The consequence of pass 2 is the single most important fact about film
budgets: **roughly 60–75% of a below-the-line budget is a function of the
number of shooting days.** Crew, rentals, catering, transport, and facilities
are all rented by the day. This is why every budget conversation eventually
becomes a schedule conversation, and why "can we lose two days" is the first
question a studio asks.

Directional all-in cost per shooting day, as a sanity check:

| Production type | Cost per shooting day |
|---|---|
| Micro-budget | $10–40K |
| Independent feature | $40–100K |
| Studio mid-budget | $150–400K |
| Tentpole | $500K–1M+ |

### 4.3 The iteration with the studio

This is a negotiation with a predictable shape, and everybody in it knows the
choreography.

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| **First scheduled budget** | Comes in **10–30% over** the target. This is normal and semi-deliberate: it is easier to cut from a real number than to defend a fantasy one |
| **The studio's own number** | Physical production has had the script budgeted independently. The two budgets are compared line by line, and the gap is usually in crew size, days, and VFX count |
| **The budget meeting** | Line producer, producer, director, studio physical production, and the studio's finance side. Every account is walked. The director is present for the ones that change the film |
| **The ask** | "Find $4M." Not a set of instructions — a number. How it is found is the production's problem |
| **The script pass** | The writer, director, and producer take a pass for budget (§4.5), and the board is rebuilt against the new pages |
| **Second budget** | Closer. Usually still over |
| **The trade** | Days for scope, or scope for days. Fee deferrals and back-end conversions appear here (`11-money-accounting-and-participations.md` §2) |
| **Lock and approval** | Studio approves; on an independent the bond company issues its no-objection and the financing can close |

**Padding is a real and mutually understood practice.** Experienced line
producers build a little air into soft lines, because they know the studio
will cut and because contingency alone rarely covers a real problem. Studio
executives know this and cut with it in mind. The practice is stable, but a
line producer who pads visibly — or who cannot say honestly which lines are
tight — loses credibility permanently, and credibility is what gets them the
next picture.

### 4.4 Cash flow, not just totals

The budget is also a **cash-flow schedule**, week by week, and on an
independent the financing plan is built against it. This has real
consequences:

- Spend front-loads badly. Construction, deposits, rights, and design money
  goes out in prep, when there is no footage and no way to reassure anyone.
- Payroll peaks during the shoot and falls off a cliff at wrap.
- Post spend is long and thin, except for VFX, which is lumpy and late.
- Incentive money arrives **after** the spend, often 6–24 months later, which
  is why incentives are usually discounted and borrowed against rather than
  spent (`03-…` §6).

### 4.5 "Taking a pass for budget" — what actually gets removed, in order

This is a specific craft, usually performed by the writer with the director
and producer over a few days, sometimes by a specialist brought in to do
exactly this. It is not "cut ten pages". The cuts are made in a strict order
of cost-efficiency, from most money saved per unit of story lost to least.

| # | Move | Why it is first | What it costs the film |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Merge locations** | Two scenes in two places become one scene in one place: kills a company move, a permit, a scout, a prep day, and a dressing package | Usually nothing; often improves the film |
| 2 | **Kill company moves** | Rewrite so a scene happens where the unit already is | Little, if the geography still reads |
| 3 | **Convert night exterior to day, or to interior** | Night exteriors are the most expensive real estate in any script: lighting packages, condor lifts, overtime, turnaround problems | Atmosphere, and sometimes the point of the scene |
| 4 | **Reduce crowd** | "A packed stadium" becomes 80 people shot tight, plus tiling in VFX — or the reverse, whichever is cheaper this week | Scale, which the audience does notice |
| 5 | **Merge or cut characters** | Two supporting parts become one: removes a cast fee, a costume, fittings, travel, and days on the DOOD | Texture; occasionally the film's best scene |
| 6 | **Downgrade speaking parts** | A day player's two lines are cut, making them background at a fraction of the cost | Almost nothing |
| 7 | **Simplify set pieces** | The chase loses a beat; the crash becomes the aftermath; the fight moves from a rooftop to a stairwell | This is where the film starts to feel smaller |
| 8 | **Cut an entire set** | The single most valuable cut available: removes construction, dressing, crew days, and shooting days at once | A whole sequence, usually |
| 9 | **Cut effects and creature work** | Convert a full-CG shot to a practical one, or a creature to a shadow | Sometimes an improvement, sometimes a disaster |
| 10 | **Trim page count everywhere** | 112 pages to 98 pages is roughly four to six shooting days on a studio drama | Pace, usually for the better |
| 11 | **Relocate the whole film** | Move to a cheaper jurisdiction with a better incentive. The largest single lever available | Rewrites the entire logistics plan and often the look; a late relocation is chaos |
| 12 | **Defer money rather than remove it** | Fees converted to back end, facilities taken as deferrals, a producer takes points instead of cash | Nothing now, a lot later (`11-…` §2) |

Two things practitioners will say about this list. First, **the top of it
usually makes the film better** — merged locations and cut scenes tighten
scripts — and the bottom of it makes it worse. Second, the cut that gets made
is frequently not the cheapest one but the least politically defended: the
scene whose champion is not in the room.

### 4.6 What separates a good line producer from an adequate one

1. **Answering the cost of a request in under a minute.** The director asks
   for a crane and a night; a good line producer knows what that is worth
   before the meeting ends, so the decision is made while it is still cheap.
2. **Knowing which lines are soft.** Every budget has ten lines that can give
   and forty that cannot. Cutting the wrong ones produces a number that meets
   the target and a shoot that fails.
3. **Honesty about the number.** A line producer who delivers a budget they
   privately know is undeliverable buys three weeks of peace and a career of
   suspicion.
4. **Protecting the shooting day.** The best ones cut anywhere before they cut
   days, because days are the only thing that cannot be recovered.
5. **Structuring for the incentive.** Knowing which spend qualifies where can
   be worth 20–40% of the below-the-line, and it is decided in prep or not at
   all.
6. **Cash-flow literacy.** Making sure the money arrives before it is needed,
   which on an independent is a genuinely hard problem.
7. **Being someone crews will work for again.** The line producer sets the
   hours culture, and the market for good crew is small and long-memoried
   (§9.4).

### 4.7 How budgets fail

| Failure | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Budgeted from the script, never rebuilt from the board | The first credible number arrives after the money has been committed |
| Fringes wrong or missing | 20–30% of the labour cost appears from nowhere in week two |
| Schedule too tight for the pages | The film "shoots the budget instead of the schedule" — overtime every day, and the overage arrives as a surprise |
| Overtime patterns not modelled | Meal penalties, forced calls, and turnaround violations are all foreseeable and are routinely under-budgeted (`06-principal-photography.md` §6) |
| Contingency spent in prep | Nothing left for the shoot, which is when things actually go wrong |
| VFX shot count guessed | The single most common overage on effects films (`08-…` §2) |
| Incentive assumptions wrong | The qualifying spend doesn't qualify; a hole opens in the financing, not the budget |
| Weather and cover sets not costed | Every lost exterior day is paid for twice |
| Reshoots not budgeted | A studio film without a reshoot allowance will get one anyway (`06-…` §11) |

## 5. Locations

**Who** — the **Location Manager** (Teamsters in the US), with **scouts**, an
**assistant location manager**, and **location assistants**. They report to the
UPM on money and to the director/DP on suitability. On a big film there are
several location managers running in parallel, one per block.

**When** — begins in the first week of prep and never really stops; a location
lost in week six of a shoot has to be replaced in days.

### 5.1 The sequence

| Stage | What happens | Who |
|---|---|---|
| **Brief** | The location manager reads the script and takes the director's and PD's intent — not "a diner", but *this kind* of diner, this feeling, this period | LM, director, PD |
| **Photo scout** | Scouts photograph options and return a package per location: wide, all four walls, approaches, parking, power | Scouts |
| **Director's scout** | The director, PD, DP, and producer visit the shortlist and choose | Director, PD, DP, producer, LM |
| **Feasibility** | Can it actually be shot? Access, power, sound, permits, cost, neighbours, hours | LM, UPM, 1st AD |
| **Negotiation** | Location fee, dates, prep/wrap days, restoration, insurance certificates | LM, UPM |
| **Tech scout / recce** | All HODs walk the location with the director and 1st AD | Everyone |
| **Prep and wrap** | Rigging days before, restoration after | LM, construction, rigging |

### 5.2 The tech scout is the important one

Every department walks the location together and answers, out loud, in front
of the others: where does camera go, where does the sun come from at 4pm, where
does the light get rigged, where do 90 trucks park, where does the crew eat,
where's the nearest hospital, where does the sound come from that will ruin
the take, where do the public get held, and can the actors get from base to set
without being photographed.

Its function is **surfacing disagreement while it's still free**. A production
that skips or rushes the tech scout discovers those answers on the shooting
day, at $200,000 a day.

### 5.3 What a location manager is actually judged on

This is the skill axis, and it is not "finding pretty places":

1. **Reading the director's intent** from a description, so the shortlist is
   right on the first pass rather than the fourth.
2. **Logistics literacy** — knowing before the director falls in love that a
   location has no power, no parking within a mile, a flight path overhead, or
   a 6pm noise curfew.
3. **Negotiation and relationships** — securing a location at a fee the film
   can afford, and being able to go back next time. Location managers work the
   same region for decades; a production that trashes a property poisons it for
   everyone.
4. **Permitting fluency** — knowing which authority, which lead time, which
   restrictions, and which official to talk to.
5. **Neighbour and community management** — the single most common cause of a
   location being lost mid-shoot is the people who live next to it.
6. **Contingency** — having a second option for every location, and a wet-weather
   cover set within reach.

### 5.4 What good and bad look like

**Good**: the unit arrives, parks, and shoots. The location matches what the
director imagined, the light does what the DP was promised, and nobody spends
the morning negotiating with a caretaker. Nearby options cluster so the
schedule can group days without company moves.

**Bad**, in rough order of cost:

| Failure | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Location lost late (owner withdraws, permit refused) | Rewrite, rebuild, or reschedule — days and money |
| Unusable sound (flight path, motorway, building site) | Every line becomes ADR (`07-postproduction.md` §3) |
| No power or access | Generators, long cable runs, hand-carrying kit; hours lost daily |
| Base too far from set | 20–40 minutes lost each way, every unit move, all day |
| Neighbours not managed | Complaints, curfews, and losing the location mid-block |
| Restoration underestimated | Damage claims and a burned relationship |
| Scattered locations | Company moves — the schedule's biggest single waste (`06-…` §5) |

### 5.5 Location vs stage

A defining budget decision, made in prep and hard to reverse.

| | Practical location | Stage build |
|---|---|---|
| **Production value** | High, cheap — the world already exists | Costs what you pay for |
| **Control** | Low — weather, sound, light, public, hours | Total |
| **Lighting** | Constrained by what's there and where the sun is | Rigged overhead, any time of day, any weather |
| **Camera** | Real walls: limited angles, cramped | **Wild walls** fly out; any angle |
| **Cost shape** | Fees, permits, travel, logistics | Construction and stage rent |
| **Schedule** | Weather risk, curfews, availability windows | Predictable, but gated by build |

Most films mix: exteriors and establishing on location, matching interiors on
stage. Getting the match right is the PD's and DP's problem, and the audience
notices when it fails.

## 6. Design and build

**Who** — the **production designer (PD)** heads what is, counting
construction and set dressing, the largest crew on the film. Under them:

| Role | Does |
|---|---|
| **Supervising art director** / **art director** | Runs the department day to day; turns design into buildable, costed drawings; deals with construction |
| **Assistant art directors, set designers (draughtspeople)** | Produce the technical drawings — plans, elevations, sections, details |
| **Concept artist / illustrator** | Produces the images that sell and settle a design |
| **Graphic designer** | Every sign, label, banknote, passport, newspaper, screen UI, and logo — and their clearance |
| **Model maker** | Physical white-card models, and now 3D digital models |
| **Art department coordinator** | Drawings, revisions, distribution, budget tracking |
| **Set decorator** (own department, own budget) | Everything inside the walls that isn't handled by an actor; runs **lead man/gang boss**, **buyers**, the **swing gang** (dressing crew), and the **on-set dresser** |
| **Prop master** | Everything an actor handles; runs prop buyers, prop makers, armourer, and **standby props** on the floor |
| **Construction coordinator** | Builds it: **construction foreman**, carpenters, welders, sculptors, plasterers, scenic artists/painters, **greensman**, riggers |

In the US these sit across several IATSE locals (Art Directors Guild Local 800
for designers, illustrators, and set designers; the property craftspersons'
local for props, set dec, and greens; separate construction crafts). In the
UK the art department, standby art director, and construction department map
similarly under BECTU, with different titles and a **standby art director**
present on the floor.

**When** — the PD is often the first HOD hired, 4–6 months out on a build-heavy
film and 3–4 weeks out on a small one. They are hired *before* the DP on most
films, which surprises people; the design has to exist before it can be lit.

### 6.1 The process, script to shooting

| # | Stage | Output | Who | Typical elapsed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Read and respond** | A visual language: references, mood boards, a lookbook. Often produced before the job is even offered, as a pitch | PD, director | 1–3 weeks |
| 2 | **Set list** | The definitive list of every set in the film, each tagged: **build on stage / practical location / location with modification / redress of another set / VFX extension** | PD, 1st AD, UPM | Week 1 of prep |
| 3 | **Concept art** | Images for the sets and moments that must be agreed or sold | Concept artists | Weeks 1–6 |
| 4 | **Model** | White card, or a 3D model in SketchUp/Rhino/Blender/Unreal, walked through with a virtual lens | Model maker, set designers | Weeks 2–8 |
| 5 | **Technical drawings** | Plans, elevations, sections, at 1:25 or 1/4" = 1'. What construction actually builds from | Set designers | Weeks 3–10 |
| 6 | **Costing and value engineering** | Construction prices the drawings; the number comes back high; the design is reduced without losing its idea | Construction coordinator, PD, UPM | Ongoing |
| 7 | **Long-lead orders** | Steel, glass, specialist hardware, printed wallpaper, bespoke fabric, period fittings | Art dept, construction | 4–12 weeks out |
| 8 | **Build** | Carpentry, plaster, sculpt | Construction | 3–8 weeks per set |
| 9 | **Scenic and ageing** | Paint, texture, distress. This is where a set stops looking like a set | Scenic artists | 3–10 days |
| 10 | **Dressing** | Set decorator's swing gang installs furniture, drapes, practicals, and detail | Set dec | 2–7 days |
| 11 | **Hand-over** | The set is signed over to the shooting company, usually **2–5 days** before it is shot so the DP can pre-rig and pre-light | PD, DP, gaffer, 1st AD | — |
| 12 | **Shoot, then strike** | Struck to free the stage for the next build, often within 24 hours | Construction | 1–3 days |

### 6.2 What concept art is actually for

Not decoration and not a portfolio. Concept art exists to do three jobs:

1. **To settle a disagreement before it costs money.** An image forces the
   director, the PD, and the studio to discover that they were imagining three
   different films, at the one point in the process where discovering that is
   free.
2. **To authorise spend.** A studio approves a $2M set from a picture, not from
   a floor plan. The image is the instrument of the approval.
3. **To brief every other department at once.** Costume, VFX, props, and the
   DP all take colour, period, scale, and mood off the same image.

Concept art is therefore deliberately *seductive*, and experienced producers
read it with that in mind: the illustration always looks better than the set,
because the illustration has perfect light, no crew, and no budget.

### 6.3 The model is where money is saved

A white-card model or a 3D walkthrough is the cheapest place on the entire
film to make a mistake. What gets found there:

- The camera cannot get far enough back for the wide shot the director has
  described.
- The sightline through the door only works from a position where the wall is.
- The set is 30% larger than it needs to be, because nobody checked what the
  lens sees.
- There is nowhere to rig a light, because the ceiling is a solid piece.
- There is nowhere for the crew to stand, so every setup costs an extra
  twenty minutes.
- A whole wall is never seen, and can be a flat.

Directors who block in the model, and DPs who bring a virtual lens to it,
routinely take days out of a schedule before a single sheet of ply is cut.

### 6.4 How construction is actually scheduled, and where it goes wrong

Construction runs a **Tetris problem**: a finite number of stages, a build
sequence that must deliver sets in shooting order with 2–5 days of slack, and
strikes that must clear floor space for the next build. On a big film one
stage may host three or four sets in sequence.

Construction is also where the film's **prep debt** becomes visible, because
it is the only department whose lateness is physically obvious.

| Failure | Cause | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Set handed over on the shooting morning** | A late director decision propagating through drawings, costing, and build | 1–2 hours off the front of the day; no pre-light; wet paint in shot |
| **Set built without wild walls** | Cost saving, or a designer thinking architecturally | Every reverse takes an hour longer; adds up to days across a block |
| **Solid ceiling** | Design realism | Nowhere to rig; the DP lights from the floor all week; the set looks flat |
| **Set too big** | Designed to a floor plan, not to a lens | More to build, more to dress, more to light, longer to shoot |
| **Set too small once rigged** | Nobody accounted for lighting rig, sound, and 40 crew | Reverses shot on a different day, or a wall comes out at 2am |
| **Redress plan not honoured** | The schedule changed after the design was locked | Two sets have to exist at once; one gets built twice |
| **Dressing bought before scenic finishes** | Everyone working to their own deadline | Furniture that fights the paint; reordering at short notice |
| **VFX extension line not agreed** | No conversation between PD and VFX supervisor | Either 20 feet of set built for nothing, or 20 feet of set missing and the shot re-scoped (`08-…` §3) |
| **Practical location modification underestimated** | Scouting optimism | Restoration cost, a damage claim, and a lost location for the industry (§5.3) |
| **Construction overtime** | Every cause above | The most common single overage in the art department, and it is nearly always paid for a decision made weeks earlier |

### 6.5 What separates a great production designer from an adequate one

1. **Designing for the camera and the schedule, not for architecture.** The
   great PD's set photographs from every angle the director wants, has room for
   the crew, breaks apart for coverage, and can be shot fast. An adequate PD
   designs a beautiful room.
2. **Storytelling through space.** The set says who lives there before anyone
   speaks — what they can afford, what they keep, what they have stopped
   maintaining. This is the actual craft, and it is invisible when it works.
3. **Cost intelligence.** Knowing what a look costs, and being able to deliver
   80% of it for 30% of the money. The single most valuable thing a PD does is
   find the practical location that removes a build.
4. **Deciding fast.** The PD is the pacing item for the largest crew on the
   film. An indecisive designer is more expensive than an expensive one, and
   the cost is paid in construction overtime, not in the design fee.
5. **Lighting the set in the design.** Where the windows are, where the
   practicals are, what the walls do to bounced light, and whether the ceiling
   flies. A PD who designs with the DP makes the whole shoot faster; one who
   doesn't makes the DP fight the set for eight weeks.
6. **Coherence across the whole film.** Forty sets, two units, and six months
   still feel like one world — palette, texture, period discipline, and scale
   holding across all of it.
7. **Spending nothing on what is not on camera.** Recognising the wall that is
   never seen, the second storey that is never entered, and the corridor that
   can be the same corridor three times.
8. **Running a large organisation.** Art, construction, and set dec can be
   100–300 people. Most of the job is management, procurement, and deadlines.
9. **Knowing the difference between period-correct and period-*legible*.** A
   set that is historically perfect but reads as ambiguous has failed; a set
   that reads instantly as 1974 with three anachronisms nobody will see has
   worked.

**Good looks like**: the director walks onto a set and finds it is what they
imagined, but better and more specific; the DP finds the light is already
half-solved; the actors find the space tells them how to behave in it; and the
schedule holds because the set was ready three days early.

**Bad looks like**: a set that reads as a set, a designer who is still
deciding in week nine, and a construction department on permanent overtime
paying for it.

### 6.6 Set dressing and props

Two separate departments with separate budgets, separated by a rule that
sounds trivial and is not: **if an actor touches it, it is a prop; if they
don't, it is dressing.** The line moves constantly, which is why it is
negotiated in the production meeting (§11).

- **Hero props** — anything seen in close-up: made or sourced to a standard
  that survives a 4K close-up, in **3–12 multiples** for handling, damage, and
  stunt versions.
- **Practical props** — anything that must work: guns (with an armourer and
  strict protocols), phones, lighters, watches, machinery.
- **Graphics** — every printed and screen element, all of which needs
  clearance. Real brands require permission or replacement; real artwork on a
  wall is a rights problem; a real newspaper masthead cannot simply be used.
- **Dressing budgets** on a contemporary film lean on hire (prop houses hold
  vast stock); period and fantasy lean on manufacture, which is a lead-time
  problem, not a money problem.

## 7. Costume, hair and makeup

Three departments, one continuity problem. They are grouped because they share
the same constraint: everything they do must be repeatable, in order, across
months of shooting, on a schedule that is not in story order.

**Who**

| Department | Chain |
|---|---|
| **Costume** | **Costume designer** → assistant costume designer → **costume supervisor** (runs money, logistics, and stock — the most underrated job in prep) → cutter/fitter, tailors, seamstresses, milliner, **ager/dyer** (breakdown artist), textile artist, buyers → **key set costumer** and set costumers on the floor |
| **Makeup** | **Makeup department head** → key makeup artist → makeup artists → prosthetics/SFX makeup (usually an outside shop) |
| **Hair** | **Hair department head** → key hairstylist → hairstylists → wig maker (often external) |

Stars frequently have **personal** hair and makeup artists written into their
deal, which the department has to absorb without a war. In the UK the costume
chain is designer → **costume supervisor** → **costume standby**, and the
supervisor's authority over the workroom and the budget is more explicit than
in the US.

**When** — costume design starts 8–14 weeks out (much earlier on period and
fantasy); makeup and hair heads join 4–8 weeks out, or months earlier if there
are prosthetics. Every one of them is blocked on casting: **nothing bespoke can
begin until the actor is signed.**

### 7.1 The process, design to shooting day

| # | Stage | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Costume plot** | Every character × every **script day** × every scene, taken straight from the breakdown (§2.3). This grid is the department's whole working life |
| 2 | **Research and design** | References, illustrations, fabric swatches. Presented to the director; palette agreed with the **PD and DP** so the costumes don't fight the sets or the grade |
| 3 | **Sourcing decision, costume by costume** | **Buy** (contemporary retail), **hire** (costume houses hold enormous period stock), **make** (bespoke, from a toile), or **alter and break down** existing stock. Most films do all four |
| 4 | **Fittings** | First fitting: measure, try options, photograph everything. Second: in real fabric. Final: complete with shoes, hair, and makeup, on camera |
| 5 | **Ageing and breakdown** | Distressing, dyeing, sanding, staining, weathering. A new-made costume reads as a *costume*; breakdown is what makes it read as clothing somebody owns |
| 6 | **Multiples** | Made to the count derived from the breakdown (§7.3) |
| 7 | **Continuity capture** | Every character in every costume photographed against the script day, with notes on how it is worn — buttons, tuck, sleeve roll, damage state |
| 8 | **Shooting day** | Set costumers dress the actors and stand by all day, resetting damage, drying, changing for each script day, and re-checking against the continuity book |

**Fittings are the binding constraint on when an actor must arrive.** Actors
typically take **2–5 fittings of 2–4 hours** each; a period lead can take ten
or more. On a bespoke costume the chain is fitting → toile → make → fitting →
break down, and it does not compress below **6–10 weeks** per garment.

Fittings are also, quietly, where the performance starts. Actors routinely say
they found the character in the fitting room — most often in the shoes, which
determine how a person walks. A designer who understands that runs the fitting
as a collaboration rather than a measurement.

### 7.2 Lead times

| Item | Lead time |
|---|---|
| Contemporary purchase | Days |
| Costume-house hire and alteration | 1–3 weeks |
| Bespoke suit or dress | 4–8 weeks |
| Corsetry, tailored period, uniform with correct detailing | 6–12 weeks |
| Armour, hero superhero suit, creature suit | 12–30 weeks |
| Hand-knotted lace-front wig | 40–120 hours of work each; **$2,000–8,000**; leads often need 2–4 |
| Prosthetic character (life cast → sculpt → mould → run → test) | 10–20 weeks |
| Full animatronic creature | 6–12 months |

### 7.3 Multiples, and why the real driver is time

Any costume subject to blood, water, fire, mud, or stunt damage needs copies.
People assume the driver is destruction. It is not — **it is drying and
cleaning time.** A soaked wool suit takes 12+ hours to dry, and there are
eleven more takes today.

| Situation | Typical multiples |
|---|---|
| Standard costume, no damage | 1–2 (one plus a backup) |
| Any costume with a stunt double | +1–2 per double, matched exactly |
| Photo double / stand-in match | +1 |
| Progressive damage across a sequence (clean → dirty → bloodied → destroyed) | One per stage, per shooting day the sequence spans: often **4–8** |
| Rain, water, or mud | **3–6** — purely a drying problem |
| Fire or burn gags | **4–8**, including fire-retardant and gel-treated versions |
| Hero suit / creature costume | **5–20**, plus stunt, plus a version for VFX reference |

Multiples are a real budget line that first-time producers reliably forget,
and the failure is expensive in the worst way: it stops the unit for hours,
with everyone standing around, waiting for a shirt.

### 7.4 Continuity, and how it is actually maintained

Continuity is not memory. It is a document set, maintained by three
departments in parallel and cross-checked:

- **Continuity stills** — every character, every costume, every script day,
  front and back, plus detail shots of how it is worn. Taken at the fitting
  and re-taken on the day.
- **The costume standby's book** — which costume, which state of damage, which
  scene, which take.
- **Hair and makeup continuity** — the same, for hairstyle, injury
  progression, beard length, tattoo cover, and body makeup.
- **The script supervisor's notes** — the authority when the departments
  disagree, and the record that editorial will rely on (`07-…` §1).

Two things follow that outsiders find odd. First, actors have **contractual
no-haircut clauses**: an actor cannot change their hair between the start of a
shoot and the end of reshoots. Second, **injury and dirt continuity is
scheduled**, not improvised — a bruise on script day 3 has an agreed
appearance that must be reproduced eleven weeks later from a photograph.

### 7.5 The shooting-day mechanics nobody outside the department sees

Makeup and hair set the **call times for the entire company**, because the
actors cannot be on set until they are ready.

| Work | Time in the chair |
|---|---|
| Contemporary "no-makeup" makeup | 30–60 min (the natural look is not the fast one) |
| Standard makeup and hair | 60–90 min |
| Period hair set, or a wig applied and dressed | 90–180 min |
| Tattoo cover-up | 30–90 min |
| Light prosthetic appliance | 2–3 hours |
| Full prosthetic character | 4–8 hours, plus 1–2 hours to remove |

The department's constraint is **throughput**: six chairs, thirty people, and
one 6am unit call. This is why the makeup trailer is the first vehicle to
arrive and the last to leave, and why an actor added to a day at short notice
is a bigger problem than it looks.

### 7.6 What separates a great costume designer from a bad one

1. **Character before period.** The costume answers "who is this person, and
   what do they want you to think of them" first, and "what year is it"
   second. A bad designer dresses the era; a good one dresses the person.
2. **Colour discipline across the whole film.** Agreed with the PD and DP in
   prep, so the frame reads, the leads separate from the background, and the
   grade doesn't have to rescue it.
3. **Knowing what fabric does on a lens.** Fine checks and herringbone moiré
   at high resolution; pure white clips; certain greens and blues fight the
   keying colour; some blacks go grey and some go dead; shine behaves badly
   under hard sources. This knowledge is invisible and non-negotiable.
4. **Fitting-room manner.** A fitting is an exposing, physically intimate
   situation with someone who is about to be photographed for two hours. A
   designer who can make a nervous actor feel powerful gets a better
   performance out of them than any note will.
5. **Multiples and continuity discipline.** The unglamorous half of the job,
   and the half that determines whether the unit ever stops.
6. **Running a workroom to a date.** 10–40 makers, a materials pipeline, and
   an immovable first day of photography.
7. **Restraint.** Knowing when the answer is a plain shirt, and being willing
   to be uncredited for the choice.

**Bad, with consequences:**

| Failure | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Design approved but not tested on camera | The fabric moirés, the colour vibrates, or the whole look changes under the show LUT; discovered in dailies |
| Insufficient multiples | The unit stops while a costume dries; a scene splits across two days |
| Palette agreed with nobody | Costumes disappear into the set, or fight the grade for the whole film |
| Continuity stills not taken or not indexed | Mismatches in the cut that cannot be fixed, or a reshoot |
| Bespoke started late (usually because casting was late) | The lead shoots in something rented and approximate |
| Designing for the still, not the movement | Looks superb standing; unwearable in the fight, the run, or the horse |
| No stunt-double matching | The double reads as a different person, and the cut has to hide the stunt |
| Ignoring the actor | A performer fighting their costume all day, which shows in the performance and poisons the department |

## 8. Casting

**Who** — the **Casting Director (CD)**, with an **associate** and
**assistant**, plus a separate **extras/background casting** company. The CD is
contracted per film for a flat fee (roughly **$30K–$150K** for a feature) and
is not a departmental head in the crew sense — they work through prep and are
largely gone by the shoot.

**When** — leads are often cast during development or financing, because the
package needs them (`03-financing-and-dealmaking.md` §2). Supporting roles are
cast through prep. Day players are cast weeks or days ahead, and background is
booked days ahead.

### 8.1 How actors are actually found

There are **two entirely different tracks**, and conflating them is the most
common misunderstanding of casting:

**Offer-only talent** (stars, and most established actors) do not audition.
The process is:

1. The CD, director, and producer build a **list** — ranked names per role,
   usually 10–30 deep, annotated with availability, quote, and likelihood.
2. **Availability check** — the CD calls the agent informally: is the client
   free in this window, would they read it. No offer yet; this is the
   temperature test.
3. **The script goes out** to the agent, sometimes with a director's letter
   explaining the vision — for a wanted actor this letter matters enormously.
4. **Meeting** — the actor and director meet. This is where most star casting
   is actually decided, and it is a mutual audition.
5. **Offer** through business affairs; negotiation on fee, dates, credit,
   approvals (`12-talent-labor-and-careers.md` §6).
6. Down the list if it's a pass — and lists exhaust, which is why films end up
   with their fifth choice more often than anyone admits.

**Audition talent** (everyone else) goes through the traditional pipeline:

1. **Breakdown** issued to agents and managers via a service (Breakdown
   Express and equivalents), describing each role, dates, and rate.
2. **Submissions** — hundreds to thousands per role, electronically.
3. **CD filters** to a manageable list from knowledge, tape, and prior work.
4. **Self-tape** — now the default first round; the actor records at home to
   a provided scene ("**sides**").
5. **Callback / session** — in the room or on video with the CD, then with the
   director. Often improvised or redirected to see if the actor can *take an
   adjustment*, which is the real test.
6. **Chemistry read** — paired candidates read together; the decision for
   couples and duos is made here and cannot be made any other way.
7. **Screen test** — on camera, in costume and makeup, sometimes competitively.
   The **test option deal** is signed *before* the test so the studio isn't
   negotiating against a winner.
8. **Studio and financier approval**, then offer.

**Background** is cast separately by an extras casting agency on the basis of
look, size, availability, and special skills (period-correct, drivers, riders,
military experience, dancers).

### 8.2 What separates a good casting director from a bad one

The skill axis, and it is much more than a big contacts list:

1. **Breadth of knowledge** — knowing 200 actors who could play the part,
   including 150 the director has never heard of. The CD's real product is the
   name nobody else would have thought of.
2. **Reading a script for what a role needs** — not the description on the
   page but the function in the story, which is often different.
3. **Taste in the room** — recognising that a mediocre audition contains a
   great performance badly directed, and being able to tell.
4. **Chemistry judgement** — predicting which pairings will work before the
   read, which saves the process weeks.
5. **Agent relationships** — getting the call returned, getting the client to
   read it, getting an honest answer about availability rather than a stall.
6. **Political skill** — managing a director's fixation on an unavailable
   actor, a studio's insistence on a name, and a producer's budget, without
   the process stalling.
7. **Discovering people** — a career is made on the unknown lead who becomes a
   star, and casting directors are hired on that record.

### 8.3 What good and bad casting look like

**Good**: the ensemble is *balanced* — the actors are plausible together, their
energies contrast usefully, and each is playing to their strength rather than
against it. A well-cast supporting role makes a thin part feel written. Good
casting is invisible and is routinely credited to the director.

**Bad**:

| Failure | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **Miscast lead** | Unfixable. No amount of editing, scoring, or reshooting repairs it |
| **No chemistry** | The central relationship doesn't land; the film has no engine |
| **Star cast for finance, not fit** | The performance fights the material; a common consequence of pre-sales (`03-…` §4) |
| **Homogeneous ensemble** | Everyone playing the same note; scenes have no friction |
| **Late casting** | No rehearsal, no fittings, no prep, and the actor arrives cold |
| **Casting to the list, not the role** | The fifth choice on a bad list is worse than the first choice on a good one |
| **Ignoring the read on temperament** | An actor who is wrong for a hard shoot — see `12-…` §3 on reliability |

### 8.4 The constraints casting works under

- **Availability** is the binding constraint far more often than money or
  interest. The right actor is usually shooting something else.
- **Approval chains** — studio, financiers, and sometimes the lead actor have
  approval over co-stars.
- **Budget shape** — cast cost is a top-sheet line, and one star can consume
  the money for the entire supporting ensemble.
- **Insurance** — a principal must be insurable (`03-…` §8).
- **Pre-sale value** — on independents, the financier may effectively choose
  the cast by refusing to fund without a specific name.

### 8.5 Role tiers

**Leads** → **supporting** → **day players** (a few lines) → **under-fives**
(fewer than five lines, a specific SAG category) → **background/extras**
(non-speaking) → **stand-ins** (for lighting) → **photo doubles** and **stunt
doubles** (matched for build and colouring). Each tier has its own rate
structure, contract form, and casting route.

## 9. Crew hiring

**Who decides** — hiring flows top-down: the **director**, with the
**producer**, picks the **heads of department**; each HOD then crews their own
department, subject to budget and **UPM** approval. The studio has approval
over the most expensive HODs and a de facto veto over anyone unknown to them.
On an independent, the **completion bond** company also has a view, because
crew experience is part of how it prices the risk (`03-…` §7).

**When** — HODs are approached the moment the film looks real, often before
it is financed, and confirmed as the ramp in §1.1 dictates. Departments crew
up 2–6 weeks before they start.

- Relationships dominate. Most HODs bring a regular team.
- **Availability** is the constraint: good crew are booked months ahead and
  a start-date slip can lose an entire department.
- **Local hire vs travelling crew** matters for incentives (which usually
  require local spend) and for per diem/housing costs.
- **Deal memos** set rate, hours, kit fee (rental of the crew member's own
  equipment), and box rental.

### 9.1 How an HOD is actually approached

The senior below-the-line market runs on the same machinery as talent, at a
smaller scale, and most people outside it are surprised to learn it has agents
at all.

| # | Step | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The list** | Director, producer, and UPM build a ranked list per department — usually 5–15 names, annotated with availability, quote, and whether the studio will approve them |
| 2 | **The availability check** | To the HOD's **agent** (DPs, PDs, editors, costume designers, and composers are agented, often at the same firms as the talent — `12-…` §1) or directly. "Are they free in this window, would they read it" |
| 3 | **The script goes out** | With the director's lookbook, references, or a conversation about intent. Good HODs choose jobs on the director and the script, not the rate |
| 4 | **The meeting** | Usually 60–90 minutes with the director, then separately with the producer and UPM. Two entirely different conversations (§9.2) |
| 5 | **The reel or portfolio** | Real, but less decisive than outsiders assume — everyone senior has good work. The meeting decides it |
| 6 | **"Who's your team?"** | The producer asks what the HOD will bring. A DP arriving with their own gaffer, key grip, operator, and 1st AC is offering a working unit, not four hires |
| 7 | **The offer and deal memo** | Rate, prep/shoot/wrap weeks, guaranteed hours, kit and box rental, travel and housing, credit, exclusivity |
| 8 | **Studio approval** | Rubber-stamped for known quantities, genuinely contested for the big three (DP, PD, editor) |

### 9.2 The two conversations in an HOD meeting

- **With the director**, the question being answered is *do you see the same
  film, and can I talk to you for six months*. Directors hire people they can
  argue with productively at 6am in the rain.
- **With the producer and UPM**, the question is *can you do it for this money
  in this time, and will you tell me the truth when you can't*. The HOD who
  says "yes, and here is what it costs" is hired over the one who says either
  "no" or an unqualified "yes".

### 9.3 What a deal memo actually contains

| Term | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Weekly or daily rate** | Usually quoted per week for HODs and per day below them |
| **Guaranteed hours** | A guaranteed day of 10, 11, or 12 hours; a **flat weekly** ("all-in") buys unlimited hours and is contested by the unions |
| **Prep / shoot / wrap weeks** | The number of each, guaranteed. This is the number that gets cut when the budget is over |
| **Overtime formula** | Time-and-a-half then double time, plus **meal penalties** and **turnaround** violations (`06-…` §6) |
| **Kit fee / box rental** | Rental paid for equipment the crew member owns and brings — real money for makeup artists, script supervisors, sound, and gaffers |
| **Travel and living** | Travel days, per diem, housing, a car or car allowance. This is what makes a travelling crew member cost 30–60% more than a local one |
| **Credit** | Position, size, and card — negotiated seriously for HODs |
| **Exclusivity / pay-or-play** | Whether they can take another job if this one slips, and whether they are paid if it does |

Directional US union weekly rates, as sanity checks only — they vary hugely by
budget tier, market, and quote (`05-departments-and-crew.md` §5):

| Role | Weekly range |
|---|---|
| Production designer | $8–20K (name PDs far more) |
| Director of photography | $7.5–25K (name DPs far more) |
| Costume designer | $5–12K |
| Gaffer, key grip | $3–5.5K |
| Camera operator, 1st AC | $3.5–6K |
| Production coordinator | $2.5–4K |
| Set PA | $200–275/day |

### 9.4 What a crew relationship is actually worth

The reason directors and DPs travel with a team is not sentiment; it is
measurable speed.

- A **familiar unit** communicates in shorthand. The DP says two words and the
  gaffer already knows the position, the source, and the diffusion. On setups,
  this is worth roughly **10–25%** — which across a 45-day schedule is days.
- A team that has worked together **anticipates**. The next setup is being
  rigged while the current one is being shot. On an unfamiliar crew,
  everything is sequential.
- **Trust survives bad days.** Week nine, night exteriors, rain — this is when
  a crew that likes each other outperforms a crew that was assembled.
- The cost of a brand-new crew is paid in the **first two weeks**, and the
  schedule almost never accounts for it.

The counter-argument, which good producers make: a permanently intact team
stops being challenged, and a local crew who know the region, the weather, and
the suppliers beat an imported one who don't.

### 9.5 How availability actually constrains

The vocabulary is precise and worth knowing, because it *is* the mechanism:

- **Pencilled** — provisionally held on specific dates. Not a commitment on
  either side.
- **Challenged** ("challenging the pencil") — another production wants the same
  dates. The first production is asked to **confirm or release**, usually
  within 24–48 hours. This is the moment films lose crew: an unconfirmed film
  loses to a confirmed one, every time.
- **Confirmed / booked** — held firm, with cancellation terms.
- **Pay-or-play** — the crew member is paid whether or not the film happens; a
  rare and expensive way to hold someone senior.
- **First refusal** — the informal right to be asked before the dates go.

The practical consequence: **a film that cannot confirm dates cannot hold
crew.** This is why an unfinanced or undated production quietly loses its
first-choice department heads one by one, usually without ever being told.

### 9.6 What a start-date slip actually does

| Slip | What happens |
|---|---|
| **A few days** | Absorbed. Some dailies rebooked, some equipment holds extended |
| **1 week** | Survivable. One or two crew with a hard out are lost and replaced from the same tier |
| **2–4 weeks** | HODs start dropping. The ones with mortgages and children take the confirmed job, and they are the good ones. The film re-crews from its second choices |
| **6+ weeks** | Effectively a re-crew. And the film often loses its second choices too, because the market has moved on — everyone good is now booked into the window it slipped into |
| **Across a season** | The exteriors no longer match: leaves, light, weather, and daylight hours have changed. This is a script and schedule problem, not just a hiring one |
| **Indefinite** | Everyone releases. When the film comes back it starts from a blank crew list, and the fact that it slipped is now public information that affects who says yes |

The same slip also hits cast (§8.4 — availability is the
binding constraint), stages (booked by the month, and someone else wants them),
and equipment orders.

### 9.7 What separates a good HOD hire from a bad one

Beyond craft, which is table stakes at this level:

1. **Communicating cost.** Saying "yes, and it costs a day" rather than "no",
   or worse, "yes" followed by a surprise.
2. **Keeping their department.** Crew who follow an HOD from film to film is
   the single most reliable quality signal in the industry.
3. **Being decisive at the right altitude.** Making the twenty decisions a day
   that don't need the director, and correctly identifying the two that do.
4. **Not being precious.** The film changes constantly; an HOD who
   re-litigates settled decisions burns everyone's time.
5. **Morale.** HODs set the tone of 10–50 people for six months. A department
   that is frightened works slowly and hides problems.
6. **Honesty about lead times.** Saying in week two that something cannot be
   ready, when it is still fixable.

| Hiring failure | Consequence |
|---|---|
| HOD hired above the film's weight | A tentpole DP on a 24-day indie schedule; beautiful, and two scenes behind by week two |
| HOD hired as a placeholder | They leave for a better job the moment it appears, mid-prep |
| Two key HODs who don't get on | Classic pairing: PD and DP. The film has two visual plans and neither wins |
| Crewing to favours rather than competence | The department cannot be corrected, because everyone is somebody's friend |
| Over-crewing | Money spent, and a slower set — more people is not more speed past a point |
| Under-crewing to hit a budget | Overtime exceeds the saving by week three |
| Replacing an HOD mid-shoot | Possible, occasionally necessary, and always visible on screen — a DP change mid-film shows in the grade |

## 10. Rehearsal, previs, and testing

The common thread: these are all ways of **buying certainty in prep at prep
prices**, instead of discovering the same information on a shooting day at
shooting-day prices. Every one of them is cut first when prep is short, and
every one of them is the reason the shoot then overruns.

### 10.1 Rehearsal

**Who** — the director with the principals, often with the writer present and
usually with nobody else. The 1st AD and script supervisor may attend blocking
rehearsals; the DP attends the on-set walk-throughs.

**When and how much** — a director may get **one to three weeks** with the
principals, or none at all. Guild rules require rehearsal time to be paid, and
a rehearsal day is a work day on an actor's contract, which is why rehearsal is
a budget line and why day players are almost never rehearsed.

| Form | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Table work** | Scene by scene, seated: what is the scene about, what does each character want, what is actually being said underneath |
| **On-feet blocking** | In a rehearsal room taped out to the set's real dimensions, so the blocking will survive contact with the walls |
| **Walk the set** | Once sets exist, the same blocking on the actual floor, with the DP present. This is where the shot list gets finalised (§13.2) |
| **Improvisation and exploration** | Some directors; used to find material rather than to fix it |
| **Stunt and action rehearsal** | Separate, physical, and safety-critical (§10.4) |

**What rehearsal actually buys**: line problems found while a rewrite is free;
blocking decided so the DP can pre-light; the actors' questions answered at a
point where answering them costs nothing; and relationships established
between actors who will have to be intimate, hostile, or married on camera in
three weeks with 90 people watching.

**What it costs**: money, actor availability, and — the real argument against
it — **freshness**. Some directors and many film actors believe a performance
peaks on take three and that rehearsal spends those takes in a room where no
camera is running. Both positions are held by excellent directors, which
suggests the answer is material-dependent: rehearsal helps ensemble and
dialogue-driven work most, and helps naturalistic or non-professional
performance least.

### 10.2 The table read, and why it is political

**Who is in the room** — the full principal cast, the director, the writer,
the producers, studio executives, sometimes financiers and distributors,
sometimes HODs. A **professional reader** delivers the stage directions, and
choosing a good one matters more than anyone expects.

**What actually happens** — the script is heard end to end for the first time.
A read runs roughly **a minute a page**, plus laughs; a two-hour-fifteen read
on a 105-page script means the film is long and everyone in the room now knows
it.

**What gets discovered**:

- Length, unarguably.
- Which jokes work with strangers in the room, and which died the moment they
  left the page.
- The scene that is dead — the one where attention visibly goes.
- The character who vanishes for forty pages, which nobody noticed reading it.
- Exposition that is inaudible when spoken.
- Whether the cast, together, sound like a film.

**Why it is political** — it is the only moment when the studio's
decision-makers experience the whole script at once, in a room, with no
defence available. A bad table read has triggered rewrites, recastings,
budget cuts, date moves, and outright shutdowns. It runs the other way too: a
great read is used deliberately as a selling event, to lock an executive's
enthusiasm before prep money is committed. Consequently:

- Writers dread it, because their work is being judged on someone else's
  delivery.
- Film actors habitually **underplay** at a read (they are not performing yet,
  and they are protecting the choices they haven't made), while comedians and
  theatre actors over-perform. Executives frequently misread both.
- A producer who knows what they are doing manages the room — who sits where,
  who is invited, who reads the stage directions, and whether the studio is
  there at all.

### 10.3 Tests, and what each one actually proves

Camera tests are shot on the real camera, with the real lenses, on the real
faces, and — critically — **graded and screened** exactly as dailies will be.
The purpose is not exploration. It is to **end the argument before the shoot
starts**.

| Test | What it proves |
|---|---|
| **Lens test** | How each set of glass renders skin, flare, bokeh, and edge fall-off at the working stop; how much it breathes on a focus pull. Chosen by looking at a face, not a chart |
| **Format / sensor test** | Dynamic range and noise floor at the working ISO, and how the codec survives the darkest part of the intended look |
| **LUT / show-look test** | The DP, director, and colourist agree a look, which is baked into dailies so nobody spends the shoot arguing with an ungraded image (`07-postproduction.md` §5) |
| **Makeup and hair test** | Foundation that goes grey under tungsten, a wig lace edge that catches the light, a prosthetic seam that shows at 40mm, ageing that reads as makeup |
| **Costume test** | Moiré on fine weave, whites clipping, colours vibrating, and how a fabric moves |
| **Skin-tone tests across the whole cast** | Lighting and makeup evaluated for every skin tone being photographed, not just the lead's. Historically neglected, and the neglect is visible in decades of film |
| **Hair, ageing, and progression tests** | That the day-1 and day-90 versions of a character are recognisably the same person |
| **VFX methodology test** | That the marker plan, the plate approach, and the on-set data capture work (`08-…` §3) |
| **Stunt, rig, and SFX test** | That the gag fires, that the rig holds, and that the safety plan survives contact with reality |

The mark of a well-tested film: the director signs off in prep, and there is
nothing left to disagree about on day one. The mark of an untested one:
somebody starts a sentence with "in dailies it looked…" in week two.

### 10.4 Previs and its relatives

Four different things, routinely conflated:

| Form | What it is | When | Who |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Previs** | Animated blocking of a sequence — camera, timing, and cut. Its real output is a **shot count and a duration**, which is what the schedule and the VFX bid are built from | 3–9 months out | Previs house, director, VFX supervisor |
| **Techvis** | The same geometry with real numbers: lens data, crane reach, stage dimensions, set scale, sun position. Its output is an equipment list and a floor plan | During prep | Previs house, DP, key grip, 1st AD |
| **Stuntvis** | Video or animated rehearsal of action, used to agree what is actually being attempted, and to brief safety | During prep | Stunt coordinator, 2nd unit director |
| **Postvis** | Rough CG dropped into cut plates so the edit can be judged before finals exist | Post, not prep | Editorial, VFX (`07-…` §4) |

Previs is not cheap — a complex sequence is weeks of work at a specialist
house — and it is defensible precisely because it converts an unpriceable
sequence into a shot list that can be scheduled, budgeted, and bid. On a
heavy-action film it is the difference between a 12-day sequence and an
open-ended one.

### 10.5 Stunt and action prep

Run by the **stunt coordinator** (with a **2nd unit director**, often the same
person, and a **fight** or **precision-driving coordinator** for specialties).
Action is designed, rehearsed in a warehouse or gym for **2–8 weeks**, recorded
as stuntvis, approved by the director, and then walked through again on the
day with a formal safety briefing before a single take.

The reason this is a prep item and not a shooting item: stunt performers must
be cast and matched to the principals, rigs must be engineered and load-tested,
permits and insurance riders must be in place, and — the part that cannot be
compressed — the performers have to be **trained to the specific action**.
Anything skipped here is skipped on the day, in front of a company, with a
person's body (`08-…` §6).

## 11. Production meetings and paperwork

### 11.1 The production meeting

**Who** — every head of department, the producers, the UPM, the director, the
script supervisor, the production accountant, the safety adviser, and often a
studio representative. **30–60 people.** The **1st AD chairs it** and drives
the pace; the director is there to confirm intent, not to design.

**When** — roughly **1–2 weeks before the shoot**, after the tech scouts and
before the script locks hard. Big films hold more than one — per block, per
unit, or per country — and hold a second, smaller one before each subsequent
block.

**How it runs** — the 1st AD walks the schedule day by day, or the script
scene by scene, and each department states out loud what they are bringing and
what they are assuming. It takes **2–5 hours**. Nothing about it is
ceremonial: it is the last structured opportunity to find a disagreement while
it is still free.

### 11.2 What actually gets discovered in a production meeting

The value is entirely in the discoveries, and they are consistent enough to
list:

| Discovery | Typical shape |
|---|---|
| **Two departments with different understandings of the same word** | "The car crashes." Is it practical or VFX? Is it a real car? Whose budget? Who is driving? Nobody had asked |
| **An element nobody costed** | The "who's got the dog?" moment. Every film has at least one |
| **A changed intent told to only one person** | The director decided three weeks ago to shoot the scene in one take on a Steadicam and mentioned it to the DP. Grip, art, and sound learn it here |
| **A missing lead time** | A prop, costume, or vehicle that takes six weeks and shoots in four |
| **An undoable day** | Everyone in the room already knew. This is the first time it is said aloud, and it is the whole reason the meeting exists |
| **A safety issue** | Raised, minuted, and assigned — or, if the meeting is rushed, not |
| **A script contradiction** | A character carries something in scene 40 that they lost in scene 32 |
| **A physical impossibility** | The crane cannot get onto the location; the truck cannot get up the lane; the set cannot take the weight |
| **A clearance problem** | The logo on the wall, the artwork in the hallway, the song on the radio |
| **A department that thought it was somebody else's** | Props vs set dressing, SFX vs VFX, stunts vs SFX. These boundaries are agreed here or fought over later |

The classic tell of a production in trouble is a production meeting that is
short and agreeable. A good one is long, mildly tense, and produces a list of
actions.

### 11.3 Script revisions and the colour system

Once the script is **locked**, scene numbers stop changing and revisions are
issued as **change pages** on coloured stock, in a fixed order, so that anyone
can tell at a glance whether they are holding the current version:

> White → Blue → Pink → Yellow → Green → Goldenrod → Buff → Salmon →
> Cherry → Tan → Grey → Ivory → (double white, and round again)

Changed lines carry an asterisk in the margin and the revision date in the
header. Added scenes become **A24, B24**; cut scenes become
**24 — OMITTED** and keep their number forever. The whole apparatus exists
because 200 people are working from documents that point at scene numbers, and
a renumbered script would invalidate all of them at once.

A film deep into goldenrod during prep is telling you something about how
settled it is.

### 11.4 The rest of the prep paperwork

| Document | Purpose | Owner |
|---|---|---|
| **Breakdown sheets** | Elements per scene (§2) | 1st AD |
| **Stripboard, one-liner, shooting schedule** | The plan (§3) | 1st AD |
| **Day out of days** | Cast working pattern and cost (§3.2) | 1st AD |
| **Crew list / contact list** | Everyone, with role and number | Production coordinator |
| **Movement orders and unit lists** | Where the trucks go, when, and by which route | Transport, LM |
| **Insurance medicals** | Principal cast must be examined and insurable (`03-…` §8) | Production, insurer |
| **Risk assessments and safety bulletins** | Per hazardous sequence | 1st AD, safety adviser |
| **Cast deal memos and start paperwork** | Contracts, tax, work eligibility, guild forms | Production, business affairs |
| **Permits, licences, and certificates of insurance** | Per location, per activity | Location manager |
| **Call sheets** | Begin the day before shooting starts (`06-principal-photography.md` §2) | 2nd AD |

**A UK/US difference worth knowing**: UK productions operate under statutory
health-and-safety law, with formal written **risk assessments** and a named
safety adviser as a legal requirement. US productions work primarily from
industry **safety bulletins** and guild protocols, with a safety-supervised
rather than statutorily-assessed model. The paperwork looks different, the
hazards do not.

## 12. What "prep is finished" actually means

On day one of photography, a well-prepped production has:

- a locked, coloured script with change pages distributed;
- a schedule everyone believes;
- a budget the studio and bond have approved;
- every location for the first fortnight permitted and scouted;
- sets built for the first block, and drawings signed off for the rest;
- principal cast signed, fitted, and tested;
- department heads hired and their orders placed;
- a call sheet for day one that the 1st AD believes is achievable.

Films regularly start with several of these missing. It shows.

film being made.*

## 13. The director's own prep

Everything in §§1–11 describes the production's prep. The director is running
a second, private one at the same time, and it is the one that determines what
the film looks like.

**Who** — the director, working principally with the **DP**, the **production
designer**, the **1st AD**, and, on films that board, a **storyboard artist**.
Some directors also work with their **editor** in prep, which is rarer and
usually a sign of an experienced one.

**When** — from attachment, but the paid, contracted prep period is typically
**8–16 weeks** on a studio feature (the DGA agreement sets a minimum on a
sliding scale by budget) and whatever was negotiated on an independent. A
director who has been attached for three years has been prepping for three
years; the contracted period is when it becomes everyone else's business.

### 13.1 The visual plan, built with the DP

The DP is hired 6–10 weeks out and the first substantial work is not
equipment, it is agreement. The conversation covers:

| Decision | What is actually being settled |
|---|---|
| **Reference** | A set of films watched together, and — more usefully — the specific reason each one is a reference. "Like *Klute*" is worthless; "like *Klute* in that we never light the ceiling" is a plan |
| **Format and aspect ratio** | Film or digital, sensor size, large format or Super 35, 1.85 / 2.39 / 1.66 / 1.43. This decides how faces sit against backgrounds and is essentially irreversible |
| **Lens set** | Vintage or modern glass, how much it flares, how it falls off, what it does to skin. Chosen in a test (§10.3), not from a catalogue |
| **Camera grammar** | When does the camera move, and why. Whether the film is handheld, dollied, locked, or Steadicam, and what a departure from that means when it happens |
| **Lighting approach** | Source-motivated or designed; hard or soft; how dark the film is willing to be; whether practicals in the set do the work (which is a **design** decision, §6.5) |
| **Coverage philosophy** | Oners and blocking, or classical coverage. This one decides the schedule more than any other |
| **Progression** | How the grammar changes across the story, so the form is doing something rather than being consistent |

The output is usually a **lookbook** — images, frames, palette, and notes —
which is also what the director sends to HODs and actors they are trying to
hire (§9.1).

### 13.2 Shot listing and boarding

| Tool | What it is | When it earns its place |
|---|---|---|
| **Shot list** | Every intended shot, scene by scene: size, lens, movement, and what it is for | Always. It is a creative document *and* a schedule document — the 1st AD converts it into setups, and setups are how a day is actually estimated (§3.4) |
| **Storyboards** | Drawn frames, by a storyboard artist working 6–12 weeks | Action, stunts, VFX, anything with complex geography, and anything where 100 people will be standing still while it is explained |
| **Overheads / floor plans** | A top-down of the set with camera positions, lens circles, and actor blocking | The least glamorous and most useful document a director makes. The gaffer's pre-light and the grip's rigging plan come off it |
| **Previs** | Animated, for sequences too complex to board (§10.4) | Effects-led sequences, where it is also the bid document |

A shot list is not a promise. Its function is that **everyone can prepare** —
the DP knows what to pre-light, the grip knows what to rig, the AD knows how
many setups are in the day, and the director is free to abandon all of it when
the actors do something better, because there is something to abandon.

### 13.3 The rest of it

- **Work with the actors** — character conversations, backstory, and the
  questions that are cheap to answer in prep and expensive on the floor.
  Distinct from rehearsal (§10.1) and often done instead of it.
- **Scouting** — the director's scout (§5.1) is where a lot of the shot list
  is actually written, because real geography beats imagined geography.
- **Set walk-throughs** — blocking on the finished set with the DP, days
  before it is shot.
- **Reference screenings** — running the reference films for the whole HOD
  group, so the entire department leadership is calibrated to the same thing.
- **The lock decision** — deciding what the film is *not*, so the departments
  can stop hedging.

### 13.4 What separates a great director's prep from a poor one

1. **Knowing what each scene is about before deciding how to shoot it.** The
   coverage plan follows the meaning, not the other way round. Directors who
   shot-list before they know the scene produce beautiful, inert footage.
2. **Prioritising within the day.** In a twelve-shot day, three shots are the
   film and nine are insurance. A great director knows which three before the
   day starts and protects them; a poor one shoots in order and runs out of
   light before the important one.
3. **Deciding early, publicly, and irreversibly enough for others to act.**
   The single most expensive thing a director does is stay open. Every
   department's lead time (§1.3) is waiting on a decision.
4. **Being able to abandon the plan for something better.** The plan exists to
   make the set fast, not to be executed. A director shooting their
   storyboards regardless of what the actors are doing has automated
   themselves out of the job.
5. **Communicating a visual idea in terms departments can act on.** "Colder"
   is not a note. "The practicals are all tungsten and everything else is
   daylight" is a note four departments can execute.
6. **Knowing what to prepare and what to discover.** Action and VFX must be
   planned to the frame; a two-hander over dinner usually should not be.
7. **Shooting an edit, not shooting everything.** Coverage discipline is a
   prep decision — it is the difference between 22 setups and 34 — and it
   determines whether the film is findable in the cut (`07-…` §1).
8. **Holding uncertainty in private.** A director who is visibly undecided on
   a set of 120 people loses the room, and departments start guessing.

| Prep failure | Consequence |
|---|---|
| No shot list | The 1st AD cannot estimate the day; the DP pre-lights nothing; every setup starts from zero |
| Boarded but not scheduled against | The board says 40 shots, the day holds 22, and nobody found out until 4pm |
| Prep spent on the sequence that was already easy | The hard sequence arrives unplanned |
| Director will not decide | Construction, costume, and VFX all default to the most expensive interpretation, because it is the safest |
| Director decides everything and cannot adapt | Set becomes an execution exercise; performances flatten |
| Visual plan never agreed with the DP | Two films are being shot at once and the grade has to pick one (`07-…` §5) |
| No conversation about coverage | Editorial discovers in week three that the scene cannot be cut the way the director imagined |

## 14. How prep differs on a stage film and a location film

§5.5 compares stages and locations as a creative and budget choice. This is
the other half of it: the two modes produce **structurally different preps**,
with different critical paths, different first hires, and different ways of
failing. Most films are a hybrid, and the hybrid has failure modes of its own.

| | **Stage-based prep** | **Location-based prep** |
|---|---|---|
| **What prep really is** | A construction management problem | A securing and logistics problem |
| **First commitment** | The **stage booking** — major facilities are booked 6–18 months ahead, and you pay from the day you take the floor, including build and strike weeks | The **location list** and the permit lead times |
| **Critical path** | Design decision → drawing → build → hand-over (§1.3) | Scout → director approval → negotiation → permit → prep day |
| **Pacing pair** | Production designer and construction coordinator | Location manager and 1st AD |
| **First HODs hired** | PD, supervising art director, construction coordinator — very early | LM, 1st AD, and a scouting team — very early |
| **What the schedule is built around** | The build sequence. The board is largely a function of when each set is ready | Location availability windows, permits, daylight, and company moves |
| **Dominant risk** | Build runs late; the set is handed over wet | The location is lost, or the permit is refused, or the neighbours revolt |
| **Weather** | Irrelevant | The organising fact of the whole schedule; cover sets are mandatory |
| **Where the money sits** | Construction, stage rent, dressing | Fees, permits, transport, base camp, police and traffic management, restoration |
| **Facilities and base** | Permanent — the stage complex has offices, workshops, parking, catering | Built and struck for every location: unit base, trucks, honeywagons, catering, parking, generators |
| **Sound** | Controlled | The most common location failure (§5.4) |
| **Hours** | Whatever you pay for | Curfews, noise restrictions, residential limits |
| **The good version** | Sets ready three days early, pre-lit, wild walls, any hour of the day available | The world already exists, costs nothing to build, and gives the film production value no budget could buy |

### 14.1 The hybrid, which is the normal case

Almost every film shoots exteriors and establishing on location and matching
interiors on stage. That match is a prep task with its own discipline:

- The art department **surveys** the real building — measurements,
  photographs, materials, sometimes a laser scan — and reproduces the geometry
  on stage, usually enlarged for camera and crew.
- **What is out of the windows** has to be solved: a photographic **backing**,
  a translight, a built exterior, an LED wall, or a VFX comp. Deciding late is
  expensive, because it belongs to a different department in each case.
- The **DP must match the light**: the direction, quality, and colour of the
  location's daylight has to be reproducible on stage, weeks later, at any
  hour.
- The **schedule must not interleave badly**: shooting the location exterior
  in week two and the stage interior in week nine means an actor's hair,
  weight, tan, and costume state all have to be tracked across two months for
  what the audience reads as one continuous moment.

Audiences notice a failed match without being able to say why. It usually
reads as the film feeling cheap.

### 14.2 Backlots, standing sets, and volumes

| Mode | What it changes about prep |
|---|---|
| **Backlot / standing sets** | Streets and facades that already exist and are re-dressed. Prep becomes a dressing and modification exercise; cheap and fast, at the cost of a look other productions have used |
| **Practical location with heavy modification** | The worst of both if managed badly: build lead times *and* location constraints, plus a restoration liability (§5.4) |
| **LED volume / virtual production** | The design lock date moves **months earlier**, because the environment has to be built and approved in a game engine before the shoot rather than after it. Requires techvis-heavy prep, a content team, a "brain bar" on the floor, and camera-tracking calibration. It converts a location problem into a prep problem, which is an excellent trade when the content is ready and a catastrophic one when it isn't (`08-vfx-and-specialty.md` §4) |
| **Full location, run-and-gun** | Prep shrinks to securing and logistics; the trade is that almost nothing is controllable, and the film's tolerance for weather and noise has to be built into the script |

## 15. Parallel prep: blocks, units, and scale

Small films have one prep. Large films run several at once, and the way they
are partitioned is itself a prep decision.

### 15.1 Blocks

A **block** is a contiguous run of shooting days grouped by place — one
country, one location cluster, one stage complex, one period of the story. Big
films are prepped and often crewed block by block:

- Each block may have its own **location manager**, its own **art director**,
  and its own **AD team**, with a travelling core (director, DP, PD, 1st AD,
  producers, principal cast) moving between them.
- Block 1 is fully prepped before the shoot starts. Blocks 3 and 4 are
  prepped **while blocks 1 and 2 are shooting**, by a team that stays behind
  the unit.
- This is normal and not a failure. It becomes a failure when the shooting
  unit's daily problems consume the prep team's attention — which is exactly
  what happens when the shoot starts going badly, and is why troubled films
  get progressively less prepared as they go.

### 15.2 Units

| Unit | What it shoots | Who runs it |
|---|---|---|
| **Main unit** | Everything with the principal cast and the director | Director, 1st AD |
| **Second unit** | Action, plates, establishing shots, doubles, inserts, scenics — sometimes half the film's running time on an action picture | 2nd unit director (often the stunt coordinator), own DP, own AD, own schedule |
| **Splinter unit** | A small piece of the main unit peeling off for a day or an afternoon | Usually the main unit's 2nd AD or an additional director |
| **VFX plate unit** | Clean plates, element photography, reference and HDRI capture | VFX supervisor, small crew (`08-…` §3) |
| **Additional photography** | Booked in prep on studio films as a held window months after wrap; pretending otherwise is how it becomes an emergency (`06-…` §11) |  |

The single most contested prep item across units is **the boundary**: which
shots belong to which unit. It is negotiated between the director, the 1st AD,
the 2nd unit director, and the UPM, and when it is left vague the main unit
ends up shooting what the second unit budgeted for, at main-unit day rates.

| Multi-unit failure | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Unit boundary never agreed | Main unit absorbs second unit's work; the schedule loses days it never allocated |
| Two DPs, no agreed look | Second unit material does not cut with main unit; regraded, reshot, or cut around |
| Continuity not shared across units | Costume, hair, and props drift between units; visible in the cut |
| Second unit given no prep of its own | It becomes a pickup unit rather than a producing one, and the main unit's overflow goes nowhere |
| Blocks prepped by a team that gets pulled onto the shoot | Each successive block is less prepared than the last |

### 15.3 International and co-production prep

Where the money must be spent shapes the schedule more than where the story is
set (`03-…` §6):

- Two crews, two sets of labour rules, two currencies, and often two
  production offices with a shared UPM structure.
- Co-production treaties impose **spend and personnel quotas** by territory,
  which effectively dictate who can be hired for which job.
- Travelling crew need visas, work permits, carnets for equipment, and
  housing; each has its own lead time and each can fail independently.
- Local **fixers** and production service companies do the work a location
  manager would do domestically, and the quality of that relationship is the
  whole film's exposure in that territory.
- The prep calendar has to absorb the fact that public holidays, weather
  seasons, and permit authorities differ by country.

## 16. Prep under compression

Prep is almost never cut because someone decided it was unnecessary. It is cut
because a date became immovable — a release date, a star's window, an
incentive deadline, a seasonal weather window, a rights expiry — and the only
flexible thing left was the front end.

What separates a professional response from a panic is that the order of
protection and the order of sacrifice are decided deliberately.

**Protect, in this order:**

1. **Breakdown and schedule accuracy.** The cheapest work on the film and the
   thing everything else is derived from. A wrong breakdown makes every
   subsequent decision wrong.
2. **Casting the leads.** Everything bespoke — costume, prosthetics, stunt
   doubles, fittings — is blocked on it (§1.3).
3. **Full prep of the first block only.** Locations secured, permits issued,
   sets built, first fortnight scouted. Prep the rest during the shoot (§15.1).
4. **Tech scouts for the first block.** Two hours per location, all HODs, no
   exceptions (§5.2).
5. **Tests on the principals.** Camera, makeup, hair, and costume, graded and
   screened. Half a day now, a reshoot later.
6. **The production meeting.** It can be shortened. It cannot be skipped
   (§11.1).
7. **Costume multiples.** Counterintuitive, but this is the item whose absence
   stops the unit dead, and it cannot be fixed on the day.

**Sacrifice, in this order:**

1. **Rehearsal.** Always the first thing cut, everywhere, on every film.
2. **Concept art beyond what is needed to get approvals.**
3. **Previs for anything that is not action or VFX.**
4. **Design depth on later-block sets** — design them while the film shoots.
5. **The director's boarding of dialogue scenes.**
6. **Second-unit prep**, which is a false economy and is usually cut anyway.

| What compression reliably breaks | How it shows |
|---|---|
| Construction | Sets handed over on the day, wet, unlit, and unfinished on the side the camera turns to on Friday |
| Costume manufacture | Leads in rented approximations of the design |
| Location depth | The first block is fine; weeks four onward are scouted in a hurry, and the film's locations get visibly worse as it goes |
| Casting below the leads | Day players cast days ahead, arriving cold with no fitting |
| VFX methodology | Decided on the floor, which is the most expensive place to decide it (`08-…` §2) |
| Everyone's confidence | Departments hedge toward the expensive interpretation, because being wrong cheaply is worse for them than being wrong expensively |

**The honest counterweight.** Some films shoot beautifully on almost no prep:
small casts, few locations, contemporary dress, a director who has made the
film in their head for years, and a crew who have worked together before. The
amount of prep a film needs scales with the number of **moving parts** — sets,
locations, cast, effects, units, territories — not with the budget. A $40M
two-hander in one house needs less prep than a $6M period film with forty
speaking parts, and treating budget as the proxy is how the second one gets
wrecked.
