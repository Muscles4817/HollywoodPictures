# 04 — Pre-Production

*Turning a screenplay into a shootable plan.* Prep is where a film is actually
designed, costed, and de-risked. Almost every problem that appears on set was
either created or prevented here.

> **Domain reference.** Real industry, not this game. See `README.md`.

---

## 1. How long prep lasts

Prep begins when the film is greenlit (or soft-greenlit) and ends on the first
day of principal photography.

| Film type | Typical prep |
|---|---|
| Micro-budget indie | 3–5 weeks |
| Independent feature ($5–20M) | 6–10 weeks |
| Studio mid-budget | 8–12 weeks |
| Effects-led tentpole | 4–12 months (VFX and design start far earlier) |
| Period / heavy build / creature | 4–9 months |

Prep is not uniform: the director, producer, production designer, and 1st AD
start first; most crew are hired in a staggered ramp, and the largest
departments (art, construction, costume) arrive well before camera and grip.

**Prep drives quality more than shoot length does.** A shortened prep is the
most reliable predictor of an overrunning, compromised shoot, because
construction, costume manufacture, location securing, and rehearsal are all
serial processes that cannot be parallelised past a point.

---

## 2. Script breakdown

The 1st AD (with the UPM/line producer) breaks the script into its physical
requirements. Every scene is decomposed into **elements**, colour-coded by
convention on the breakdown sheet:

| Element | Examples |
|---|---|
| Cast (speaking) | Numbered; "day players" vs series regulars |
| Background / extras | Crowd sizes, special ability extras |
| Stunts | Falls, fights, driving, fire |
| Vehicles | Picture vehicles, action vehicles, process |
| Props | Anything an actor handles |
| Set dressing | Anything they don't |
| Wardrobe | Including doubles/multiples for damage or stunts |
| Hair & makeup | Prosthetics, blood, ageing |
| Special effects (practical) | Rain, wind, squibs, breakaways, pyro |
| VFX | Every shot requiring post work |
| Animals | Wrangler, humane oversight |
| Music/sound | Playback, source music |
| Special equipment | Crane, techno, drone, underwater, Steadicam |
| Security / medics / permits | |

**Page counts** are measured in **eighths of a page** — a scene is "2 4/8
pages." This is the unit in which a day's work is estimated and in which
progress is reported.

Scene headers give **INT/EXT** and **DAY/NIGHT**, which are the two variables
that dominate scheduling.

## 3. Scheduling

### 3.1 The board

Each scene becomes a **strip** on a **stripboard** (historically physical
cardboard strips in a board; now software — Movie Magic Scheduling, Gorilla,
StudioBinder). A strip carries scene number, INT/EXT, D/N, set, page count,
cast IDs, and a description.

Strips are then reordered. **Films are almost never shot in script order.**
Scheduling optimises for:

1. **Location** — shoot everything at a location while you have it. Company
   moves are the single biggest waste of a shooting day; a **company move**
   costs 1–3 hours.
2. **Cast availability and cost** — group a day player's scenes so they work
   the fewest days. Star availability is often a hard window.
3. **Day/night** — night shoots are grouped, and switching between day and
   night work requires a **split day** or a weekend to reset the crew's clock
   (a "**French hours**" or turnaround problem).
4. **Set construction readiness** — a set being built cannot be shot on.
5. **Weather and season** — exterior work needs **cover sets** (an interior
   ready to shoot if the weather turns).
6. **Actor continuity needs** — hair length, physical transformation, injury
   makeup, or a performance arc a director wants shot in order.
7. **Child labour rules** — minors have strict daily limits including schooling
   and rest; they are often the most constrained element on a family film.
8. **Animals, stunts, and SFX prep** — need dedicated rigging and rehearsal
   time.

### 3.2 Day out of days (DOOD)

A grid of every cast member against every shooting day, marking
**SW / W / WF / H / R** (Start Work, Work, Work Finish, Hold, Rehearsal). It
determines exactly how many days each actor is paid for, including **holds**
(paid, not working) and **drop/pickup** (a permitted gap of at least 10 days
before rehiring, avoiding continuous payment — with strict guild rules).

DOOD is where scheduling meets money: a badly shaped schedule can double an
actor's cost without changing a frame.

### 3.3 One-liner and shooting schedule

- **One-liner** — the condensed schedule: one line per scene, in shooting
  order, day by day. What everyone carries.
- **Full shooting schedule** — the same with all elements listed per day.

### 3.4 How much work is a day?

Directional norms for **pages per day**:

| Production type | Pages/day |
|---|---|
| Studio tentpole / heavy action | 1/8 – 1 |
| Studio drama | 2 – 3 |
| Independent feature | 4 – 6 |
| Low-budget / limited-location | 6 – 10+ |
| Multi-camera TV / soap | 20+ |

Also tracked: **setups per day** (a "setup" is a new camera position;
20–40 setups is a busy day) and **shots per day**.

### 3.5 Shoot length

| Film type | Shooting days |
|---|---|
| Micro-budget | 12–20 |
| Independent feature | 20–30 |
| Studio mid-budget | 35–50 |
| Tentpole | 60–120+ (plus 2nd unit and reshoots) |

Six-day weeks are possible but expensive (premium pay) and degrade quality
through fatigue. Standard is a **five-day week**, with a **6th day** as an
overage decision.

## 4. Budgeting

The **line producer** or **UPM** builds the budget from the breakdown and
schedule, in standard accounts (Movie Magic Budgeting). Structure follows the
ATL/BTL split in `03-financing-and-dealmaking.md` §9.

Process:

1. **Rough budget** from the script alone, for greenlight modelling.
2. **Scheduled budget** once the board exists — the first credible number.
3. **Locked budget** approved by studio/bond, with contingency.
4. **Cost reporting** thereafter, weekly (`03-…` §10).

**Budget-driven rewriting** is normal: if the number comes in high, the
producer, director, and writer strip scenes, merge locations, cut cast, or
reduce a set piece. "Taking a pass for budget" is a standard script step.

---

## 5. Locations

- **Scouting** — the location manager and scouts photograph options against
  the script's needs; the director and DP choose.
- **Tech scout / recce** — all department heads visit the chosen locations
  with the director and 1st AD, and walk the day: where camera goes, where
  the sun is, where the trucks park, where the crew eats, where power comes
  from. This is the single most important prep event for the shoot's
  efficiency.
- **Permits and agreements** — filming permits, location agreements, road
  closures, aviation clearance, marine coordination, neighbour notification.
- **Unit base** — where the trucks, trailers, and catering live; distance from
  set determines lost time.
- **Practical location vs stage.** A stage build gives control (weather,
  sound, wild walls, lighting rigs, time) at the cost of construction money.
  A location gives production value cheaply at the cost of control. Most films
  mix, and the split is a defining budget decision.

---

## 6. Design and build

The **production designer** delivers, over prep:

- **Concept art and illustrations**, then **set drawings** (plans, elevations,
  sections) from the art director and draughtspeople.
- A **model or previs** of key sets.
- A **construction schedule** run by the construction coordinator: build,
  paint, dress, then **hand over** to shoot, then **strike**.
- **Set dressing** and **props** sourced, hired, made, or bought by the set
  decorator and prop master.

Construction is the classic serial constraint: it needs drawings, drawings need
decisions, decisions need the director, and the stage floor is only free for so
long. Late design decisions are the most common cause of overtime construction
and of a set being shot before it is finished.

---

## 7. Costume, hair and makeup

- **Costume designer** produces sketches, then sources/hires/manufactures.
  Period and fantasy work is *manufacture*, with long lead times and fittings.
- **Multiples** — any costume subject to blood, water, fire, or stunt damage
  needs 3–12 copies. This is a real budget item people forget.
- **Fittings** are scheduled around actor availability, often the binding
  constraint on when an actor must arrive.
- **Prosthetics and makeup effects** need life-casting, sculpting, moulding,
  and testing — months for a creature build, and daily application time
  (2–5 hours) that shapes the shooting day's call times.

---

## 8. Casting

Casting runs through prep and often starts in development.

1. **Breakdown** issued to agents and managers (via services such as Breakdown
   Express) listing roles, dates, and rates.
2. **Submissions** come back in volume; the casting director filters.
3. **Auditions / self-tapes** — self-tape is now the default first round.
4. **Callbacks** with the director; **chemistry reads** for paired roles.
5. **Screen test** for major roles — on camera, in costume, sometimes in
   competition, often a contractual **test option deal** signed *before* the
   test so the studio isn't negotiating from a weak position.
6. **Studio and financier approvals** — most cast lists require sign-off.
7. **Offers** — via business affairs; quote, dates, perks, credit, approvals.
8. **Deal memo**, then long-form contract.

**Roles by tier**: leads → supporting → **day players** (a few lines) →
**under-fives** (fewer than five lines, a specific SAG category) →
**background/extras** (non-speaking) → **stand-ins** (for lighting) →
**photo doubles** and **stunt doubles**.

**Casting director** is contracted for the film and paid a flat fee; they do
not appear in the crew departments (`05-departments-and-crew.md`) day-to-day.

---

## 9. Crew hiring

Hiring flows top-down: the director (with the producer) picks the **heads of
department**; each HOD then crews their own department, subject to budget and
UPM approval.

- Relationships dominate. Most HODs bring a regular team.
- **Availability** is the constraint: good crew are booked months ahead and
  a start-date slip can lose an entire department.
- **Local hire vs travelling crew** matters for incentives (which usually
  require local spend) and for per diem/housing costs.
- **Deal memos** set rate, hours, kit fee (rental of the crew member's own
  equipment), and box rental.

---

## 10. Rehearsal, previs, and testing

- **Rehearsal** — a director may get one to three weeks with the principals,
  or none. Guild rules require rehearsal time to be paid.
- **Camera tests** — lens, format, and look tests, plus **makeup and wardrobe
  tests** shot and graded so everyone agrees what the film looks like.
- **Previs** — animated blocking of complex sequences (`08-vfx-and-specialty.md`).
- **Stunt rehearsal and pre-vis of action** — separate, and safety-critical.
- **Table read** — the full cast reads the script aloud, usually once, often
  with executives present. It is as much a political event as a creative one.

---

## 11. Production meetings and paperwork

- **Production meeting** — a scene-by-scene walk of the whole script with every
  HOD, a week or so before shooting. The last chance to discover that two
  departments have different understandings.
- **Insurance medicals** for principal cast.
- **Safety meetings and risk assessments** per hazardous sequence.
- **Contact list / crew list, movement orders, unit list.**
- **Call sheets** begin the day before shooting starts
  (`06-principal-photography.md` §2).

---

## 12. What "prep is finished" actually means

On day one of photography, a well-prepped production has:

- a locked, coloured script with change pages distributed;
- a schedule everyone believes;
- a budget the studio and bond have approved;
- every location for the first fortnight permitted and scouted;
- sets built for the first block, and drawings signed off for the rest;
- principal cast signed, fitted, and tested;
- department heads hired and their orders placed;
- a call sheet for day one that the 1st AD believes is achievable.

Films regularly start with several of these missing. It shows.
