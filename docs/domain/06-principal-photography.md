# 06 — Principal Photography

*The shoot.* The most expensive and least reversible phase: a studio unit burns
its daily cost whether it shoots one setup or forty, and the schedule is the
only currency that matters.

> **Domain reference.** Real industry, not this game. See `README.md`.

---

## 1. The economics of a shooting day

A shooting day has a **daily burn rate** — total crew, equipment, locations,
cast, and facilities cost divided by shooting days. Directional:

| Production | Daily burn |
|---|---|
| Micro-budget | $5K–$30K |
| Independent feature | $50K–$150K |
| Studio mid-budget | $150K–$400K |
| Tentpole main unit | $500K–$1.5M+ |

Every decision on set is implicitly priced against this. "We'll get it after
lunch" costs real money; "we've lost the day" is a catastrophic sentence.

---

## 2. The call sheet

Issued by the 2nd AD, approved by the 1st AD and UPM, distributed the evening
before. It is the single authoritative instruction for the next day.

Contents:

- **Crew call** and **shooting call** (crew arrive, camera turns).
- **Scenes** to be shot, in order, with page counts, cast IDs, D/N, INT/EXT.
- **Cast list** with individual pickup, makeup, costume, and set times.
- **Background** numbers and calls.
- **Special instructions** per department: props, SFX, stunts, animals, VFX.
- **Location**, unit base, parking, hospital address (always), safety notes.
- **Weather**, sunrise/sunset — the day's real deadline for exteriors.
- **Advance schedule** — what's coming tomorrow and the day after.

The reciprocal document is the **daily production report (DPR)**, filed after
wrap: actual call and wrap times, scenes and pages completed, setups, minutes
of film, overtime, meal breaks and penalties, accidents, and reasons for lost
time. The DPR is the historical record that the cost report and the completion
bond company read.

## 3. Anatomy of a shooting day

A conventional **12-hour crew day** (which is standard, and already long):

| Time | Event |
|---|---|
| −2h to −4h | Cast makeup/hair/costume; rigging crew already on location |
| 0:00 | **Crew call.** Trucks unloaded, first setup begun |
| 0:00–0:30 | **Blocking rehearsal**: director and actors walk the scene for camera; HODs watch |
| 0:30 | Actors released to final touch-ups; **stand-ins** take their marks |
| 0:30–1:30 | **Lighting and setup**: gaffer/grip build the light, camera sets, sound places mics, dressing adjusted |
| 1:30 | Actors back; **rehearsal for camera** with the real cast |
| 1:35 | **"Picture is up"** → roll sound → roll camera → mark it → **action** |
| … | Multiple **takes**; director selects **circle takes** |
| … | **Turnaround** — the camera is repositioned for the reverse angle; relight (the biggest time cost in coverage) |
| ~6h | **Lunch** — must break within 6 hours of call or incur meal penalties |
| ~6h–11h | Second half; more setups |
| 11h–12h | Last shot ("**the martini**"), then **wrap**: strike, load, secure |
| +12h | **Turnaround clock starts** — minimum rest before next call |

A day is measured in **setups completed** and **pages in the can**, both
reported on the DPR against what the schedule promised.

## 4. Coverage

**Coverage** is the set of angles from which a scene is shot, giving the editor
options. Standard grammar:

- **Master** — the whole scene, wide, from one angle.
- **Two-shot / over-the-shoulder (OTS)** — the conversational middle ground.
- **Singles / close-ups (CU)** — one per actor, both directions.
- **Inserts** — hands, objects, screens, notes.
- **Cutaways / reaction shots** — for rhythm and for repair.
- **Establishing shot** — the geography, often second unit.

Shooting a scene "**in the round**" or with **oners** (a single continuous
take) is faster on the day but strips the editor of options and makes the scene
un-fixable in post. Directors vary enormously in coverage philosophy, and it is
one of the most consequential choices in whether a film can be saved in the
cutting room.

**Screen direction** and the **180° rule** are the continuity constraints the
script supervisor polices; violating them without intent produces a scene that
cannot be cut together.

## 5. Time sinks — what actually loses days

In rough order of destructiveness:

1. **Company moves.** Relocating the unit mid-day: 1–3 hours gone.
2. **Lighting turnarounds** for coverage, especially large exteriors or
   night work.
3. **Weather.** Rain, wind noise, and — worst — inconsistent sun, which
   destroys continuity between shots of the same scene. Hence **cover sets**.
4. **Losing the light.** Exteriors have a hard deadline; "magic hour" is
   20–40 usable minutes and cannot be extended.
5. **Actor unavailability or unpreparedness.** Late arrivals, illness, and
   scenes that need more rehearsal than the schedule assumed.
6. **Director indecision.** Not knowing the shot when the unit is standing by.
7. **Technical failures.** Camera, generator, or data problems.
8. **SFX/stunt resets.** A pyro or water gag can take hours to reset for a
   second take, so it is often covered by multiple cameras and shot once.
9. **Child and animal work limits.**
10. **Crowd management** on location with public access.

## 6. Night, weather, and special conditions

- **Night shoots** pay a premium in some agreements, degrade crew performance,
  and require careful scheduling blocks — a unit cannot flip between day and
  night work without a reset period.
- **Day for night** (shooting day, grading to night) is cheaper and looks it;
  **night for night** is expensive and looks right.
- **Water work** — slow, dangerous, requires marine coordinators, safety
  divers, and tanks; the standard estimate is that everything takes twice as
  long.
- **Aerial and drone work** — licensed, weather-bound, and separately
  scheduled.
- **Cold, altitude, desert** — reduce achievable hours and increase medical
  and welfare requirements.

## 7. Overtime, turnaround, and penalties

Hard constraints, negotiated by union agreement:

- **Standard day**: typically 8 hours at base rate, then time-and-a-half, then
  double time, with a "**golden time**" tier beyond a threshold on some
  agreements.
- **Meal penalties**: escalating per-person charges for every increment past
  the 6-hour meal deadline. On a 200-person crew these mount very quickly.
- **Turnaround**: a minimum rest period (commonly 10 hours, and longer over a
  weekend — "**weekend turnaround**"). Breaking it means paying a **forced
  call** penalty for every affected crew member.
- **6th and 7th day**: premium rates.
- **Distant location**: per diem, housing, and travel days are all
  compensable.

Consequence: pushing a unit to finish a day is not free, and the marginal cost
of the last two hours can exceed the cost of the first eight.

## 8. Safety

Legally the 1st AD's responsibility on the floor, and the producer's overall.

- **Risk assessments** and **safety meetings** before any hazardous sequence,
  documented and signed.
- **Firearms**: the armourer controls all weapons; industry practice bans live
  ammunition entirely, requires cold-weapon checks, and restricts who may
  handle them. This is regulated and enforced, and failures here have been
  fatal.
- **Stunts**: never performed without a coordinator; performers are qualified
  and rehearsed; medics on standby.
- **Fatigue** is a recognised safety issue — the long-hours culture has
  produced fatal driving accidents, and turnaround rules exist partly for this.
- **Set closure**: closed sets for nudity/intimacy, with an intimacy
  coordinator and consent-based choreography.

## 9. Dailies

**Dailies** (UK: **rushes**) are the previous day's material, synced and
graded to a viewing standard, distributed same-day.

- The **DIT/data wrangler** offloads and verifies; the **post house** or
  in-house dailies operation syncs to sound and applies a **look LUT**.
- Distributed securely (watermarked streaming, historically screened in a
  theatre) to the director, DP, editor, producers, studio executives, and
  relevant HODs.
- Their function is *quality control and reassurance*: is focus good, is the
  performance there, does the set read, is there a technical fault while the
  set still exists to reshoot on.
- The **editor's assembly** builds alongside, so scenes can be checked for
  whether they cut together before the location is lost.

---

## 10. Reshoots, additional photography, and pickups

Three distinct things, often conflated:

- **Pickups** — small missing pieces (an insert, a line, a reaction) shot
  cheaply with a minimal unit.
- **Additional photography** — planned, budgeted extra days after the main
  shoot; on tentpoles this is normal and scheduled from the start, not a
  distress signal.
- **Reshoots** — re-shooting material that exists because it didn't work,
  usually triggered by an assembly screening or a test screening
  (`07-postproduction.md` §9). Expensive: the unit, cast, and sets must be
  reassembled, and cast availability may have evaporated.

Practical constraints: actors' appearance changes (hair, weight, other jobs),
sets have been struck, locations are unavailable, and reassembly costs more
per day than the original shoot.

---

## 11. Multiple units and blocks

Large films run:

- **Main unit** — director, principal cast.
- **Second unit** — see `05-departments-and-crew.md` §16.
- **Splinter unit** — a small crew splitting off the main unit for a few hours.
- **Blocks** — the schedule divided into location or stage phases, sometimes
  with gaps (a **hiatus**) for construction, actor availability, or seasonal
  requirements.

---

## 12. Wrap

- **Wrap of a cast member** ("that's a wrap for…") ends their contract days.
- **Unit wrap** — strike, return of equipment, restoring locations, wrap
  parties, and the closing of the production office over the following weeks.
- **Wrap book / cost final** — the accountant closes the production accounts,
  which then feed the incentive audit and the studio's cost of the negative.
- Sets are **struck** unless a reshoot hold is paid for — a real decision, since
  holding a stage costs weekly rent but re-building is far worse.

---

## 13. What the shoot hands to post

- Camera media and its backups, with camera reports.
- Sound recordings, sound reports, and timecode sync.
- The **lined script**, **script supervisor's notes**, and **circle takes**.
- Continuity stills.
- **VFX data**: plates, HDRIs, LiDAR scans, camera and lens metadata, tracking
  markers, reference photography (`08-vfx-and-specialty.md`).
- The editor's ongoing assembly.
- The daily production reports, which are the record of what happened.
