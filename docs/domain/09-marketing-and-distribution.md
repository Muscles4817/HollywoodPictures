# 09 — Marketing & Distribution

*Manufacturing awareness, and choosing when and where to spend it.* Marketing
is the second budget, frequently as large as the film's negative cost, and it
is spent almost entirely before anyone knows whether the film works.

> **Domain reference.** Real industry, not this game. See `README.md`.

---

## 1. P&A: the second budget

**P&A** = Prints and Advertising (the "prints" half is now DCP delivery and
virtual print fees, a rounding error; the spend is advertising).

Directional US-domestic P&A:

| Release type | Domestic P&A |
|---|---|
| Platform / specialty | $2M–$15M |
| Wide independent | $15M–$30M |
| Studio mid-budget wide | $30M–$60M |
| Studio tentpole | $100M–$200M+ (global, all-in) |

Rules of thumb:

- A wide studio release costs roughly **$30M–$50M minimum** in domestic
  marketing simply to establish awareness.
- Global marketing on a tentpole often approximates **the negative cost**.
- Marketing spend is **committed weeks before release** and is largely
  unrecoverable — the media is bought, the trailers are in cinemas. This is why
  studios rarely dump a big film quietly: they have already spent.

### 1.1 Who builds the number, and who signs it

**Who** — the P&A budget is drafted by the **VP/SVP of Marketing Finance**
(sometimes titled Marketing Operations or Marketing Planning) working to the
**President of Marketing**, with line inputs from **Media**, **Creative
Advertising**, **Publicity**, **Promotions**, **Field**, and **Research**.
**Distribution** supplies the release pattern and screen assumptions, because
the number of locations drives the floor. **Finance/Corporate Strategy**
models it against the greenlight P&L (`11-money-accounting-and-participations.md`
§5). The **studio chairman** or the **Motion Picture Group president** signs the
final commitment; on a tentpole it may go to the parent company's CFO.

**When** — a **placeholder P&A** exists at greenlight, 18–30 months out,
because the greenlight model cannot compute a break-even without it. The real
budget is built in three passes:

| Pass | When | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **Greenlight placeholder** | At greenlight | A slate-average number by budget tier; often literally a rule (e.g. 0.8× negative cost domestic) |
| **Planning budget** | 9–12 months out | The first real build, channel by channel, after positioning is set (§6) and the release date is locked (§7.1) |
| **Committed budget** | 8–12 weeks out | Media is actually bought; from here the number is largely fixed and largely unrecoverable |
| **Live reallocation** | Final 3 weeks, and the opening weekend itself | Money moves between markets, channels and dayparts, but the total barely moves |

### 1.2 How the money is actually allocated

A wide domestic studio campaign, directionally, as a share of domestic P&A:

| Line | Share | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Media (paid)** | 65–80% | The bulk. TV/CTV, digital, outdoor, audio, cinema |
| **Creative production** | 8–15% | Trailer house fees and finishing, key art, TV spot finishing, music licences, photo shoots |
| **Publicity** | 3–7% | Junkets, premiere, press tour travel, screenings, unit stills/EPK amortised |
| **Research** | 1–3% | Tracking subscription, trailer and art testing, exit polling |
| **Field & promotions** | 2–5% | Screenings, radio, grassroots, partnership execution costs |
| **Prints/DCP & delivery** | 1–2% | Virtual print fees, KDMs, localisation masters |
| **Contingency** | 3–5% | Held by the President of Marketing for the final-week heavy-up |

Two structural facts that outsiders miss:

- **The creative line is small and the media line is huge.** Making the trailer
  costs a fraction of what showing it costs. This is why a studio will happily
  pay for five competing trailer cuts: the creative is cheap relative to the
  media it will spend, and a better trailer makes every media dollar work
  harder. Creative is the **leverage**; media is the **cost**.
- **Publicity is cheap and enormously efficient**, which is why talent
  cooperation is negotiated hard (`12-talent-labor-and-careers.md` §6). A star
  on a late-night couch is a national TV spot the studio did not pay for.

### 1.3 What sets the floor and the ceiling

The floor is **structural, not strategic**. To open wide you must:

- Buy enough national reach to get the target audience to ~85–90% awareness
  (§5.2). Below that the film opens invisibly regardless of quality.
- Produce a full asset package: teaser, trailer, 8–20 TV spots, dozens of
  digital cutdowns, a one-sheet plus 20–60 art variants, international
  adaptations.
- Support the exhibitor relationship — a circuit that gives you 3,500
  locations expects to see the film advertised, and quietly notices when it
  isn't.

The ceiling is set by the **break-even** the film can plausibly reach. The
crude studio arithmetic:

```
Domestic break-even (theatrical only, very rough):
  negative cost + domestic P&A  ≈  domestic gross × ~0.5
  → a $40M film with $40M domestic P&A needs ~$160M domestic
    from theatrical alone to break even before any other window.
```

Nobody actually break-evens on theatrical alone any more, which is why the
real model includes the licensing windows (§11) — but the ratio explains why
P&A is the number most likely to turn a modestly profitable film into a loss.
**Over-spending on a small film is a more common studio error than
under-spending on a big one**, because the downside is invisible: the film
performs respectably and still loses money.

### 1.4 How P&A flexes with performance

P&A is not one decision. It is a series of **commit points**, each of which is
a chance to add or stop.

| Decision point | When | What moves |
|---|---|---|
| **Planning lock** | 9–12 months | Total envelope and release pattern |
| **Upfront TV commitments** | 4–9 months | Long-lead national inventory bought early for price |
| **Heavy-up decision** | 6–8 weeks | Whether the contingency goes in; driven by first tracking and trailer test scores |
| **Final week** | Days −7 to 0 | Market-level and daypart-level reallocation; adding weekend spots |
| **Saturday morning** | Opening weekend | The most consequential meeting of the campaign (below) |
| **Week 2 sustain or cut** | Monday after opening | Whether to keep spending |

**The Saturday morning call.** Friday's actual gross (and Thursday previews)
arrives overnight. The President of Marketing, President of Distribution, the
studio chairman and Research meet — often by phone, early — and decide:

- **A hit**: sustain, sometimes increase. Money shifts from "come see it" to
  "everyone is seeing it" — new spots cut around the opening number, quotes,
  audience reaction, CinemaScore if it is good.
- **In line**: hold the plan and let it play.
- **A miss**: cut. Cancel the following week's national buys where the
  cancellation windows allow, pull back to the markets that over-performed, and
  redirect the remaining money to holding the film's core audience rather than
  recruiting a new one. Some spend is genuinely unrecoverable — outdoor is
  posted, cinema trailers are attached, and much of the digital is committed.

The asymmetry matters: **you can add money faster than you can remove it.**
Cancellation windows on national television are typically 2–4 weeks for
non-scatter inventory; digital can be turned off in hours. This is a real
reason campaigns are back-weighted into digital.

### 1.5 Co-financing, splits, and other people's money

P&A is not always the distributor's alone:

- **Co-financing partners** typically fund their share of P&A alongside their
  share of negative cost (`03-financing-and-dealmaking.md` §1), and get a
  consultation right, not control.
- **International distributors** on an independent film fund their own
  territory P&A entirely, which is why a film's campaign can be radically
  different in France than in the US (§15).
- **Promotional partners** contribute *media weight*, not cash: a fast-food
  chain running your character on 6,000 store fronts and its own TV buy is
  worth $20M–$60M of equivalent media on a big family title, at no P&A cost
  (§5.5).
- **Exhibitor co-op** — modest local advertising money contributed by
  circuits, mostly historical now but still present in some markets.
- **Talent P&A commitments** — occasionally a star or director's deal
  specifies a *minimum* P&A spend or a minimum release width, which is a
  contractual floor the studio cannot quietly drop below.

### 1.6 How P&A goes wrong

| Failure | What it looks like | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Placeholder becomes real** | The greenlight rule-of-thumb number is never rebuilt for the actual film | A drama carries a tentpole media plan, or a genre film is starved |
| **Front-loaded spend** | Money spent 4 months out on awareness, nothing left for urgency | High awareness, low first choice; the film "everyone had heard of and nobody saw" |
| **Late heavy-up on a dead film** | Tracking is soft, so more money is thrown at it | Awareness rises, conversion doesn't; the extra spend is pure loss |
| **No contingency** | The whole envelope is committed at planning lock | No ability to respond to a strong trailer test or a rival's date move |
| **Ignoring the ceiling** | P&A set by ambition rather than plausible gross | A profitable $18M film turned into a $10M loss |
| **Unbalanced international** | Domestic-scale campaign, token international | Territories that could have paid for the film open to nothing (§15) |

## 2. Who actually does it

Marketing is a **studio department**, not a production one. Nobody who made the
film works on the campaign, and the filmmakers' involvement is a contractual
consultation right, not control. This is a structural source of conflict:
the campaign that sells a film best is often not the campaign its director
wants.

| Role | Owns |
|---|---|
| **President of Marketing** | The whole campaign; sits on the greenlight committee, because "can we sell it?" is a greenlight question |
| **Creative Advertising** | Trailers, TV spots, key art, digital creative. Commissions and supervises outside agencies; runs the testing of every asset |
| **Media Buying** | Where the money goes: TV, digital, outdoor, cinema. The largest share of the spend, and the most measurable |
| **Publicity** | Earned media — press, junkets, premieres, festivals, talent tours, EPK. Manages the review embargo |
| **Promotions & Partnerships** | Brand tie-ins, retail, fast food, sweepstakes — other people's money advertising your film |
| **Research** | Tracking, testing, exit polling; the department everyone else's decisions are hostage to (§8) |
| **Field marketing** | Regional and local activity, screenings, radio, grassroots |
| **International marketing** | The whole above, per territory, with local creative and local dating |
| **Unit publicist** (production side) | The only marketing person on set: stills, EPK, set visits, behind-the-scenes |

Outside vendors do most of the creative: **trailer houses** (specialists who
cut on spec, competitively — several houses may cut the same trailer and the
studio picks) and **key art houses** (who deliver dozens of poster concepts per
film). This is why trailers have a house style independent of the film's.

**Filmmaker involvement** varies by deal: consultation on key art and trailer,
approval for a small number of powerful directors, and — separately — **talent
likeness approval**, which is contractual for most stars and is a genuine
scheduling bottleneck on artwork.

### 2.1 The shape of the department

A major studio's domestic marketing department runs **150–400 people**;
international adds a similar number across regional hubs and local offices.
Reporting is functional, and every function has a per-film owner:

```
            Studio Chairman / Motion Picture Group President
                              │
                President of Marketing (Domestic)
                              │
   ┌──────────┬───────────┬───┴────┬───────────┬──────────┬─────────┐
Creative     Media      Publicity  Research  Promotions  Field   Marketing
Advertising  (paid)     (earned)                        Mktg     Finance
   │            │           │          │         │        │         │
 EVP/SVP      EVP/SVP     EVP/SVP    SVP      SVP      VP      SVP
   │            │           │
 VPs per     Planners /   Unit and
 title       buyers       title
 ("creative  (often an    publicists
  execs")    outside
             agency)
```

The unit of ownership is the **title team**: for each film, one creative
advertising executive, one media planner, one publicist, one research analyst,
one promotions executive, one field manager and one international counterpart.
They meet weekly on the title from roughly a year out, daily in the final
fortnight.

**Distribution is a separate department** with its own president, reporting to
the same studio head. Marketing and Distribution are peers who need each other
and frequently disagree — Distribution wants the widest release the exhibitors
will give; Marketing has to buy the awareness that justifies it.

### 2.2 The rhythms

| Meeting | Cadence | Who | What is decided |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Title meeting** | Weekly, from ~12 months out | The title team | Asset schedule, creative reviews, issues |
| **Creative review** | As assets arrive | President of Marketing, creative advertising, sometimes the chairman | Which cut, which art, what note |
| **Tracking meeting** | Weekly from ~5 weeks out, daily in the last 10 days | Marketing, Distribution, Research, chairman | Heavy-up, market weighting, projection |
| **Release calendar meeting** | Monthly, slate-wide | Distribution, Marketing, chairman | Dating and re-dating across the whole slate (§7.1) |
| **Weekend call** | Every Friday night / Saturday morning of a release | Marketing, Distribution, chairman | Sustain or cut (§1.4) |

### 2.3 What a great head of marketing does that a mediocre one does not

The skill axis for the job, which is not "having good taste in trailers":

1. **Finding the film's real audience, not its intended one.** The best
   marketing executives are the ones who watch a cut and say "this isn't the
   thriller you think you made, it's a revenge movie for women over 30" — and
   then rebuild the campaign around that. Most campaigns fail at this stage,
   not at execution.
2. **Saying "we can't sell this" at greenlight, loudly and early.** The
   President of Marketing is on the greenlight committee precisely so that
   unmarketable films are caught before they are made. A marketing head who
   only ever discovers the problem in the edit is failing at half the job.
3. **Ruthlessness about the hook.** Killing an asset everyone in the building
   loves because it does not answer "why would someone go this weekend".
4. **Reading research without being governed by it.** Tracking and testing are
   inputs with known biases (§8.4). Knowing when a genre film's numbers are
   structurally understated — and holding the plan — is a specific, expensive
   skill.
5. **Managing filmmakers without losing the campaign.** Getting a difficult
   director to a "yes" on a trailer they instinctively hate, without either
   capitulating or triggering a public fight (§14).
6. **Timing the money.** Correct escalation with the weight at the end, and the
   nerve not to spend the contingency early.
7. **Slate thinking.** No campaign exists alone. Trailer placement, date
   moves, and partnership capacity are traded across a whole year of releases,
   and the executives who win are the ones who play the slate rather than the
   film.
8. **Institutional credibility.** When the marketing head projects $28M and
   Distribution projects $35M, whose number the chairman believes is a
   function of years of accuracy. Credibility is the currency that lets you
   protect a film that tracks badly and is actually fine.

## 3. The campaign timeline

| When | Activity |
|---|---|
| **12–24 months out** | Title announcement, first-look image, comic-con/CinemaCon presentations, teaser for tentpoles |
| **6–9 months** | Teaser trailer, key art development, tracking begins |
| **3–4 months** | Full trailer, poster campaign, TV spot production, partnerships locked |
| **6–8 weeks** | Media buying heavy-up begins; long-lead press; junket |
| **3–4 weeks** | TV/digital saturation, talent press tour, late-night appearances |
| **1–2 weeks** | Premiere, press screenings, review embargo lifts, final push |
| **Opening weekend** | Front-loaded spend Thursday–Sunday |
| **Post-opening** | Spend adjusted to performance; a hit gets sustained, a miss gets cut |

**What each phase is actually buying:**

- **The early phase buys existence.** Announcement, first image, and teaser
  exist to put the film on the calendar in the audience's mind and to stake the
  date against rivals. Almost no one who sees a teaser buys a ticket because of
  it.
- **The middle phase buys comprehension.** The full trailer must land the
  premise, the tone, and the reason to care in 150 seconds. This is where
  campaigns are won and lost, and where a film that can't be explained in one
  sentence starts to fail.
- **The late phase buys urgency.** Saturation in the last three weeks converts
  awareness into "this weekend". It is the most expensive and most necessary
  part, and it cannot be substituted for by having started early.
- **Post-opening buys legs, or admits defeat.** Spend after the first weekend
  is a live decision made on Saturday morning from Friday's number.

**The timing relationship with production**: marketing needs finished material
long before the film is finished. Trailers are cut 6–9 months before release
from an unlocked cut, frequently using shots that don't survive
(`07-postproduction.md` §2), and sometimes from footage shot specially for the
trailer. Key art needs approved likenesses months out. A film that finishes
late doesn't just risk its date — it starves its own campaign of material.

### 3.1 The asset delivery calendar

The campaign timeline is really a **materials** timeline, and each item has a
lead time that production has to serve:

| Asset | Needed by | Lead time to make | What it depends on |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Title and logo** | 18–24 months | 4–12 weeks | Legal clearance, international translation check |
| **First-look still** | 12–18 months | Days, from unit stills | Talent likeness approval |
| **Teaser** | 6–12 months | 6–10 weeks from footage | Usable footage; often pre-VFX, often shot for the purpose |
| **Gallery / key art shoot** | 9–14 months | 1–2 days of shoot, 6–10 weeks of retouching | Cast availability *during production*, in costume |
| **Teaser one-sheet** | 6–9 months | 8–16 weeks of concepting | Approved logo, approved likenesses |
| **Main trailer** | 3–5 months | 8–14 weeks, multiple houses | A cut coherent enough to sell from |
| **Final one-sheet** | 3–4 months | 12–20 weeks total | Billing block from Legal; likeness approvals |
| **TV spots** | 6–10 weeks | 4–8 weeks, iterating weekly | The trailer's approved creative direction |
| **EPK / behind-the-scenes** | 4–6 months | Shot during production | A unit publicist actually being funded |
| **International adaptations** | 4–6 months | 6–12 weeks per region | Locked domestic creative to adapt from |

Two consequences practitioners take for granted:

- **The poster shoot happens during principal photography**, not after. Cast
  are contracted, in costume, in makeup, and in one place exactly once. Missing
  that window means a composite built from frame grabs and stock plates, which
  is why some posters look like the actors were never in the same room — they
  weren't.
- **Marketing's deadline is earlier than post's.** The trailer deadline is a
  real constraint on the edit (`07-postproduction.md` §2): editorial is
  routinely asked to deliver finished-looking VFX shots months before the film
  needs them, and those "trailer shots" are prioritised in the VFX schedule
  (`08-vfx-and-specialty.md` §2) at real cost.

## 4. Creative advertising

Made by specialist agencies (trailer houses, key art houses), not by the film's
own crew.

- **Teaser** (60–90s), **theatrical trailer** (~2:30 — a self-imposed MPA
  guideline, one over-length trailer per film permitted), **TV spots** (:15,
  :30, :60), digital cutdowns, and vertical-format social edits.
- **Key art** — the one-sheet, plus billboards, bus sides, and digital
  variants. Often dozens of concepts tested before one is chosen.
- **Trailers are cut from unfinished films**, frequently with shots that don't
  survive the final cut, and often from footage delivered specially for the
  purpose.
- **Green band** (all audiences) vs **red band** (restricted) trailers.
- Approvals: studio marketing, filmmakers (varying by contract), and talent
  likeness approvals in key art — a major star's approval rights over their own
  image on a poster are real and slow.

### 4.1 How a trailer actually gets made

**Who** — the studio's **creative advertising executive** on the title owns
it. The work is done by **trailer houses** (also "AV houses"): independent
agencies of 20–200 people staffed with **trailer editors**, **copywriters**,
**producers**, **graphics/finishing artists**, and their own **music
supervisors**. The editors are a distinct craft from film editors
(`07-postproduction.md` §1) — they cut for persuasion, not for story, and very
few people are good at both.

**When** — the teaser process starts 8–14 months out, the main trailer 4–7
months out. Each round takes 2–4 weeks. A tentpole trailer campaign will run 6
months and produce hundreds of cuts.

The sequence:

| Stage | What happens | Who |
|---|---|---|
| **The brief** | The studio writes a creative brief: positioning, target quadrant, what the film is, what must be conveyed, what must *not* be revealed, tone references, mandatory elements | Creative advertising exec, President of Marketing, Research |
| **Footage delivery** | Selects, dailies, an assembly or rough cut, plus VFX in whatever state exists. Often a "trailer pull" of specific shots requested from editorial | Studio post, editorial, VFX |
| **Spec round** | **3–8 houses** are given the same footage and brief and cut competitively, usually unpaid or on a small fee, on the promise that the winner gets the paid finishing work | Trailer houses |
| **Internal screening** | The studio watches all the specs back to back in a screening room. This is the decisive meeting | President of Marketing, creative exec, sometimes the chairman |
| **Direction chosen** | Usually one house's cut wins outright; sometimes the studio takes the structure from one and the music from another and asks a house to combine them | Studio |
| **Refinement rounds** | 3–10 rounds of notes over 4–8 weeks. Shots swapped, VO rewritten, music changed, act breaks moved | Studio + winning house |
| **Filmmaker pass** | The director sees it, at a stage that varies enormously by deal (§14) | Director, producers |
| **Testing** | The cut goes to a research panel; scores come back against genre norms | Research (§4.4) |
| **Finishing** | Online conform, colour, graphics, title treatment, 5.1/Atmos mix, music licences cleared | Trailer house finishing team |
| **Approvals** | MPA green/red band card, legal clearance of claims, billing block if present, talent likeness | MPA, Legal, Talent |
| **Delivery** | DCP for cinemas, broadcast masters, dozens of digital and social ratios | Studio ops |

**Costs, directional**: a spec cut costs a house $10K–$40K of its own money and
time. The winning house's creative fee runs **$50K–$150K**; **finishing**
(conform, graphics, mix, colour) adds **$40K–$120K**; a bespoke **trailer music
cue** from a trailer-music house is $5K–$40K; a famous **needle drop** licensed
for a trailer runs **$50K–$500K+**, and occasionally more than that for the
right song on the right tentpole. A full campaign's AV creative — teaser, main
trailer, 15–25 TV spots, 50–200 digital cutdowns — lands around **$1M–$4M** on
a wide release, i.e. a few percent of media.

**Why spec.** The studio is buying **options on ideas**, cheaply. Five houses
will find five different films inside the same footage, and one of them will
find the one that sells. Houses accept the odds because winning a tentpole
campaign is a year of finishing revenue and a reference credit. The cost of
this system is that the people who define how the film is perceived have often
never spoken to the director and have not read the script.

### 4.2 The anatomy of a modern trailer

The dominant structure is a **three-act escalation with a button**, and it is
remarkably stable across genres:

```
0:00  Distributor/studio logos, MPA green-band card
0:05  COLD OPEN — one image or one line that stops the scroll.
      On digital cuts this compresses to ~2 seconds; the first
      3 seconds are the entire battle for a skippable pre-roll.
0:15  ACT ONE — the world and the character. Diegetic sound,
      sparse or no score. "Here is a person you like, in a place."
0:45  THE TURN — the inciting incident. The premise lands here.
      Usually a line of dialogue that *is* the logline.
0:55  TITLE/BRAND CARD — franchise logo, or "FROM THE DIRECTOR OF…",
      or the studio's tentpole ident. Music enters properly.
1:00  ACT TWO — escalation montage. Stakes, scale, set pieces, the
      antagonist, the promise of the premise. Music builds.
1:35  THE BUTTON / BREAKDOWN — hard cut to black and near-silence,
      or a single sustained note. This is the reset that makes
      act three land. Modern trailers live or die on this beat.
1:45  ACT THREE — the drop. Rapid montage cut to the music, 1–2
      second shots, the biggest images, the loudest moment.
2:10  TITLE CARD — the actual main title, full screen.
2:15  THE TAG / STINGER — one last joke, scare, or line after the
      title. Comedies live here; the final laugh is the one people
      quote.
2:20  DATE / BILLING BLOCK / "ONLY IN THEATERS"
2:30  Out.
```

Vocabulary a trailer editor uses in the room: **the drop** (where the music
breaks into the final montage), **the button** (the silence before it), **the
riser** (the ascending sound design under act two), **braams** (the low brass
hits, now a cliché the good houses avoid), **whips** (the whoosh between quick
cuts), **the fake-out** (implying one genre before revealing another), **needle
drop** (a licensed song), **the bug** (the date/logo lower third), **VO**
(voice-over — largely extinct in features since the 2000s outside comedy and
family), and **the billing block** (the contractually specified credit text,
whose contents, order and relative size are dictated by talent agreements and
which Legal, not Marketing, controls).

**Format variants that exist for every film**, and the reason "the trailer" is
a misleading singular:

| Asset | Typical count on a wide release |
|---|---|
| Teasers | 1–2 |
| Main trailers | 2–3 (trailer 1, trailer 2, sometimes a final "in theaters Friday" cut) |
| Red band versions | 0–2 |
| International trailers | 1–3 base versions, then 20–50 localised (subtitled, dubbed, re-titled) |
| TV spots | 15–40 (:15, :30, :60, plus genre-, cast-, and review-focused variants) |
| Digital/social cutdowns | 50–300 (16:9, 1:1, 9:16, 6s bumpers, 15s, 30s) |
| Featurettes / behind-the-scenes | 3–10 |
| Cinema-specific | Big-format and 3D-specific versions |

### 4.3 What makes a trailer work — and what makes it fail

The skill axis for a trailer editor, which is not "cutting fast to music":

1. **Finding the sell inside footage that doesn't obviously contain it.** The
   great trailer editors watch 40 hours of dailies and identify the six shots
   and two lines that constitute a reason to go. This is a *reading* skill
   before it is a cutting one.
2. **Structure over spectacle.** A trailer with a clear act break, one turn
   and one button beats a faster, louder trailer with no shape. Audiences
   respond to the *rhythm* of an argument.
3. **Music as architecture, not decoration.** The cut is built to the music;
   the drop is chosen first and everything else is timed to it. Trailer editors
   temp obsessively and the music choice is usually the single biggest
   determinant of whether a cut plays.
4. **Restraint about the best material.** Knowing that showing the twist, the
   best joke, the monster, or the third-act reveal buys a point of interest now
   and costs word of mouth later (§12.3).
5. **Legibility at low attention.** The trailer must survive being watched at
   half-volume on a phone with the first three seconds deciding everything.
   The good houses cut a 2:30 that also works as a 6-second bumper.
6. **Tone control.** Landing exactly one tonal promise. A trailer that is
   funny for 40 seconds and then serious for 40 seconds sells nothing to
   anybody.
7. **Casting the trailer.** Deciding which of five actors is the trailer's
   protagonist — frequently not the film's — because one of them is the reason
   the audience will go.

Failure modes:

| Failure | What it looks like | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **The whole plot** | The trailer narrates the film beginning to end, including the third act | Nothing left to discover; interest without urgency |
| **Spending the best joke** | The one genuinely funny beat is in the :30 spot | The film plays flat to an audience that has already laughed |
| **No premise by 0:45** | Atmosphere and montage with no stated situation | "Aware, not interested" in tracking (§8.3) |
| **Tone mismatch** | Sold as a broad comedy; the film is a bleak drama | Great opening, catastrophic second weekend, D CinemaScore |
| **Shots that aren't in the film** | Trailer VFX or scenes cut in post | Audience betrayal; a recurring social-media news story and occasionally litigation |
| **Genre invisibility** | Three houses cut it and nobody can say what genre it is | The film has no audience to buy media against |
| **Cliché signature** | Braams, whip cuts, and a slowed-down cover of a pop song | Indistinguishable from the six other trailers in the same block |
| **Overlength** | 3-minute trailers to fit everything in | Drop-off before the sell; and only one over-length trailer per film is permitted |
| **Wrong protagonist** | The trailer's lead is not the film's | Mis-set expectations; a specific driver of bad exit polling |

### 4.4 How trailers are tested

Every significant asset is tested before it runs, by the research department
(§8) through an outside vendor.

**Method** — a recruited online panel of **300–800 respondents** matched to the
target audience watches the cut once, in a controlled player, and then answers:

- **Definite interest** — "How interested would you be in seeing this film in a
  theatre?" on a five-point scale; the top box is the headline number.
- **Norms comparison** — the score is meaningless alone and is always reported
  against **genre and season norms** ("a 34 definite interest against a horror
  norm of 27").
- **Comprehension open-ends** — "In your own words, what is this movie about?"
  This is the most diagnostic question in the whole apparatus. If a third of
  respondents can't say, the campaign has a positioning problem, not a
  trailer problem.
- **Genre attribution** — "What kind of movie is this?"
- **Appeal drivers and detractors** — what made them want to see it, what put
  them off.
- **Moment-by-moment** — on some tests, a dial or click track showing where
  attention drops.

Assets are frequently tested **head to head**: two cuts from two houses, split
sample. Key art is tested the same way, often with **eye-tracking or first-
glance recall** (respondents see the poster for 3 seconds and are asked what
they remember).

**How studios read it.** The absolute number matters less than three things:
the delta against norm, the comprehension open-ends, and whether the target
quadrant scores well even if the total does not. A cut that scores badly
overall but wins its primary quadrant convincingly is usually the right cut.

**How it misleads.** Trailer testing over-rewards familiarity — a cut for a
sequel or a known IP will beat an original with the same quality of execution.
It rewards clarity over intrigue, which biases the system toward trailers that
explain too much. And panels self-select toward heavy moviegoers, which
understates the mainstream response to broad four-quadrant material.

### 4.5 Key art: how a poster actually gets made

**Who** — the studio's creative advertising **print** group (a separate team
from AV), commissioning **key art agencies** ("print houses"): design studios
of 10–80 people with **art directors**, **designers**, **retouchers**,
**illustrators**, **typographers**, and **photo producers**.

**When** — art development starts 9–14 months out for a teaser one-sheet and
runs continuously until 8–10 weeks before release, when outdoor has to be
printed and posted.

The sequence:

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Brief** | A written positioning brief plus a look-book of references; the same brief goes to **5–15 agencies simultaneously** |
| **Concept round** | Each agency returns **20–100 comps** — rough compositions built from stills, stock, and sketch. A tentpole will generate **1,000–3,000 comps in total across all rounds** |
| **Cull** | The studio picks 5–20 directions to develop, usually in a room with the boards on a wall |
| **Photo shoot** | A dedicated **gallery shoot** during production: cast in costume, shot against grey or white, lit for compositing. One or two days, $75K–$500K |
| **Development rounds** | Chosen directions refined over 4–12 weeks; typography, title treatment, colour, composition |
| **Testing** | Panel testing of 3–8 finalists (§4.4) |
| **Approvals** | Studio marketing → filmmakers (consultation or approval) → **talent likeness approval**, per contract, per actor → Legal for the billing block |
| **Adaptation** | The chosen art is rebuilt for 20–60 formats: one-sheet, banner, bus shelter, wallscape, 24-sheet, digital square, vertical, thumbnail, plus international |
| **Print and post** | Outdoor is booked and printed 4–8 weeks before it goes up |

**Costs, directional**: agency concept rounds are paid at **$5K–$25K per
agency per round**; the winning agency's finishing and adaptation work runs
**$100K–$400K**; the gallery shoot and retouching add **$150K–$600K**; a full
international art package can double it.

**The likeness bottleneck is real.** A star with photo approval may reject
every image of themselves in a round, and the process restarts. Approval
clauses commonly specify a number of images the actor pre-approves from a
contact sheet, a turnaround window (often 5–10 business days), and rules on
retouching. Several stars have approval over *placement and size* as well as
image, which is why poster credit order is negotiated by lawyers and not
designers.

### 4.6 What makes a poster work

1. **One idea, readable in one second, at 40 feet and at 40 pixels.** The
   working test is whether the composition survives being shrunk to a phone
   thumbnail in a streaming grid. Most posters are now consumed at that size.
2. **Genre is legible before the title is read.** Colour, typography and
   composition should tell the audience what kind of night out this is before
   any information is processed. Orange-and-teal action, desaturated prestige,
   high-contrast red horror, warm pastel comedy — these conventions exist
   because they work as signals, and the craft is using them without looking
   generic.
3. **The star is recognisable.** For a star vehicle, this is the whole job, and
   it is a specific technical problem: a compositing job that flattens a face
   into an unrecognisable retouched mask defeats the reason the star was cast.
4. **The title treatment does real work.** Custom lettering is a substantial
   part of the design budget and carries genre and tone independently of the
   image.
5. **It is distinctive within its own block.** Great art is judged against the
   wall of the multiplex lobby it will hang on, not in isolation.
6. **It scales through the whole format family.** A composition that only works
   as a vertical one-sheet is a failure, because the majority of impressions
   will be horizontal or square.

Poster failure modes: the **floating heads** collage that says nothing; the
**back-to-back leads over a city** that is indistinguishable from a hundred
others; the **too-dark image** that dies on a phone screen and on a bus shelter
at night; the **title in the wrong place** so it is covered by streaming
platform UI; the **committee composite** where every stakeholder's mandatory
element has been added until nothing reads; and the **wrong genre signal**,
which is the most expensive because it puts media weight behind attracting the
wrong audience.

### 4.7 What separates a great creative advertising executive

1. **Writing a brief that contains an argument**, not a description. "Sell the
   friendship, not the heist" is a brief; "capture the tone of the film" is
   not, and it produces five weeks of wasted spec.
2. **Knowing which house to call.** Houses have specialisms — one cuts horror,
   one cuts comedy, one is unmatched at family, one has the best music
   department. Casting the roster is half the job.
3. **Giving a note that identifies the problem, not the fix.** "The premise
   isn't landing until 1:10" is useful; "put the car shot earlier" wastes a
   round.
4. **Protecting a good cut from the building.** Every executive who sees a
   trailer has a note. The exec's job is to filter, not to transmit.
5. **Knowing when to stop.** Trailers get worse after round eight. The
   difference between a good campaign and a mushy one is often that someone
   stopped taking notes.
6. **Managing the filmmaker relationship** so the director's genuine insight
   about their own film gets in, and their instinct to under-sell does not
   (§14).

## 5. Media buying and channels

- **Television** — still the largest single line for wide releases, targeted
  at broad reach; sport is the premium buy.
- **Digital and social** — targeted, measurable, increasingly dominant;
  influencer and creator campaigns.
- **Outdoor** — billboards, transit, wraps; high-impact awareness for events.
- **In-cinema** — trailers attached to comparable films; the single most
  efficient channel because the audience is self-selected. Trailer placement
  is negotiated with exhibitors.
- **Publicity (earned media)** — junkets, premieres, talk shows, festival
  slots, magazine covers. Cheap relative to paid media and dependent on talent
  cooperation, which is contractually obligated for a specified number of days.
- **Promotional partners** — fast food, automotive, beverage, retail tie-ins.
  A partner spends *their* money advertising *their* product with your film,
  producing enormous incremental awareness at no cost. Only available to
  family/four-quadrant titles, which is a real structural advantage for them.

### 5.1 How a media plan is actually built

**Who** — a **media director** and **planners** inside the studio, working
with an **outside media agency** that holds the buying relationships and the
volume discounts. Buying is split by channel: **national broadcast/cable
buyers**, **local/spot buyers** (market by market), **digital/programmatic
traders**, **out-of-home planners**, and **audio**. Research supplies the
target definition; Distribution supplies the location list.

**When** — planning starts 6–9 months out. **Upfront** commitments for national
television inventory are made months ahead for price; **scatter** inventory is
bought closer in at a premium. Digital is bought progressively and can be
adjusted daily. Outdoor is booked 8–12 weeks ahead because it has to be
printed and physically posted.

The build, in order:

| Step | What is decided | Inputs |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Target definition** | Who exactly are we buying — "A18-34, skewing male, heavy moviegoers, top 30 DMAs" | Positioning (§6), Research |
| **2. Reach and frequency goal** | E.g. 88% reach of target at 6+ average frequency in the final 21 days | Genre norms, comps |
| **3. Budget envelope** | The media line from P&A (§1.2) | Marketing Finance |
| **4. Channel mix** | Share to TV/CTV, digital, OOH, audio, cinema | Target's media consumption; genre precedent |
| **5. Flighting** | When money lands week by week — the spend curve | Release date; competitive calendar |
| **6. Market weighting** | National vs the top 10/25/50 DMAs; where to over-index | Comps by market; the film's regional skew |
| **7. Inventory selection** | Which programmes, which platforms, which placements | Buyers |
| **8. Reserved contingency** | 10–20% held back for the final fortnight | President of Marketing |

**The spend curve** for a wide release is heavily back-loaded, roughly:

```
% of total media spend by week before release
 W-8  W-7  W-6  W-5  W-4  W-3  W-2  W-1  OPEN WK
  2%   3%   4%   6%   9%  14%  20%  28%   14%
        └── awareness ──┘ └──── urgency ────┘
```

Roughly **60–70% of the media lands in the final three weeks**, and the single
biggest day of spending is the Thursday and Friday of the opening weekend
itself. Anything spent more than 8 weeks out is buying *existence*, not
tickets.

### 5.2 Reach, frequency, and what "enough" means

The two numbers the plan is actually judged on:

- **Reach** — the percentage of the target audience that sees the campaign at
  least once. For a wide studio release the goal is **85–92% of the target**.
  Below roughly 70% a wide release is structurally unable to open.
- **Frequency** — the average number of exposures per person reached.
  **Effective frequency** for a film is usually taken as **5–8 exposures** in
  the final three weeks. Below 3, the message doesn't stick; above about 12,
  the extra exposures largely stop converting and start irritating.
- **GRPs / TRPs** — Gross (or Target) Rating Points = reach × frequency. A
  wide release will buy roughly **1,500–3,000 TRPs** in the final month; a
  tentpole considerably more.

The practical consequence: **reach is bought first, frequency second.** A plan
that reaches 60% of the target ten times is worse than one that reaches 88% six
times, because the people you never reached cannot be converted at any price.
This is the single most common way an under-funded campaign is mis-built.

### 5.3 What each channel actually delivers per dollar

Directional, US, and moving constantly. CPM = cost per thousand impressions.

| Channel | Typical cost | CPM | What it is good at | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **National broadcast primetime :30** | $80K–$200K/spot | $25–$45 | Mass reach fast; the only way to add several reach points in a night | Expensive, unmeasurable, ageing audience |
| **Live sport** | $400K–$800K+ /spot; Super Bowl ~$7M | $40–$70 | Simultaneous mass reach; the only true "event" buy left | Cost; skews male |
| **Cable** | $5K–$60K/spot | $12–$30 | Genre targeting (horror on cable genre nets, family on kids nets) | Fragmenting audience |
| **CTV / streaming ad tiers** | Bought on CPM | $30–$60 | Reaches the cord-cutters TV lost; addressable | Frequency capping is imperfect; expensive |
| **YouTube pre-roll** | Cost-per-view $0.05–$0.20 | $10–$25 | Cheap reach for trailers; the natural home of a 2:30 asset | Skippable — the first 5 seconds are everything |
| **Meta / Instagram** | Auction | $8–$20 | Precise targeting, cheap frequency, retargeting | Low attention; needs vertical-native creative |
| **TikTok / short-form** | Auction | $6–$15 | Young audiences, cultural momentum, organic amplification | Requires genuinely native creative, not cutdowns |
| **Search** | CPC $0.50–$3 | — | Capturing existing intent ("showtimes near me") | Cannot create demand, only harvest it |
| **Out-of-home** | Premium LA/NY billboard $30K–$100K/month; national package $2M–$8M | $3–$10 | Scale signalling — telling the *industry* and the audience this is an event | No targeting, no measurement, long lead |
| **Audio / podcast** | $18–$50 CPM | $18–$50 | Cheap frequency; host-read endorsement converts unusually well | No visual |
| **In-cinema trailers** | Effectively traded with exhibitors, or bought via cinema advertising networks | $30–$60 nominal | **The highest-converting channel that exists** — the audience is in a cinema, on a Friday, already buying tickets | Tiny absolute reach; capped by what plays |
| **Ticketing platforms** | Fee/CPM deals | — | Bottom-of-funnel: presales, reminders, "on sale now" | Only reaches deciders |

Two things practitioners know that the CPM table hides:

- **In-cinema is the best channel and cannot be scaled.** A trailer in front of
  a comparable film converts at a rate no digital placement approaches, because
  the viewer is a moviegoer, in a cinema, in a receptive state, at full
  attention. But the number of impressions available is capped by the box
  office of the films you can attach to. Trailer placement is therefore a
  **negotiated and traded resource** with exhibitors and with other
  distributors, and losing the attachment to the year's biggest film is a
  material campaign setback.
- **Outdoor's real audience is partly the industry.** A wallscape on Sunset and
  a takeover of the Times Square subway station are bought partly to signal
  commitment to exhibitors, partners, talent and the trades. This is not
  irrational — screen counts (§7.7) are set by people who form impressions.

### 5.4 Market weighting and the final week

Media is not bought uniformly across the country. Standard practice:

- **National base** — broadcast, cable, CTV, digital run everywhere.
- **Spot heavy-up** — additional local buys in the top 10–50 **DMAs**
  (Designated Market Areas: Nielsen's market definitions — New York, LA,
  Chicago, Philadelphia, Dallas, and so on down).
- **Index-based weighting** — comps tell you where the genre over-performs.
  Urban markets over-index for some genres; the South and Midwest for faith,
  family and broad comedy; coastal and top-5 markets for prestige and
  specialty.
- **Platform releases** are bought almost entirely as spot: a 4-theatre New
  York/LA opening buys New York and LA press, outdoor and radio, and nothing
  else at all.

**The final week** is where a good media team earns its fee:

| Day | What happens |
|---|---|
| **T−10 to T−7** | Last tracking read informs the heavy-up; contingency released or held |
| **T−7** | Final national weight locked; digital budgets raised in under-indexing DMAs |
| **T−5 to T−3** | Presale data starts arriving from ticketing platforms — the first *behavioural* signal, far better than survey data. Buys shift toward markets with weak presales relative to comps |
| **T−3 to T−1** | Dayparting shifts to evenings and late night; social frequency capped up; search budgets uncapped |
| **Thursday** | Previews. Spend peaks. "Tonight" creative replaces "Friday" creative |
| **Friday–Saturday** | Live optimisation against actuals; the Saturday call (§1.4) decides the following week |

The final week's decisions are made on **presales and daily tracking**, not on
weekly research. A film whose presales index below comps in the South but above
in the Northeast will have money moved within 48 hours.

### 5.5 Promotional partnerships, in detail

**Who** — the **Promotions & Partnerships** department, working 12–24 months
ahead with brand marketing teams and their agencies.

**What a partner actually provides**: not cash to the studio (usually), but
**media weight and distribution**. A quick-service restaurant tie-in on a
family tentpole can be worth **$20M–$60M in equivalent media** — its own TV
buy featuring your characters, packaging in thousands of outlets, in-store
display, a toy programme, and digital. A beverage or automotive partner adds
national TV of their own. Some partners also pay a **licence fee** to the
studio, though on the biggest titles the studio is often the one competing for
the partner.

**Why only some films get it.** Brands need: a family or four-quadrant
audience, a release date locked far enough ahead for their own annual planning
cycle, characters or imagery that can appear on a cup without embarrassment,
and a PG/PG-13 rating. This is a **genuine structural advantage for family and
franchise films** and a reason the economics of an R-rated adult drama and an
animated film are not comparable even at the same P&A.

**Lead times**: partner conversations begin 18–24 months out and lock 9–12
months out. A date move inside that window can collapse a partnership, which
is one of the quiet reasons studios defend dates so stubbornly (§7.2).

### 5.6 How media buying goes wrong

| Failure | What it looks like | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Frequency over reach** | Heavy weight against a narrow, cheap audience | High awareness in a small group; the film opens to its trailer audience only |
| **Front-loading** | Big spend at 8–10 weeks to "build" | Awareness decays; nothing left for the conversion window |
| **Wrong target** | Media bought against the intended audience, not the actual one | Media efficiency collapses; the people who would have gone never see it |
| **No spot layer** | Pure national buy on a film with strong regional skew | Under-delivers exactly where the film would have over-performed |
| **Trailer placement lost** | Failure to secure attachment to comparable titles | Loses the highest-converting channel entirely |
| **Late creative** | Spots not approved in time for the buy | Inventory bought and paid for runs weaker creative, or runs the trailer verbatim |
| **Over-buying outdoor** | An expensive OOH package on a film with no event status | Impressive-looking campaign, no measurable conversion |
| **Uncancellable commitments** | Everything bought upfront for price | No ability to cut after a bad Friday (§1.4) |

## 6. Positioning

The core marketing decision is **who is this film for**, expressed in
**quadrants**:

| Quadrant | Definition |
|---|---|
| Q1 | Male, under 25 |
| Q2 | Male, 25+ |
| Q3 | Female, under 25 |
| Q4 | Female, 25+ |

A "**four-quadrant**" film plays to all of them (animation, big family
adventure). Most films are two-quadrant, and campaigns are built to win the
primary quadrant and not repel the secondary.

Related concepts: the **hook** (what makes someone go), **the promise of the
premise** (the trailer must deliver what the concept implies), **awareness**
(have you heard of it), **interest** (do you want to see it), **first choice**
(is it the film you'll pick this weekend), and **unaided awareness** (can you
name it without prompting).

### 6.1 How a positioning is actually decided

**Who** — the President of Marketing chairs it; Creative Advertising, Research,
Distribution, Publicity and the film's producers are in the room. On a studio
film the director is usually *briefed on* the positioning rather than party to
choosing it (§14).

**When** — the first positioning meeting happens around **9–12 months out**,
after there is a cut worth watching, and it is revisited after the first
trailer test and again after the first tracking read. It has to precede the
creative brief (§4.1), because the brief is the positioning written down.

The process:

1. **Watch the film.** The whole marketing leadership sits down with the
   director's cut or an assembly. The first conversation afterward is
   deliberately naive: *what did you just watch, and who did you think it was
   for?* The gap between what the film thinks it is and what the room saw is
   the positioning problem in miniature.
2. **Build the comp set.** Research produces 8–20 comparable titles with their
   openings, multiples, quadrant splits, CinemaScores and P&A. Comps are the
   industry's substitute for a theory of demand, and choosing them is itself an
   argument: a studio that comps its film to hits has already lost the
   discipline.
3. **Name the primary audience.** Not "everyone" — a specific quadrant, with a
   specific reason to go this weekend rather than in six weeks on a streamer.
4. **Find the hook** (§6.2).
5. **Write the positioning statement.** One paragraph, internal, never seen by
   the public. Typically: *For [audience], [title] is the [genre] that
   [promise], because [reason to believe].* Everything downstream — every
   trailer brief, every art brief, every media target — is tested against this
   sentence.
6. **Concept-test it** — before any creative exists, Research can test the
   *idea*: a written premise plus cast and a poster comp, asked of a panel.
   This is cheap and catches unmarketable positioning before $2M of creative
   is commissioned.
7. **Decide what to hide.** Positioning is as much about suppression as
   emphasis: a downbeat ending, a slow first act, an unknown lead, a genre the
   audience is currently tired of.

### 6.2 The hook, and how it is found

A **hook** is the specific, compressible reason a person chooses to leave the
house. It is not the theme, not the plot, and not the quality of the film.
Useful test: it fits in a sentence a person would say to a friend, unprompted,
and the friend would understand it without follow-up questions.

Hooks come from a small number of places:

| Hook source | Example shape | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| **Premise** | "A man relives the same day forever" | Strongest — self-explaining, works in any medium |
| **Spectacle** | "You have not seen this before" | Strong for event films; needs a single unforgettable image |
| **Star** | "The new [star] movie" | Weakening; works only for a handful of names and only in some genres |
| **Franchise / IP** | "The next one" | Strongest of all commercially, and entirely borrowed |
| **Filmmaker** | "From the director of…" | Real but narrow; moves specialty and genre audiences, not general |
| **Event / cultural moment** | The film everyone is arguing about | Powerful, unbuyable, and the thing publicity actually chases |
| **True story** | "This actually happened" | Reliable for adult audiences; needs a known subject |
| **Fear / thrill** | "The scariest film of the year" | Extremely efficient; horror's hook is a promise of an experience |
| **Emotion** | "Bring tissues" | Works for Q4 and family; hard to convey in 30 seconds |

**How a hook is found when it isn't obvious.** The practical technique is to
watch the film with the question "what is the *one* thing here nobody else has
this year?" and then check whether that thing can be *shown*. A hook that can
only be described in words is much weaker than one that has an image. Marketing
departments will occasionally build a campaign around a scene that lasts 90
seconds in the film, because it is the only sequence that contains a reason to
go.

**When there is no hook**, the honest options are: sell the cast, sell the
reviews (which means platforming and waiting, §7.2), sell it as an event
through scale and outdoor, or reduce the P&A to match the film's real ceiling.
The dishonest option — manufacturing a hook the film does not deliver — is the
single most reliable way to produce a good opening and a catastrophic second
weekend (§12.3).

### 6.3 Positioning a difficult film

Recognisable hard cases and the standard plays:

| Problem | Standard play | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Tonal hybrid** (comedy-drama, horror-comedy) | Pick one and sell it cleanly; let the other be a discovery | Half the audience is surprised; can help or hurt word of mouth |
| **Downbeat ending** | Sell the journey and the performances; never the destination | Punished at exit polling if the trailer implied uplift |
| **Slow, adult, well-reviewed** | Platform release; let critics do the selling; expand on per-screen average (§7.2) | Requires patience and a Q4 release corridor |
| **Unknown lead** | Sell the premise or the director; keep the face out of the art in favour of an image | Loses the "who's in it" shortcut |
| **Unfamiliar world/original sci-fi** | One legible image that states the rules of the world | The most common failure: audiences cannot picture the film |
| **Star playing against type** | Decide early: is the surprise the sell, or the liability? | If the audience wants the old persona, the campaign fights the film |
| **Difficult subject matter** | Reframe from subject to relationship — the film is "about" the two people, not the atrocity | Accusations of soft-pedalling |
| **Bad test scores, locked film** | Narrow the target to the audience that liked it, cut P&A, protect the downside | Guarantees a small opening; the correct decision anyway |
| **Rating problem (R on a would-be four-quadrant)** | Recut for a PG-13, or accept the smaller ceiling | Recutting for rating is a filmmaker fight (`07-postproduction.md` §8) |
| **Long runtime** | Emphasise event status; never mention the runtime | Exhibitors dislike it; fewer showtimes per screen per day |

### 6.4 Positioning problems by genre

Each genre has a characteristic difficulty, and experienced marketers think in
these terms:

- **Comedy** — the trailer must *be funny*, which means spending real jokes. A
  comedy trailer that is not funny is fatal, and a comedy trailer that contains
  the four best jokes leaves the film feeling thin. Comedy also travels worst
  internationally (§15) and is the genre most damaged by the fact that
  audiences now expect to laugh at home for free.
- **Horror** — structurally the cheapest genre to market. A title, an image,
  and a premise will open a horror film with **$10M–$20M** of domestic P&A.
  The failure mode is over-explaining: horror sells a *promise of an
  experience*, and a trailer that shows the creature in full has spent its
  main asset. Horror also undertracks badly (§8.4) because its audience is
  young, decides late, and is under-sampled.
- **Action / four-quadrant spectacle** — needs one image nobody has seen. The
  failure mode is genericism; a competent action trailer is invisible.
- **Adult drama** — has no hook by construction. Sells on cast, reviews,
  awards positioning and time. Requires a platform pattern and a Q4 corridor,
  or it will be crushed.
- **Romantic comedy / romance** — sells the pairing. The chemistry has to be
  visible in the trailer in about 15 seconds; if it isn't there, no amount of
  media buys it.
- **Family / animation** — a four-quadrant sell aimed at two audiences at once:
  the child who wants it and the parent who authorises it. Practically, the
  trailer needs a character a child will name, a joke a parent will laugh at,
  and a song. This genre is also where partnerships (§5.5) live.
- **Prestige biopic** — sells transformation ("you will not recognise them")
  and inevitability of awards. Fails when the subject is unknown to the general
  audience, which is more often than the industry assumes.
- **Franchise sequel** — awareness is free, and the entire task is escalation:
  proving this one is bigger, or different, or the end. The failure mode is
  fatigue, which shows up as high awareness with flat first choice — the most
  ominous tracking pattern that exists (§8.3).
- **Book / game adaptation** — pre-sold awareness and a fanbase that both
  amplifies and polices. The campaign must satisfy the fans in the first asset
  and recruit everyone else in the second.
- **Faith-based and underserved-audience films** — marketed through community,
  church and organisational channels rather than national media, screened
  months ahead to group leaders, and almost always understated by tracking by a
  wide margin.

### 6.5 The skill axis in positioning

1. **Watching the film that exists**, not the film in the script, the budget,
   or the director's description of it.
2. **Naming an audience narrowly enough to buy media against.** "Adults" is not
   a target. "Women 30+ who saw these four films" is.
3. **Finding the hook in the film rather than inventing one outside it.** The
   difference between clever and mis-selling is whether the film pays it off.
4. **Choosing honest comps.** The discipline to comp downward when the evidence
   says so, and to hold that number in front of the chairman.
5. **Holding one idea through 300 assets.** Consistency across trailer, art,
   spots and social is what converts awareness into an impression of what the
   film *is*.
6. **Knowing what to leave out**, and defending the omission when a producer
   insists their favourite scene must be in the trailer.

## 7. Distribution: dating and release strategy

### 7.1 Release date

Set 6–24 months out, and negotiated against the whole industry's calendar.
Considerations:

- **Competition** — nobody wants two comparable tentpoles on one weekend.
  Dates are claimed early and studios play chicken; moving off a date is a
  visible signal of weakness or of respect for a rival.
- **Seasonality** — see `10-theatrical-release-and-box-office.md` §5.
- **Holidays and school terms** — family films need them.
- **Awards calendar** — prestige releases target Q4 (`13-awards…`).
- **Talent availability** for the press tour.
- **Post-production reality** — VFX finish dates.

**Who decides.** The **President of Distribution** owns the date, in a monthly
slate meeting with the President of Marketing, the studio chairman, and the
production executives whose films are being dated. On a co-financed or
IP-partnered title the partner has consultation and sometimes approval. For an
independent, the date is set by the distributor after acquisition, and the
producers usually have no say at all.

**How a date is actually claimed.** A studio announces a date publicly — via
the trades, or by putting it on a slate slide at CinemaCon — often before the
film is cast. The announcement *is* the claim. The trades maintain the release
calendar, and the entire industry reads it weekly. Claiming early is cheap and
buys optionality; the cost is that everyone now knows what you intend, and a
bigger studio may park a tentpole on top of you.

The typical sequence for a big film:

| Stage | Timing | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **Date staked** | 24–36 months out, sometimes before greenlight | An untitled slot: "Untitled [Studio] Event Film, July 3" |
| **Title attached** | 18–24 months | The slot becomes a specific film |
| **Calendar shakeout** | 12–18 months | Rivals move onto or off the weekend; a war of nerves |
| **Date confirmed / defended** | 6–12 months | Partnerships and exhibitor conversations lock to it |
| **Hard lock** | ~4 months | Media upfronts and outdoor bookings make moving genuinely expensive |

**Corridor vocabulary**: films are dated into recognised corridors — the
early-May summer kickoff, the July 4 frame, the pre-Labor Day dumping ground,
the early-October horror corridor, the pre-Thanksgiving family window, the
Christmas Day frame, the January dump months, the President's Day frame, and
the spring break window. Each has known audience availability, known
competitive pressure, and known multiples
(`10-theatrical-release-and-box-office.md` §5). There are only about 30–40
viable wide-release weekends in a year, shared by every distributor, which is
why the calendar behaves like a contested resource rather than a schedule.

### 7.2 Release patterns

| Pattern | Shape | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| **Wide** | 2,000–4,400 locations day one | Studio commercial films |
| **Platform** | 2–10 theatres in NY/LA, expanding weekly on strong per-screen averages | Prestige, awards, specialty |
| **Limited** | A few hundred locations | Niche and documentary |
| **Day-and-date** | Theatrical + home simultaneously | Streamer titles, some genre |
| **Exclusive premium** | Big-format-first (IMAX/PLF) | Event titles |
| **Qualifying run** | One week in LA/NY to satisfy Academy rules | Streaming awards plays |
| **Four-wall** | The distributor rents the screen outright and keeps the gross | Rare; niche and faith-based |

**How a platform expansion is actually run.** The distributor opens in 2–6
theatres in New York and Los Angeles, watches the **per-screen average** (PSA),
and expands weekly on the strength of it. A PSA above roughly **$20K–$30K** in
the opening frame is a strong platform result; $10K is respectable; below $6K
the expansion is usually curtailed. The cadence is typically 4 → 15 → 60 → 250
→ 800+ locations across five weeks, with marketing spend released at each step
only if the previous step held. It is the only release pattern where the P&A is
genuinely **optional and incremental**, which is why specialty distributors
prefer it: the downside is capped. The costs are patience and a Q4 corridor —
platform releases depend on critics and awards attention doing the selling
(`13-awards-and-critical-reception.md` §2), and neither is available in March.

### 7.3 Booking and terms

- The distributor pitches exhibitors (at CinemaCon and directly), who commit
  screens and terms.
- **Film rental terms**: a sliding scale that starts high and declines weekly,
  or an aggregate deal. Net-net, a studio takes roughly **50–55%** of US gross
  and **40–45%** internationally.
- **Screen count is a forecast made visible**: what an exhibitor gives you
  is a direct read of what they think it will do.
- **Holdovers** — a hit is held and can expand; a flop is pulled and its
  screens reassigned within a week or two.
- **Big formats** — IMAX and premium large format screens are a limited,
  contested resource, booked in blocks; they carry a ticket premium and a
  disproportionate share of opening gross for event films.

Detail on how the conversation runs is in §7.6, on the terms themselves in
§7.7, and on how the screen count is arrived at in §7.8.

### 7.4 Windows

The exclusive theatrical period before home release. Historically ~90 days;
now typically **17–45 days** to PVOD, with the length negotiated per title and
often tied to opening performance. Subsequent windows: PVOD/TVOD → EST →
premium SVOD → pay TV → basic SVOD/AVOD → free TV, plus airline, hotel, and
military — see `11-money-accounting-and-participations.md`.

Practical detail worth knowing:

- The window is now often **performance-triggered**: a contract may specify 17
  days *unless* the film exceeds a gross threshold, in which case it extends to
  31 or 45. Exhibitors negotiate for length; distributors for flexibility.
- A **short window is itself a marketing signal**. Announcing a 17-day window
  before release tells the audience they can wait, and depresses the second
  weekend measurably.
- Exhibitors retaliate against very short windows by reducing screens or
  refusing the title outright, which is why day-and-date releases from
  streamers have historically played on a limited circuit footprint.
- **International windows vary by territory** and are set by local distributors
  and local exhibitor agreements, so a film can be on PVOD in one country while
  still in exclusive theatrical in another — a piracy problem the studios
  manage with staggered dating (§15).
- The window's length is one of the few genuinely adversarial issues between
  distributor and exhibitor. Their interests diverge structurally: the
  distributor's money is in week one (§7.7), the exhibitor's is in weeks four
  to eight, and a short window removes exactly the part of the run the theatre
  owns.

### 7.5 Defending, moving, and the calendar war

**Defending a date** means signalling you will not move: locking partnerships,
announcing your trailer attachment, briefing exhibitors, and letting the trades
know your tracking is strong. The credible threat is what makes rivals move.

**Moving off a date** happens for four reasons, in descending order of
frequency:

1. **A bigger film moves in.** The smaller title moves rather than be crushed.
   Studios do this quietly and quickly; the trades report it within a day.
2. **The film isn't ready.** Post overruns, VFX won't finish, a reshoot is
   needed (`07-postproduction.md` §2). This is the most common cause of the
   very late move.
3. **The film isn't working.** A bad test screening, a disastrous internal
   screening, or a soft trailer test. The date move is dressed as a scheduling
   decision, and the industry reads it correctly anyway.
4. **External events** — a competitor's collapse creating an opening, an
   industry-wide shutdown, a scandal involving talent (§9.5), or a real-world
   event making the subject matter untenable.

**What a move costs**: staff time across marketing and distribution;
forfeited or renegotiated media commitments, which get expensive after the
upfront window closes; damaged partnerships (§5.5), which plan on annual
cycles and cannot simply be re-pointed; credibility with exhibitors, who
planned their year around your date; and a public signal. A move *forward*
into a weaker corridor is read as a dump; a move *back* is read as either
trouble or ambition depending on where it lands.

**Counter-programming** is the deliberate play against a crowded weekend:
opening a Q4-skewing adult drama or a horror film opposite a Q1 tentpole,
taking the audience the tentpole does not serve. It is the single most reliable
dating strategy for a non-tentpole. The rule of thumb is that two films only
truly compete when they share a **primary quadrant and a primary hook**; a
horror film and a family animation on the same weekend barely notice each
other.

**Chicken, and how it is played.** When two comparable titles sit on the same
date 12 months out, the mechanism is public and slow: each side briefs the
trades on its confidence, locks partnerships to demonstrate commitment, and
waits. Whoever has more sunk cost, a bigger comp history, or the more
inflexible post schedule usually wins. Losing this game gracefully — moving
early, to a good corridor — is worth far more than losing it late.

### 7.6 How a booking conversation actually runs

**Who** — on the distributor side, the **President of Distribution**, an
**EVP/SVP of Sales**, and **division or branch managers** covering regions. On
the exhibitor side, a **film buyer** at each circuit — a small number of people
who book thousands of screens between them — plus independent theatre owners
booked individually or through booking agents.

**When** — the relationship is continuous, but the specific conversation for a
title runs from **6 months out** (indicative) to **3–6 weeks out** (firm
commitments) to **the week of** (final screen and showtime allocation).

The shape of it:

1. **The pitch.** The distributor presents the film — footage, campaign,
   tracking, comps. At **CinemaCon** (the annual exhibitor convention in Las
   Vegas, April) each studio presents its whole year in a two-hour show with
   talent on stage. This is a genuinely load-bearing sales event: it is where
   exhibitors form the impression that sets screen counts nine months later.
2. **The ask.** The distributor asks for a location count and a minimum
   engagement — e.g. "3,900 locations, two screens in your top 400, a
   three-week holdover minimum, and the large-format screen for two weeks."
3. **The counter.** The buyer responds with what they will actually give,
   based on their own read: comps in their circuit, the competitive slate that
   weekend, and how the studio's last three films performed for them.
4. **Terms.** Rental percentage, holdover requirements and any guarantees are
   negotiated per title, typically per circuit nationally rather than per
   theatre (§7.7).
5. **The slate leverage.** This is the part outsiders miss. Booking is not
   film-by-film. A major studio negotiates across its **whole year**: taking a
   studio's small February horror film is implicitly the price of good
   treatment on its December tentpole. Exhibitors, in turn, have their own
   leverage — a circuit that feels badly treated can quietly under-screen a
   title. Neither side puts any of this in writing.
6. **Final allocation.** In the last week, the exhibitor decides not just
   locations but **screens and showtimes per location**, which is where actual
   capacity is set.

**Locations vs screens vs showtimes.** The reported number is *locations*
(theatres). Inside a multiplex, a film may be on one screen or five, and may
have four showtimes a day or eighteen. **Showtime count is the real supply
number**, and it is adjusted daily during the run — a film that over-performs
on Friday gains showtimes on Saturday, and one that doesn't loses them by
Sunday.

**What separates a good distribution executive**: an accurate opening
projection that they will state before the fact and be held to; relationships
with buyers that survive a bad year; the judgement to ask for the right screen
count rather than the biggest one (an over-booked film posts a humiliating
per-screen average and loses its holdover); and the nerve to hold a date when
the tracking is soft and the film is actually good.

### 7.7 Rental terms in detail

Three deal structures are in common use, all negotiated per title:

**1. The sliding scale (declining weekly percentage).** The classic domestic
structure. Directional shape for a studio wide release:

| Week | Distributor's share of gross |
|---|---|
| 1 | 60–70% (tentpoles at the top of the range, occasionally higher) |
| 2 | 55–60% |
| 3 | 50% |
| 4 | 45% |
| 5 | 40% |
| 6+ | 30–35% |

The economics of this are the reason **openings matter most to studios and legs
matter most to exhibitors**: the distributor takes most of week one and the
theatre keeps most of week six. It is also why exhibitors fight for longer
windows (§7.4) — their money is in the back half of a run the distributor has
already been paid for.

**2. 90/10 over the house nut.** The older structure, still used on some
engagements. The theatre first retains an agreed weekly **house allowance**
(the "nut" — its operating cost for that screen), and the distributor takes
**90%** of everything above it, subject to a declining **floor** (e.g.
70/60/50/45%) so that the distributor's share never falls below the floor
percentage of gross regardless of the nut. The distributor's take is the
*greater* of the 90/10 calculation and the floor.

**3. The aggregate deal.** A single flat percentage across the whole run, e.g.
**53–55%** for a major studio title. Simpler, increasingly common, and it
shifts the risk of a short run from the exhibitor toward the distributor.

**Other terms that get negotiated:**

- **Holdover requirement** — a minimum number of weeks the exhibitor must play
  the film before pulling it. Standard for tentpoles; resented, and one of the
  perennial exhibitor grievances.
- **Minimum guarantee** — rare in the US, common internationally and in
  four-walling.
- **Screen commitment** — number of locations and often number of screens per
  location in specified tiers of theatre.
- **Large-format allocation** — IMAX and PLF screens are booked in blocks,
  months ahead, and are genuinely scarce: on the order of **400 IMAX screens**
  and roughly **1,000 other PLF screens** domestically. A tentpole may take
  every one of them for two to three weeks, which forces every other event
  title out of that corridor. IMAX also takes its own share of the gross on
  top of the exhibitor's.
- **Virtual print fee / delivery** — largely wound down; historically the
  mechanism by which distributors funded exhibitors' digital conversion.
- **Settlements** — in practice the final number is often *settled* after the
  run rather than applied mechanically, particularly for independents. A
  distributor whose film underperformed may take less than contract to protect
  the relationship, and this discretion is a real feature of the system.

Net of all this, the **distributor's share of US gross lands around 50–55%**
across a typical run, and **40–45% internationally**, where exhibitor splits
favour theatres more and local taxes intervene.

### 7.8 How screen counts are actually decided

The number is the *output* of five inputs, in roughly this order of weight:

1. **Tracking and comps** (§8). The exhibitor's buyers read the same tracking
   the studio does, and they have their own circuit-level history of how
   comparable films performed in their theatres.
2. **The campaign they can see.** Buyers watch the trailer in their own
   theatres, see the outdoor, and count the TV spots. A visibly under-supported
   film gets fewer screens, which is a self-fulfilling loop and a real reason
   media weight is bought partly for its signalling value (§5.3).
3. **The competitive frame.** A multiplex has a fixed number of screens. Three
   wide releases on one weekend means all three get less than they asked for.
4. **The studio relationship and slate leverage** (§7.6).
5. **Format and runtime.** A three-hour film yields fewer showtimes per screen
   per day, and buyers price that in. A film needing large-format screens is
   competing against every other such film.

Directional bands for domestic wide releases:

| Locations | What it signals |
|---|---|
| 4,000–4,400 | Maximum saturation; a tentpole or a major franchise |
| 3,300–4,000 | A confident studio wide release |
| 2,700–3,300 | A standard wide release; the most common band |
| 2,000–2,700 | Modest wide; genre, mid-budget, or low confidence |
| 1,000–2,000 | A soft wide release, or a targeted play |
| Under 1,000 | Limited, specialty, or an expansion in progress |

The count is **adjusted right up to the week of release**, and the studio's
requested number and the final number are frequently different. A
lower-than-expected screen count is one of the earliest public signals that a
studio has lost confidence in a film — the trades notice, and so does the
market.

## 8. Research: tracking and testing

The studio's research department produces the numbers that everyone in the
industry actually acts on.

- **Tracking** — weekly (then daily) survey data from ~3–5 weeks out measuring
  **unaided awareness**, **total awareness**, **definite interest**, and
  **first choice**, by quadrant. Tracking is leaked to the trades and is how
  the market forms an opening-weekend expectation.
- **Opening weekend projection** — modelled from tracking plus comps plus
  screen count. Studios habitually project *low* publicly, to be beaten.
- **Test screenings** — see `07-postproduction.md` §9.
- **Exit polling** — on opening night: **CinemaScore** (a letter grade A+ to F
  from opening-night audiences, an excellent predictor of legs) and
  **PostTrak** (demographics and recommend scores).
- **Rotten Tomatoes / Metacritic** — aggregate critical scores that now
  function as marketing assets or liabilities; the "certified fresh" badge is
  used in advertising.

### 8.1 How tracking is actually conducted

**Who** — an outside research vendor conducts the fieldwork and sells the
service to every major studio simultaneously; the studio's own **Research**
department interprets it. Historically the National Research Group defined the
form; the function is now served by a small number of vendors, with
Screen Engine/ASI and Comscore the ones most often named. The important
structural fact is that **all the studios buy the same tracking**, so everyone
sees everyone else's numbers. Tracking is an industry-wide public utility, not
a private research asset.

**When** — the standard tracking report is issued **weekly on a Wednesday or
Thursday**, beginning about **4 weeks out** for a wide release and **8–12
weeks** for a tentpole. In the final 10 days it goes **daily**, and on the
Wednesday of release week the vendor and each studio issue an
opening-weekend **projection**.

**Method** — an online panel survey, typically **1,000–2,500 respondents per
wave**, weighted to the national moviegoing population and reported by
quadrant, by age break, and sometimes by ethnicity and market. Respondents are
shown a list of films releasing in the next several weeks.

**The questions**, in the order they are asked, because order matters:

| Measure | The question, roughly | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| **Unaided awareness** | "What films opening in the next few months can you name?" | Whether the film is in the conversation at all — the hardest number to move and the best single indicator of cultural presence |
| **Total (aided) awareness** | Shown a list: "Which of these have you heard of?" | Whether the media plan is delivering reach (§5.2) |
| **Definite interest** | "How interested are you in seeing this in a theatre?" — five-point scale, top box reported | Whether the campaign converted awareness into desire |
| **First choice** | "Which one of these would you choose to see first?" | The most predictive single number; it is a *competitive* measure, unlike the others |
| **Unaided intent / "must see"** | Variants by vendor | Intensity of demand |

Directional health for a wide studio release **one week out**, as a rule of
thumb only:

| Measure | Weak | Normal | Strong |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total awareness | <50% | 60–75% | 85%+ |
| Unaided awareness | <5% | 8–15% | 25%+ |
| Definite interest | <15% | 20–30% | 40%+ |
| First choice | <4% | 5–10% | 15%+ |

These are not thresholds for a good film; they are thresholds for a
**campaign** that has done its mechanical job. A film can post all four and
still open badly if the interest is shallow, and can post none of them and open
well if it is a genre title with an under-sampled audience (§8.4).

### 8.2 The other research instruments

- **Concept testing** — before creative exists: a written premise plus cast
  read to a panel. Cheap, early, and used to sanity-check positioning (§6.1).
- **Asset testing** — trailers, spots and key art, against genre norms (§4.4).
- **Test screenings** — the film itself, run by the same department for the
  production side (`07-postproduction.md` §9). Marketing attends and takes an
  entirely different set of notes from the filmmakers: not "does the second act
  work" but "what did they think it was, and would they have gone".
- **Positioning / message testing** — which of six taglines, which of four
  "reasons to see it".
- **Presales data** — from ticketing platforms in the final 10 days. The first
  *behavioural* rather than *stated* data, and treated as far more reliable
  than anything a survey produces.
- **Exit polling** — opening night (§8.3).
- **Social listening** — volume and sentiment of conversation; useful as a
  directional signal, notoriously bad as a predictor of gross because it
  measures the most online tenth of the audience.

### 8.3 How studios actually read tracking

Practitioners read **shape and gaps**, not levels:

1. **The trajectory matters more than the level.** A film at 55% awareness and
   rising 8 points a week is healthier than one at 70% and flat. The final
   week routinely adds 15–25 awareness points on the back of the saturation
   buy, and a film that isn't moving in the last 10 days will not be rescued.
2. **The gap between awareness and interest is the diagnosis.** High awareness
   with low definite interest means the campaign is being seen and is not
   working — a **creative and positioning problem**, and the most expensive one
   to discover late because the media is already bought. Low awareness with
   high interest is a **media problem**, which money can still fix.
3. **First choice is the money number.** It is competitive: it accounts for
   what else is opening. A film can have excellent interest and no first
   choice because a bigger film is sitting on its weekend.
4. **Quadrant shape decides the projection.** A film with 20% definite
   interest concentrated in Q1 will open bigger than one with 20% spread
   evenly, because intensity converts and breadth does not.
5. **Comps supply the multiplier.** The projection is essentially: comparable
   film's opening × ratio of tracking numbers × adjustment for screen count,
   season, and competition. It is a regression on a handful of prior films,
   which is why it fails hardest on genuinely novel titles.
6. **Everyone lies about their own number.** Studios brief the trades
   *conservatively* — a $40M-tracking film is briefed at $30M so the actual
   result is a "beat". Rival studios brief the same film *optimistically* for
   the opposite reason. Reading a published projection therefore requires
   knowing who briefed it.

### 8.4 How tracking misleads

This is the part every experienced marketer knows and every model gets wrong.

| Bias | Mechanism | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Undertracks horror** | Young audience, decides late, under-represented in panels, and the genre's demand is not well captured by "definite interest" | Horror routinely opens 1.5–3× its tracking |
| **Undertracks faith-based and community-marketed films** | Sold through churches and organisations, invisible to national panels | Can open at multiples of tracking |
| **Undertracks films with heavily Black or Latino audiences** | Sample sizes too small at the relevant cell; panels skew | Systematic underestimation |
| **Overtracks franchise fatigue** | Awareness is inherited from the previous film and costs nothing; interest is not | High awareness, flat first choice — the classic pattern before a franchise collapses |
| **Overtracks four-quadrant breadth** | Broad, shallow interest scores well and converts badly | Projection too high for wide-but-tepid titles |
| **Blind to late surges** | Word of mouth from previews and early screenings arrives after the last survey | Sleeper hits are invisible until Thursday night |
| **Blind to intensity** | A five-point scale cannot distinguish "I will be there opening night" from "sure, sounds good" | Fan-driven titles are misread in both directions |
| **Self-referential** | Everyone books, buys, and projects off the same numbers | Tracking partly *causes* the outcome it predicts — a soft-tracking film loses screens and spend and then underperforms |

That last row is the one worth dwelling on. Tracking is not a neutral
observation of demand. Exhibitors set screen counts from it (§7.8), studios set
heavy-up from it (§1.4), and the trades set expectations from it. A film that
tracks badly is *given* a smaller release and a smaller final push, and then
underperforms, confirming the number.

### 8.5 Exit polling, in detail

The first honest data in the whole process, because it comes from people who
actually paid.

- **CinemaScore** — surveys roughly **400–500 opening-night attendees** across
  a couple of dozen theatres in five markets. Produces a single letter grade
  (**A+ to F**), plus demographic splits and a "would you rent/buy it"
  question. The grade is published Friday night and is the best available
  predictor of **legs** (`16-critics-reviews-and-word-of-mouth.md` §5). The
  scale is compressed by self-selection — people who chose to see a film on
  opening night mostly like it — so **B is mediocre, B− is a warning, and
  anything C+ or below is a collapse in progress.**
- **PostTrak** — a larger sample, more granular: demographic breakdown,
  percentage "definite recommend", a five-point rating, what drove attendance
  (star, trailer, franchise, word of mouth), and whether the audience felt the
  film matched its marketing. That last measure is the direct read on
  mis-selling (§12.3).

**What the studio does with it on the night**: a strong grade goes straight
into Saturday's creative — the number, the grade, and audience quotes appear in
new spots within 24 hours. A poor grade is simply never mentioned, and the
campaign pivots to whatever *is* defensible (an opening-weekend figure, a
critic quote, a cast).

### 8.6 What separates a good research executive

1. **Knowing the vendor's biases well enough to correct for them**, and being
   able to say "this will open at twice its tracking" and be right.
2. **Choosing honest comps** — and refusing the ones the room wants.
3. **Reading the open-ends.** The verbatim answers to "what is this movie
   about" contain the campaign's real diagnosis; the scores rarely do.
4. **Delivering bad news early enough to act on it.** Research that arrives
   after the media is committed is expensive commentary.
5. **Resisting the pressure to move a projection.** The number is only
   valuable if it is not negotiable, and every research head is leaned on to
   change it.

## 9. Publicity and press

- **Review embargo** — the date critics may publish. Timing is itself a
  signal; see `16-critics-reviews-and-word-of-mouth.md` §3.1 for how it is
  read. An embargo lifting very late (or no press screenings at all) is universally read as a lack of
  confidence, and is itself a news story.
- **Junket** — a day or two of round-robin interviews with cast and director;
  contractually mandated talent days.
- **Premiere** — an expensive publicity event, not a revenue one.
- **Festivals** — see §10.
- **Awards publicity** is a separate campaign with its own budget and staff
  (`13-awards-and-critical-reception.md`).

### 9.1 Who a publicist is and what they actually do

There are **three distinct publicists** on any film, with different employers
and different loyalties, and confusing them is the standard outsider mistake:

| Publicist | Employed by | Serves | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Studio publicist** (unit or title publicist) | The studio's publicity department | The film | Runs the campaign's earned media: press strategy, screenings, junket, premiere, embargo, crisis |
| **Personal publicist** | The actor or director, on retainer (roughly **$5K–$15K a month per client**) | The client's career | Protects and shapes the client's image; negotiates which outlets, which questions, which cover; will pull their client out of a film's campaign if it serves the client |
| **Unit publicist** | The production, on payroll during the shoot | The film's raw material | On set daily: stills approvals, EPK, set visits, behind-the-scenes, managing what leaks |

The **personal publicist's** independent loyalty is the most consequential
structural fact in film publicity. The studio wants the star on every show
talking about the film; the personal publicist wants a magazine cover, a
prestige profile, and no exposure to a question about the client's private
life. These are negotiated, not commanded, and a bad relationship here can cost
a campaign its most valuable asset.

The day-to-day work of a studio publicist:

- **Pitching and placing** — securing covers, profiles, features, late-night
  bookings, podcast appearances, morning-show segments. A cover is pitched
  6–9 months out; a late-night booking 6–10 weeks out.
- **Screenings** — long-lead press (monthlies, 3–6 months out), national
  press, guild and awards screenings, influencer screenings, "friends and
  family" recruitment for word of mouth.
- **The embargo** — setting it, communicating it, policing it.
- **Materials** — press notes, production notes, the EPK, approved stills,
  b-roll, clips for broadcast.
- **Talent management** — the schedule, the prep, the media training, the
  list of questions the talent will and will not take.
- **Damage control** (§9.5).

### 9.2 How a press tour is built

**When** — the tour occupies the final **3–6 weeks**, and the talent days it
consumes are contractually specified (typically **5–15 days** of promotional
services in a star's deal, with travel and per-diem obligations attached;
`12-talent-labor-and-careers.md` §6). Booking those days is a scheduling
negotiation that begins 6+ months ahead, because the actor is usually shooting
something else.

A typical wide-release tour:

| Stage | Timing | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **Long-lead** | 3–6 months out | Monthly magazines see the film; covers shot; profiles conducted |
| **First look / trailer moment** | 4–6 months | An exclusive with one outlet to launch the trailer |
| **Junket** | 2–4 weeks out | 1–2 days in a hotel. Cast and director sit in suites; outlets rotate through in **4–5 minute** slots, 25–60 outlets a day, filmed against a step-and-repeat. Efficient, gruelling, and the source of nearly all the "interview" content that exists |
| **Press screenings** | 1–3 weeks | Critics see it (`16` §3) |
| **Broadcast / late night** | 1–2 weeks | Talk shows, morning shows, radio tours |
| **Premiere** | 3–10 days out | Red carpet, screening, party |
| **International legs** | Final 3 weeks | Multi-city, often 4–8 cities in 10–14 days |
| **Opening weekend** | Day of | Local market interviews, surprise theatre visits |

**Costs, directional**: a domestic junket runs **$150K–$500K** (hotel suites,
crews, travel, catering, talent glam). A **premiere** runs **$200K–$500K** for
a modest one and **$1M–$3M+** for a tentpole with a party. A **global press
tour** with private travel for a top-line cast is **$1M–$5M**. Talent **glam**
(stylist, hair, makeup, wardrobe) is a real line and is contractually the
studio's cost during promotional services — **$5K–$50K per appearance** for a
major star.

**Why bother**: earned media is the cheapest reach in the campaign. A
late-night appearance delivers national reach at roughly the cost of the
travel, and a magazine cover delivers positioning the studio could not buy at
any price. It is also the only channel where the film's *people* rather than
its *assets* do the selling, which is what moves adult audiences.

### 9.3 The premiere and the screening ladder

Publicity screenings are a ladder, each rung with a purpose:

| Screening | Who | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Internal / friends and family** | Studio, filmmakers | Confidence-building; the first outside temperature read |
| **Long-lead press** | Monthlies, 3–6 months out | Feeds the long-lead cycle |
| **Guild / industry** | Academy and guild members | Awards groundwork (`13`) |
| **Influencer / social** | Online creators, 2–4 weeks out | Generates a reaction wave at a chosen moment — the "first reactions" embargo, deliberately set earlier than the critical embargo to seed positive sentiment |
| **Critics' screenings** | Reviewers, 1–3 weeks out | Reviews (`16` §3) |
| **Premiere** | Everyone, 3–10 days out | Photographs, talent, a news moment |
| **Promotional / word-of-mouth** | Recruited public audiences, 1–2 weeks out | Building advance word of mouth in target markets; used heavily on films expected to play better than they track |

The **"first reactions" screening** deserves calling out because it is a
modern invention with real mechanics: a social embargo lifts days before the
review embargo, producing a wave of short, enthusiastic posts that dominate the
conversation before any considered criticism exists. It works, it is
transparently manipulative, and audiences have begun to discount it.

### 9.4 What separates a good publicist

1. **Relationships that survive a bad film.** A publicist places a difficult
   title with an outlet on the strength of years of straight dealing. Burning a
   journalist once for one film costs the next ten.
2. **Knowing what the talent can and can't do.** Matching the actor to the
   format — some are wonderful on a couch and terrible in a profile, and the
   reverse.
3. **Manufacturing a moment.** The single well-placed exclusive, the
   unexpected appearance, the story that makes the film a topic rather than a
   release. This is the difference between coverage and culture.
4. **Setting the embargo correctly** — early enough to help if the film is
   good, late enough to limit damage if it isn't, without choosing a date that
   is itself the story (`16` §3.1).
5. **Speed under pressure.** In a crisis, the first two hours determine the
   next two weeks.
6. **Saying no to the studio.** Refusing to put a fragile actor into a hostile
   interview, and being believed when they say why.

### 9.5 Crisis management

**Who** — the studio's head of publicity and corporate communications, the
talent's personal publicist, the studio's legal department, and — on anything
serious — outside crisis specialists. The studio chairman is briefed
immediately.

**The recognised crisis types**, and the standard playbook for each:

| Crisis | Standard response |
|---|---|
| **Talent scandal before release** | Assess whether it is survivable; reduce the actor's visibility in creative and press; if it is not survivable, remove them from the campaign entirely and, in extreme cases, move or shelve the film |
| **On-set death or injury** | Everything stops. Publicity ceases entirely; statements are legal-cleared; the film's release is usually delayed (`06-principal-photography.md` §8) |
| **Leak of the film or key footage** | Legal takedowns, and an accelerated release of an official asset to reclaim the narrative |
| **Disastrous first reactions / test leak** | Push the embargo later, pivot creative away from quality claims toward premise and spectacle, and manage expectations downward publicly |
| **A cast member goes off-message** | Contain in the room, don't amplify; the worst response is a studio statement that turns a bad interview into a story |
| **Real-world event makes the film untenable** | Pull creative from affected markets, delay, or in rare cases re-edit and re-date |
| **Boycott or organised backlash** | Almost never engage directly; engagement converts a niche campaign into a national story |

The two rules practitioners repeat: **do not feed it** (a response is a second
news cycle) and **get ahead of what you know is coming** (a story you release
yourself is a paragraph; a story someone else breaks is a week). The corollary
is that the studio needs to know what is coming, which is why unit publicists
exist and why they are on set every day.

## 10. Festivals

Two distinct functions:

- **Market** — Cannes (Marché), Berlin (EFM), Toronto, AFM, Sundance: where
  unfinished films are pre-sold and finished films are acquired
  (`03-financing-and-dealmaking.md` §4–5).
- **Launch** — a premiere at Cannes, Venice, Telluride, Toronto, Sundance, or
  Berlin creates critical consensus and awards momentum months before release.
  **Toronto's People's Choice Award** and Venice/Telluride debuts have become
  the standard opening move of an awards campaign.

Risk: a festival premiere is an irreversible, public verdict on an unfinished
commercial proposition. A bad Cannes reception can devalue a film's remaining
territory sales overnight.

### 10.1 How a festival launch is planned

**Who** — the distributor's publicity head plus a specialist **festival
publicist** (an outside firm hired for the festival specifically), the sales
agent if the film is unsold, the producers, and the talent's own
representatives. The festival's own programmers are courted for months.

**When** — the calendar is fixed and the whole strategy is built around it:

| Festival | When | Submission / selection | What it is for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sundance** | Late January | Submissions from August; selections announced late November/December | US independent acquisitions; discovery |
| **Berlin (EFM)** | February | Selections announced ~5–6 weeks ahead | European market; arthouse launch |
| **SXSW** | March | Announced late January | Genre, comedy, audience-first launches |
| **Cannes** | May | Selection announced ~4 weeks ahead, agonisingly | International prestige; the biggest market |
| **Venice** | Late Aug/Sept | Announced late July | The opening move of awards season |
| **Telluride** | Labor Day weekend | Programme announced the day it starts | A quiet, curated awards launch |
| **Toronto (TIFF)** | September | Announced from July | Audience validation; the People's Choice Award |
| **New York (NYFF)** | Sept/Oct | Announced August | Critical prestige |
| **AFM** | Autumn | — | Pure market; no premieres of consequence |

A launch is planned **4–8 months** ahead, and the sequencing decision — which
festival, and whether to stack Venice → Telluride → Toronto — is one of the
most consequential calls an awards-hopeful film makes
(`13-awards-and-critical-reception.md` §3).

**What it costs.** A festival launch is a real P&A line:

| Line | Directional cost |
|---|---|
| Festival publicist (outside firm) | $30K–$100K for the festival |
| Travel and hotels for cast, filmmakers, executives | $100K–$500K |
| Talent glam and wardrobe | $50K–$250K |
| Premiere party / dinner | $50K–$400K |
| Screening and print/DCP costs, subtitling | $10K–$50K |
| Venue, activation, or market suite | $50K–$300K |
| **Total, a studio-scale Cannes or Venice launch** | **$300K–$1.5M** |

For an acquisition title at Sundance the numbers are smaller but the stakes
higher: the entire film's value is decided in the 48 hours after the first
screening.

### 10.2 What a festival launch is actually buying

1. **A critical verdict, early and permanent.** Reviews from a festival are the
   film's first impression and they never expire; they are the reviews that
   will be quoted in the campaign eight months later (`16` §3).
2. **Awards eligibility and positioning.** A film that premieres in September
   is in the conversation from September (`13` §3).
3. **Distribution value.** For an unsold film, the premiere *is* the sale.
4. **Word of mouth with a deadline.** A standing ovation is a measurable,
   reportable event, and festivals manufacture them reliably enough that the
   industry now discounts the length of them.

### 10.3 The risks, concretely

| Risk | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **Bad reviews at the premiere** | Permanent. The film carries them to release; remaining territory sales collapse |
| **Premiering too early** | Eight months of decay between the review moment and the release; the conversation is over before tickets are on sale |
| **Wrong festival** | A broad crowd-pleaser in Cannes Competition gets savaged by a critical audience that wanted something else |
| **Unfinished print** | Screening a cut with temp VFX and temp music to the world's harshest audience |
| **No distributor and no offers** | A public failure to sell, which follows the film |
| **Talent unavailable** | No red carpet, no photographs, no coverage — most of the value forgone |

## 11. Home entertainment and licensing

Post-theatrical is where a large fraction of a film's lifetime revenue is
earned, and virtually all of a library's.

- **TVOD/PVOD** (rental), **EST** (purchase), physical (Blu-ray/4K, declining
  but high-margin for catalogue and collector editions).
- **SVOD licensing** — a fixed licence fee for a window; the biggest single
  post-theatrical cheque for most films.
- **Pay TV and free TV** windows.
- **AVOD/FAST** — ad-supported, valuable for deep library.
- **International TV and airline**.
- **Merchandising and licensing** — negligible for most films and enormous for
  a handful of franchises, where it can dwarf box office entirely.
- **Music/soundtrack, publishing, games, theme park, and stage rights** for
  franchise properties.

### 11.1 Who sells the windows

**Who** — a separate **Home Entertainment** or **Distribution & Content Sales**
division, with its own president. Sub-teams: **digital retail** (managing the
relationships with the transactional platforms), **content sales / licensing**
(negotiating SVOD, pay TV and free TV deals, usually as multi-year **output
deals** covering a whole slate rather than title by title), **physical**
(retail accounts, packaging, bonus features), **international sales**, and
**non-theatrical** (airline, hotel, military, education, ships).

**When** — the windows are planned before theatrical release and executed on a
fixed clock from the theatrical date. Output deals are negotiated years ahead
and cover films not yet made, which means the licence value of an individual
film is often set by a formula agreed long before anyone saw it.

### 11.2 The windows, how each is priced

Directional, US, and in constant flux:

| Window | Timing from theatrical | Consumer price | How the studio is paid |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PVOD (premium rental)** | 17–45 days | $19.99–$24.99 rental | Revenue share; the platform typically keeps **20–30%** |
| **TVOD (standard rental)** | ~60–90 days, or immediately for smaller titles | $5.99–$6.99 | Same revenue share |
| **EST (digital purchase)** | Often 2 weeks before rental ("digital HD first") | $19.99–$24.99, falling to $9.99–$14.99 over time | Revenue share |
| **Physical (4K/Blu-ray/DVD)** | 60–120 days | $14.99–$34.99 | Wholesale to retail, with returns risk; high margin on catalogue, declining volume |
| **Pay-1 SVOD** | 4–9 months | — | A **licence fee**, historically benchmarked at roughly **5–12% of domestic box office** for a studio title, but increasingly set by an output deal formula or an internal transfer price if the studio owns the streamer |
| **Pay-2 / second SVOD** | 2.5–4 years | — | A smaller flat fee |
| **Free / basic TV** | 4–6 years | — | Flat fee per run or per window |
| **AVOD / FAST** | Deep catalogue | — | Revenue share on advertising; small per title, meaningful per library |
| **Non-theatrical (airline, hotel, military)** | Often day-and-date with home | — | Flat fees, **$5K–$75K** per licence per period |

Three things that are widely misunderstood:

- **PVOD's economics are excellent per transaction and thin in volume.** A
  $19.99 rental nets the studio roughly $14–$16 with no exhibitor split, which
  is why studios pushed windows shorter — but the number of households willing
  to pay $19.99 is far smaller than the number who will pay $12 for a cinema
  seat.
- **The pay-1 window is the single biggest post-theatrical cheque** for a
  typical studio film, and it is often not a market price at all. When a studio
  owns the streamer, the "licence fee" is an internal transfer price set by
  policy, which has substantial consequences for participations
  (`11-money-accounting-and-participations.md` §2).
- **Physical media is not dead where it matters.** Volume has collapsed, but
  margins on collector editions and catalogue remain good, and physical is the
  only window in which the consumer's payment is not conditional on a platform
  continuing to carry the film.

### 11.3 How home entertainment is marketed

A second, much smaller campaign with its own budget — typically **5–15% of the
theatrical P&A** — and its own calendar:

- **Announcement** of the digital and physical dates, timed to catch the
  theatrical run's tail.
- **Re-cut creative** aimed at the people who did *not* go: the emphasis moves
  from event and urgency to convenience, quality claims, reviews and awards.
- **Bonus features** — commentary, deleted scenes, making-of. Produced from the
  EPK material shot by the unit publicist, which is the reason that job exists.
- **Retail and platform placement** — the merchandising equivalent of screen
  count: front-page placement on a transactional platform is negotiated and is
  worth a large multiple of the film's organic traffic.
- **Awards-season re-release** of catalogue creative when a film is nominated.

### 11.4 Where the money actually comes from over a film's life

Directional lifetime revenue mix for a mid-budget studio film, with the caveat
that the spread is enormous and franchise titles look nothing like this:

| Source | Share of lifetime revenue |
|---|---|
| Domestic theatrical rentals | 20–30% |
| International theatrical rentals | 15–30% |
| PVOD/TVOD/EST | 10–20% |
| Pay-1 SVOD licence | 15–30% |
| Physical | 2–8% |
| Later TV windows, AVOD, non-theatrical | 5–15% |
| Merchandising and other rights | 0–2% (and 50%+ for a handful of franchises) |

The point of the table is the one every studio executive already knows:
**theatrical is under half of the money and all of the marketing cost.** The
theatrical release is, among other things, an expensive advertisement for every
window that follows it, and a film's home-entertainment value is set in large
part by how visible its theatrical release was.

## 12. What makes a campaign work — and fail

### 12.1 What good looks like

1. **A hook that survives compression.** The premise lands in one sentence and
   one image. Films that need a paragraph are structurally hard to market, and
   this is knowable at greenlight.
2. **Honest positioning.** The campaign promises what the film delivers. This
   matters more than how exciting the campaign is, because mis-sold films get
   punished in week two.
3. **The right primary quadrant, won properly.** A campaign that tries to be
   for everyone usually reaches no one with enough intensity to convert.
4. **Escalation.** Awareness early, comprehension in the middle, urgency at the
   end — in that order, with the money weighted to the end.
5. **Talent who show up.** A star doing the full press tour is worth an
   enormous amount of earned media, and refusal is visible in the numbers.
6. **Distinctive creative.** Key art and trailers that don't look like the six
   other films opening that month.
7. **A date the film can win.** Counter-programming a crowded weekend beats
   competing head-on for the same quadrant.

### 12.2 How campaigns fail

| Failure | What it looks like | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Mis-selling** | Trailer promises a comedy; film is a drama | Good opening, catastrophic drop, bad CinemaScore |
| **No hook** | Campaign can't say what the film is | Awareness without interest; tracking shows "aware, not interested" |
| **Too broad** | Trying to win all four quadrants with one asset | Diffuse messaging, nobody's first choice |
| **Under-spend** | Insufficient saturation in the final three weeks | The film opens invisibly regardless of quality |
| **Over-spend on a small film** | P&A exceeds any realistic gross | Profitable film turned into a loss |
| **Wrong date** | Opposite a bigger film for the same audience | Screens and attention both lost |
| **Late assets** | Post overruns; trailer and art arrive too late to build | The campaign never reaches full awareness |
| **Talent unavailable or hostile** | No press tour, or a bad-news tour | Earned media replaced by paid, at multiples of the cost |
| **Reviews mishandled** | Late embargo, no press screenings | Read as no confidence; becomes its own negative story |
| **Spoiling the film** | The trailer gives away the twist or the best joke | Reduces the reason to attend and damages word of mouth |

### 12.3 The hard limit

Worth stating plainly because it constrains any honest model:

- Marketing reliably buys **opening weekend**. It buys awareness and first
  choice, and a big enough campaign can open almost anything.
- Marketing does **not** buy **legs**. After the first weekend, word of mouth
  dominates, and the **multiple** (`10-theatrical-release-and-box-office.md`
  §4) is set by whether the film is good and whether it delivered the promise
  of its own campaign.
- A campaign that *mis-sells* is worse than a small one: it delivers an
  audience that dislikes what it gets, producing a bad CinemaScore and a
  collapse in week two.
- Marketing cannot fix a **release date problem**, a **rating problem**, or a
  film nobody can describe. Those are greenlight-stage decisions
  (`02-development.md` §8).

### 12.4 The early warning signs, in order of when they arrive

A campaign in trouble announces itself well before the opening. What
practitioners watch for, and roughly when:

| Signal | When it arrives | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **The positioning meeting can't agree what the film is** | 9–12 months out | The most fixable moment, and the most often ignored |
| **Every spec trailer is bad** | 5–7 months out | Five good houses failing on the same footage is a film problem, not a creative problem |
| **Comprehension open-ends are vague** | Trailer test, 4–6 months | Audiences cannot say what it is; nothing downstream will fix this |
| **Art testing has no winner** | 4–6 months | Usually a genre-legibility failure |
| **No partner interest** | 9–12 months | The brands' read on family appeal is unsentimental and usually right |
| **Unaided awareness flat despite spend** | 4 weeks out | The creative is not memorable; more money will not make it so |
| **Awareness rising, interest flat** | 3 weeks out | Positioning failure; too late to fix, in time to cut |
| **First choice below comps** | 2 weeks out | A competitive problem — the audience prefers something else that weekend |
| **Presales soft against comps** | 7–10 days out | The first behavioural confirmation; the projection comes down |
| **Exhibitor screen count below the ask** | 2–4 weeks out | The market has independently reached the same conclusion |
| **Thursday previews under-index** | The night before | Confirmation, and the last chance to manage expectations publicly |

## 13. What separates a great campaign from a merely expensive one

Two campaigns with the same P&A, the same release date and comparable films
routinely produce openings that differ by a factor of two. The difference is
craft, and it is specific.

### 13.1 The skill axis

1. **The campaign has one idea, and every asset is that idea.** A great
   campaign can be described in a sentence by someone who has only seen the
   billboard. An expensive one is a collection of well-made assets pointing in
   slightly different directions. Consistency across 300 pieces of creative is
   not a tidiness virtue — it is how repeated exposure compounds into an
   impression instead of accumulating into noise.
2. **It sells the film that exists.** The great campaigns are the ones where
   somebody watched the cut honestly and built the campaign around what was
   actually there, including when that was not what anybody intended to make.
3. **It picks a fight it can win.** The right primary quadrant, the right
   corridor, the right competitive frame. A great campaign is often a great
   *dating* decision wearing a creative costume.
4. **It escalates.** Existence → comprehension → urgency, with the money at
   the end and nothing wasted on awareness the film can't convert (§3).
5. **It creates one moment the audience did not have to be paid to notice.**
   A trailer that gets watched voluntarily, a poster that gets shared, a
   late-night appearance that becomes a clip, a piece of casting news that
   becomes a topic. Earned attention is the multiplier on everything bought.
6. **It leaves something to discover.** The disciplined campaign withholds the
   best 20% of the film. This costs a little opening and buys the multiple,
   which is where the money actually is
   (`10-theatrical-release-and-box-office.md` §4).
7. **It tells the truth about tone.** The most reliable single predictor of a
   good multiple is that the audience got the film they were promised
   (`16-critics-reviews-and-word-of-mouth.md` §5).
8. **It is decided early and defended.** Great campaigns are usually the ones
   where the positioning was settled at 10 months and not relitigated at 6
   weeks. Late reversals produce mixed messages and blown lead times.

### 13.2 What an expensive campaign does instead

| Expensive | Great |
|---|---|
| Buys more impressions | Buys the *right* impressions, then more of them |
| Makes every asset as big as possible | Makes every asset say the same thing |
| Solves a soft trailer test with a heavier media plan | Solves it by recutting the trailer, or repositioning |
| Shows everything, to prove there is a lot | Shows one thing, to create a reason |
| Adds a mandatory element for every stakeholder | Removes everything that isn't the idea |
| Times the money evenly across four months | Weights 65% of it into three weeks |
| Treats research as a verdict | Treats research as a diagnostic |
| Optimises the opening | Optimises the opening *and* the multiple |

### 13.3 The cheap campaign that works

The opposite case is instructive and common: horror, faith-based films,
targeted comedies and event documentaries routinely open well on **a fifth of
a studio campaign's P&A**. The mechanism is always the same — a hook that does
its own work, a narrow audience that is cheap to reach, and a promise the film
plainly keeps. A $12M horror campaign that opens to $25M is a better piece of
marketing than a $70M campaign that opens to $60M, and the industry knows it,
which is why the genre keeps getting made.

The three transferable lessons:

1. **Narrow beats broad when the money is finite.** Intensity in one quadrant
   converts; breadth across four does not.
2. **A promise of an experience beats a description of a story.** "You will be
   scared" and "you will cry" are stronger sells than any plot.
3. **The concept is the campaign.** When the premise is the hook, creative gets
   cheap, testing gets easy, and every dollar of media does more.

## 14. Where marketing and the filmmakers clash

The structural fact from §2 — that nobody who made the film works on the
campaign — produces a predictable, recurring set of fights. They are worth
understanding as *mechanics*, not personality.

### 14.1 Why the conflict is structural

- **Different objects.** The director made a two-hour thing and is protective
  of its experience. Marketing is selling a two-minute thing whose only job is
  to make someone leave the house. Those two artefacts optimise differently,
  and honestly so.
- **Different audiences.** The director imagines the ideal viewer. Marketing is
  addressing the marginal viewer — the person who is not yet going.
- **Different clocks.** Marketing needs finished-looking material months before
  the film is finished (§3.1), so filmmakers are asked to hand over unfinished
  work to be re-cut by strangers.
- **Different information.** Marketing has tracking, testing and comps.
  Filmmakers have the film. Each side thinks the other is arguing from the
  wrong evidence, and each is partly right.

### 14.2 The recurring flashpoints

| Flashpoint | The filmmaker's objection | The marketing answer |
|---|---|---|
| **The trailer reveals too much** | "You've given away the third act" | "Nobody will be there to see the third act if we don't" |
| **Tone flattened** | A tonally complex film sold as a straight genre piece | The marginal viewer buys a genre, not a tone |
| **Wrong protagonist foregrounded** | The trailer's lead isn't the film's | The audience is buying the star, or the character with the hook |
| **Shots not in the film** | Trailer-only footage or VFX | Sometimes the only usable material existed at trailer time |
| **Key art doesn't look like the film** | A composited, retouched image with no relation to the photography | Posters must read at a thumbnail; a beautiful frame usually doesn't |
| **Music** | A pop needle drop over a film with an orchestral score | Trailer music is a genre signal, not a preview of the score |
| **Title change** | Imposed for international, testing, or clarity reasons | Titles that test badly cost real money |
| **Ratings recut** | Cutting for a PG-13 to widen the audience (`07-postproduction.md` §8) | The rating is a ceiling on the gross, decided before marketing gets involved |
| **The billing block and poster credit order** | Perceived slights to cast and crew | Contractual, controlled by Legal, and genuinely non-negotiable |
| **Late-stage recutting of the film itself** | Marketing-driven notes on the film after testing | Rare and resented; usually routed through production, not marketing |
| **Awards positioning** | Category placement, or the decision not to campaign at all | Budget allocation across the slate (`13`) |

### 14.3 What rights filmmakers actually have

Almost always less than they believe, and it is worth being precise:

| Right | Who typically has it |
|---|---|
| **Consultation** on trailer and key art | Most directors on a studio film, and most producers. It means the studio must show it and listen; it does not mean the studio must change it |
| **Approval** of trailer and key art | A small number of directors with the leverage to negotiate it, and some IP rights holders |
| **Likeness / photo approval** | Most stars, contractually, over their own image in paid advertising — often the *only* hard veto in the room (§4.5) |
| **Credit and billing block terms** | Contractual for cast, director, writers, producers, and enforced by the guilds (`12-talent-labor-and-careers.md` §7) |
| **Final cut of the film** | A minority of directors; entirely separate from marketing approvals (`07-postproduction.md` §1) |
| **Approval of the marketing plan or spend** | Essentially nobody, though minimum-release and minimum-P&A commitments appear in some deals (§1.5) |
| **Right to be consulted on the release date** | Rare; producers and financiers more often than directors |

The DGA agreement protects credits, not campaigns. A director's real leverage
over marketing is reputational and relational: a studio that wants the director
back, or wants them promoting the film enthusiastically for three weeks, has a
practical reason to accommodate them that no contract supplies.

### 14.4 How it actually gets resolved

The escalation ladder, in order:

1. **The creative executive absorbs it.** Most notes are handled by the
   creative advertising exec, who takes the director's note, translates the
   part of it that is a real diagnosis, and quietly declines the rest.
2. **Show them a better version.** The most effective technique in the room is
   not argument but an alternative cut. Filmmakers usually object to a specific
   failure, not to the existence of a trailer.
3. **Give them one asset.** A common settlement: the director gets a
   "filmmaker's trailer" or a teaser cut their way, released online, while the
   studio runs its own creative on television. Both sides can point at
   something.
4. **Data, carefully.** Showing a director the comprehension open-ends from a
   trailer test — real people, in their own words, unable to say what the film
   is — ends more arguments than any assertion of expertise. Used badly, it
   reads as bullying.
5. **Trade.** The director concedes the trailer and gets the poster, or gets
   the international campaign, or gets the awards campaign they want.
6. **Escalate to the chairman.** Rare, and it burns capital on both sides. The
   studio almost always wins on marketing questions, because it is the studio's
   money and its department.
7. **It goes public.** The last resort, and mutually destructive: a director
   publicly disowning the campaign generates coverage, hardens the studio, and
   is remembered for years. It occasionally works, in the narrow sense that a
   studio will change an asset to end the story.

**What good looks like on both sides.** A good marketing executive extracts
the filmmaker's genuine insight — nobody knows better which 90 seconds of the
film are its best argument — while resisting the instinct to under-sell that
almost every filmmaker has. A good director gives specific, diagnostic notes
early, understands that the trailer is not the film, and shows up for the
press tour regardless of how the argument went. The films that suffer most are
the ones where the fight consumed the lead time, and the campaign that
eventually ran was late, compromised, and nobody's idea.

## 15. International marketing

Roughly **60–70% of a big film's theatrical gross** is earned outside North
America, and international is not one market.

### 15.1 How it is organised

**Who** — a **President of International Marketing** at the studio, with
**regional hubs** (typically EMEA, Latin America, Asia-Pacific) and **local
offices or distribution partners** in each significant territory. Territories
where the studio does not have an office are handled by an **output partner**
or licensed territory by territory (`03-financing-and-dealmaking.md` §4).

**How the creative flows.** The domestic campaign produces a **master
campaign** — trailer, key art, positioning — which is then **adapted**, not
merely translated:

- **Localisation** — subtitling and dubbing of trailers and spots; local
  voice casting for animation is a marketing decision as much as a creative
  one, since a famous local dubbing voice is a hook in itself.
- **Re-titling** — many titles do not travel. Local titles are chosen by the
  local office and tested locally, and can differ completely from the original.
- **Art adaptation** — different cast emphasis in different territories,
  reflecting who is actually famous where. This is routine and occasionally
  produces embarrassing inconsistencies when the versions are compared online.
- **Bespoke local creative** — larger territories will cut their own trailers
  and TV spots where the domestic positioning does not work locally.

### 15.2 Why the campaign changes by territory

| Factor | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **Star equity differs** | An actor who opens films in the US may be unknown in Korea, and vice versa; the art changes to match |
| **Genre preferences differ** | Comedy travels worst; action, animation and spectacle travel best; horror is strong and cheap almost everywhere |
| **Local release dates differ** | Piracy pressure pushes toward day-and-date globally, but local holidays, school terms, and competing local films push the other way |
| **Local competition** | In several major territories, domestic-language films are the dominant competition, not other Hollywood titles |
| **Censorship and certification** | Cuts, rating differences, and outright bans; a market's certification can require creative changes |
| **Media landscape** | Television's share varies enormously; some markets are messaging-app and social-first |
| **Local exhibition economics** | Splits, screen counts, and window lengths are negotiated locally (§7.7) |

### 15.3 What separates good international marketing

1. **Knowing which territories the film can actually win**, and spending
   there rather than evenly. Most films are big in five or six territories and
   marginal in twenty.
2. **Sequencing the dates** — using a strong early territory to build
   international word of mouth, or opening globally day-and-date to outrun
   piracy and spoilers on a title that depends on surprise.
3. **Letting local offices deviate.** The worst international campaigns are
   the ones where a domestic master is imposed unchanged on markets where the
   positioning does not work.
4. **Casting the local voice track** on animation and family, which is a
   genuine box-office variable.
5. **Getting talent to travel.** International legs of a press tour are
   expensive and disproportionately effective, because a Hollywood star in a
   local market is a national news event in a way they are not at home.

## 16. When a campaign changes course mid-flight

Campaigns are replanned more often than the finished product suggests. The
recognised interventions, with what each costs and when it is still possible:

| Intervention | Latest it is possible | Cost | When it is the right call |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reposition** (new target, new hook) | ~4 months out | Re-brief and re-cut everything; $500K–$2M in creative, plus lost lead time | Trailer testing shows the audience doesn't understand or want the film as sold |
| **Recut the trailer** | ~8 weeks out | $100K–$400K; media already bought will run the new cut | The positioning is right and the execution isn't |
| **Change the key art** | ~10 weeks out (outdoor printing) | $150K–$500K | Art tests badly or the genre isn't legible |
| **Move the date** | ~3 months out cheaply; later, expensively | Media penalties, partnership damage, credibility (§7.5) | A bigger film moves in, the film isn't ready, or the corridor is wrong |
| **Change the pattern** (wide → platform) | ~2 months out | Saves P&A; forfeits the wide-release infrastructure | Reviews are strong and tracking is not — the film needs critics to sell it |
| **Cut the P&A** | Any time, with diminishing recoverability | Saves cash; guarantees a smaller opening | The realistic gross no longer supports the spend (§1.3) |
| **Heavy-up** | Final 3 weeks | Spends the contingency | Tracking is above comps and the film can convert more reach |
| **Change the title** | ~6 months out | Everything with the title on it is remade | Rare, and almost always a testing or international-clearance problem |
| **Add reshoots and re-date** | 6–12 months out | $2M–$30M and a date move | A film problem, not a marketing one (`06-principal-photography.md` §9) |
| **Sell the film** | Any time before release | Recovers some cost, forfeits upside | The studio concludes it cannot market it at any spend |
| **Dump it** | Any time | Minimal P&A, a dead corridor, a short window | The last resort, and unavailable on a film whose P&A is already committed (§1) |

**The governing asymmetry**: every one of these gets more expensive and less
effective the later it happens, and the information that would justify it —
test scores, tracking, reviews — arrives in exactly the reverse order. This is
the permanent condition of film marketing, and it is why so much of the craft
consists of forming an accurate view of a film early, from incomplete material,
and having the nerve to act on it.
