# 13 — Awards & Critical Reception

*The prestige economy.* Awards are a marketing mechanism, a career currency, and
a genuine influence on what gets made — and the season runs on a fixed calendar
that shapes release dating for a third of the year.

> **Domain reference.** Real industry, not this game. See `README.md`.

---

## 1. Why awards matter commercially

Awards are usually discussed as recognition. Commercially they are five
separate mechanisms with different sizes, different beneficiaries, and
different half-lives. Conflating them is the most common analytical error.

### 1.1 Box office lift

The lift is real, highly variable, and almost entirely a function of **how
much room the film still has to expand**.

| Situation | Typical effect of a Best Picture nomination |
|---|---|
| Platform release still on 200–800 screens in January | The largest effect: a wide expansion becomes justifiable, and remaining gross can rise **30–100%+** against its no-nomination path |
| Adult drama already played out to 1,500 screens | Modest — a **10–25%** bump in remaining gross, mostly a slowed decline plus a re-expansion weekend |
| Tentpole or franchise title with a craft nomination | Effectively zero. The audience that buys tickets does not consult the ballot |
| Film already on PVOD/streaming | Lift moves to the digital window: viewing spikes, theatrical does not |

Rules of thumb practitioners use:

- **The nomination is worth more than the win**, because nominations land in
  late January while the film is still bookable, and the ceremony lands in
  March when most of the run is over. The exceptions are films deliberately
  held back to re-expand after the ceremony.
- **Best Picture is worth far more than any other category.** An acting
  nomination is worth a fraction of it; craft nominations are worth close to
  nothing at the box office and a great deal in the crafts' own labour market
  (§11).
- **The bump is multiplicative on what exists.** A film nobody has heard of
  gets a smaller absolute lift than a film with existing awareness, which is
  why campaigns and P&A are run together and not sequentially
  (`09-marketing-and-distribution.md` §7.2).
- The modern ceremony date (early March, sometimes late February) and the
  compressed theatrical window have **shrunk the post-win bump** substantially
  versus the 1990s, when a March win could add tens of millions to a film still
  playing on 2,000 screens.

### 1.2 Ancillary, library, and licensing value

The most durable effect and the least visible.

- "Academy Award Winner" is a **permanent** metadata tag. It survives every
  window, every format transition, and every catalogue sale.
- Library licensing: awarded titles command higher per-title fees in output and
  catalogue deals, appear in curated tiers on streaming services, and are the
  titles that get restored, re-released, and boxed.
- Physical and premium formats (boutique 4K labels, repertory bookings) are
  disproportionately awarded titles.
- For a specialty distributor whose business *is* a library, an awards record
  is a balance-sheet item, not a marketing one (`11` §5).

### 1.3 Talent quotes and access

- A win moves an actor's **quote** up a bracket — see `12` §2 — but the fee
  change is the smaller half of the effect.
- The larger half is **which scripts arrive first**, which directors take the
  meeting, and whether the actor can now be the reason a film is financed.
- The effect decays. Rules of thumb: an acting win buys roughly **18–36 months**
  of elevated offer flow, and the permanent gain is the title, not the tier.
- For directors, a Best Director nomination changes **control terms** more than
  fees: final cut, casting approval, and the ability to get an unusual project
  greenlit (§11.2).

### 1.4 Filmmaker attraction and corporate prestige

- Specialty labels exist substantially to win awards. The awards record is the
  **recruiting pitch** to filmmakers who could go anywhere, and filmmakers
  choose distributors on the basis of who campaigned well for their peers.
- The parent company's own executives and board value the ceremony
  disproportionately relative to its P&L contribution, which is why awards
  budgets survive cost-cutting rounds that kill better-returning spends.
- Awards records also matter in **acquisitions bidding**: at a festival, a
  seller will take less money from a buyer with a campaigning reputation than
  from a buyer without one (`09-…` §10).

### 1.5 Streaming legitimacy

- Awards were the explicit strategic reason streamers entered theatrical
  distribution at all: the Academy's eligibility rules require a theatrical
  run, and the talent community treats theatrical as a marker of seriousness.
- For a streamer, the awards return is measured in **talent-deal terms** — the
  ability to sign filmmakers and stars who would otherwise refuse — far more
  than in subscriptions attributable to a trophy.

### 1.6 What awards do not do

Worth stating because it constrains any honest model:

- They do not rescue a film with no audience. A Best Picture nominee that
  nobody wants to see still grosses little.
- They do not reliably repay their own campaign. A $15M campaign for a film
  whose remaining theatrical upside is $20M gross (≈$10M rentals) is being
  bought for reasons in §1.3 and §1.4, not §1.1.
- They do not travel evenly internationally. The Oscars move tickets in some
  territories and almost none in others; national academies (§7) matter more
  in their own markets.

## 2. The Academy

**AMPAS** — roughly **10,000+ voting members** organised into ~19 **branches**
(actors, directors, writers, producers, editors, cinematographers, casting
directors, production design, costume, makeup and hairstyling, sound, music,
visual effects, documentary, short films and feature animation, executives,
marketing and public relations, plus Members-at-Large and Associates). The
membership has been deliberately expanded and internationalised since 2016,
which measurably changed outcomes.

### 2.1 How membership actually works

- **Invitation only.** Each summer the Board of Governors issues an invitation
  class — recent classes have run to several hundred names. There is no
  application in the ordinary sense.
- **Route in:** either (a) an Oscar nomination, which makes a person
  automatically eligible for consideration, or (b) **sponsorship by two
  existing members of the branch** you would join, followed by review by that
  branch's membership committee and the Board.
- **You join a branch, not "the Academy".** The branch determines what you
  nominate. A sound mixer cannot nominate directors.
- **Voting status is not permanent.** Since 2016, voting rights run in
  renewable ~10-year terms tied to continued professional activity; three
  terms, or an Oscar nomination, confers lifetime voting status. This
  reclassified a large tail of inactive members out of the electorate.
- **The 2016 expansion mattered.** After the #OscarsSoWhite seasons the Academy
  ran a multi-year initiative that roughly doubled the number of women and
  members from under-represented groups and pushed international membership
  past a fifth of the body, drawn from 80+ countries. The practical
  consequences: a younger, less US-centric, more festival-literate electorate;
  non-English-language films became genuinely winnable; and older
  "industry-veteran-vote" assumptions stopped predicting well.

**Rough branch sizes** (directional — the Academy publishes composition
irregularly):

| Branch | Approx. share of the electorate | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Actors** | ~13–15%, the largest single branch | Films with visible ensemble acting over-perform in Best Picture nominations |
| Producers, Directors, Writers | ~5–6% each | Small relative to their categories' prestige |
| Short Films & Feature Animation, Documentary | ~7–8% combined | Large enough to swing a Best Picture slot toward a well-liked animated or documentary title |
| Executives, Marketing & PR, Members-at-Large | ~12–15% combined | Vote in the final round on everything, nominate in little; the "industry-wide" component |
| Craft branches (sound, design, VFX, editing, camera, music, costume, makeup, casting) | The remainder | Each is decisive in exactly one category and diluted everywhere else |

### 2.2 How nominations actually happen

1. **Distributors submit** titles to the Academy against the eligibility rules
   (§2.4) by a set deadline, with a submitted credit list per category.
2. The Academy publishes a **Reminder List** of all eligible releases and
   category-specific submission lists.
3. **Branch ballots go out** in late December / early January. Each member
   receives a ballot for **their own branch's category or categories**, plus the
   **Best Picture** ballot, which the whole membership votes.
4. Several categories interpose a **committee or shortlist stage** before the
   branch votes: Documentary, International Feature, Makeup and Hairstyling,
   Score, Song, Sound, VFX and the Shorts all shortlist (usually to 10–15
   titles) before nominating five.
5. **Nominations are counted preferentially** — see §2.3 — and announced in
   late January.
6. Final ballots go out in February. In the final round **every member votes
   every category**, whether or not they have the expertise, which is the
   single biggest source of noise in craft outcomes: the sound categories are
   decided largely by people who are not sound professionals, and tend to
   reward *loud* rather than *good*.

A recent tightening worth noting: the Academy has moved toward **requiring
members to have viewed all nominated titles in a category** before voting in
that category in the final round, tracked through the Academy Screening Room.
This is aimed squarely at the "voted for the one I saw" problem.

### 2.3 The preferential ballot, worked

This is the most important structural fact about Best Picture and the most
widely misunderstood.

**Two different preferential counts do two opposite things.**

**(a) Nominations — a quota count.** With 10 Best Picture slots, the threshold
is roughly `total ballots ÷ (slots + 1)`, i.e. about **9%** of first-place
votes. A film reaching the quota is locked in immediately; ballots beyond what
it needed pass down at reduced weight, and the lowest-placed film is eliminated
with its ballots redistributed to each voter's next choice. **A passionate
minority of one voter in eleven can secure a nomination.** The nomination round
is therefore *friendly to polarising films* — this is how a divisive
festival title, a horror film, or a non-English-language film gets in.

**(b) The final round — instant runoff.** Best Picture is **won** by needing
**more than 50%**. Lowest film eliminated, its ballots redistributed to the
next-listed choice, repeat until one film crosses the line. The final round is
*hostile to polarising films*, because a film ranked 9th or 10th on a third of
ballots receives almost no transfers.

A worked illustration (percentages of ballots; ten nominees):

```
                       1st prefs   after eliminations   final round
 A  polarising, adored     24%   ──►      36%      ──►     45%   LOSES
 B  broad, well-liked      19%   ──►      33%      ──►     55%   WINS
 C  crowd-pleaser          16%   ──►      31%      ──► eliminated
 D                         12%              │
 E   9%  F  7%  G  5%              redistributed
 H   4%  I  3%  J  1%                upward
```

Film A leads on first preferences in **every single round** and loses. When C
is eliminated, its 31% splits roughly 22 to B and 9 to A — C's voters find the
well-liked drama an acceptable second choice and the polarising film an
unacceptable one. B wins 55–45 having never led.

The operational consequences campaigns act on:

- The goal is not to be the most loved film. It is to be **nobody's last
  choice**. A campaign's second half is spent making the film feel safe,
  generous, and easy to admire.
- Campaigns openly solicit **"rank us second if not first."** This is legal and
  routine. Telling voters to rank a rival *last* is not (§2.5).
- Films with a passionate base and an active detractor faction — violent,
  formally aggressive, politically abrasive, or three-hours-plus — reliably
  convert nominations into losses.
- **Every other category is plurality**, so the polarising film can and often
  does win Director, Screenplay, or an acting category on the same night it
  loses Best Picture. The much-discussed "split" is not a mystery; it is two
  different counting systems.

### 2.4 Eligibility

The rules are technical, enforced literally, and have been revised repeatedly.

| Rule | Substance |
|---|---|
| **Feature length** | Over 40 minutes |
| **Calendar year** | First theatrical release between 1 January and 31 December of the award year |
| **Qualifying run** | Seven consecutive days, at least three screenings daily, in one of six designated US metropolitan areas — Los Angeles County, New York City, the Bay Area, Chicago, Atlanta, and Dallas–Fort Worth |
| **Expanded theatrical** | Since the mid-2020s, Best Picture contenders additionally need a wider run — a further multi-day engagement across a set number of the top US markets within a window after the qualifying run. This was aimed at streamer "token" releases |
| **Prior exhibition** | A film shown on a non-theatrical medium (streaming, broadcast, home video) **before or on the same day** as its theatrical release is disqualified. Festival screenings do not count as public exhibition |
| **Screening format** | Delivery of a compliant DCP; the film must have played to paying public audiences, not just press |
| **International Feature** | One submission per country, chosen by that country's official selection body; predominantly non-English dialogue; a release window offset from the calendar year (roughly Nov–Oct) |
| **Documentary** | Qualifying runs in both New York and Los Angeles plus a published review in a designated outlet |
| **Score / Song** | Minimum quantity of original music; songs must be written for the film and used in body or first-position credits |

**How the rules have moved:**

- The 2020–21 season suspended the theatrical requirement entirely for
  pandemic reasons, permitting streaming-first eligibility. It was reinstated
  and then *strengthened* — the expanded-markets rule exists because of what
  the suspension revealed.
- Best Picture moved from 5 nominees (to 2008) to 10 (2009–10), to a sliding
  5–10 (2011–2020), and back to a **fixed 10**. More slots means a lower
  nomination quota and more room for outlier titles.
- Physical screener DVDs were phased out in favour of the **Academy Screening
  Room**, a members-only streaming platform. This removed a large campaign cost
  line, removed the "did they even open the envelope" problem, and gave the
  Academy **viewing telemetry** it did not previously have.
- Category structure keeps moving: Casting has been added as a competitive
  award, Stunt Design is scheduled to follow, Sound Editing and Sound Mixing
  were merged, and the International Feature and Animated Feature voting
  franchises have been progressively widened from committees toward the full
  membership.
- The Academy has issued guidance that use of generative tools in a film's
  making **neither helps nor harms** its chances, while asking voters to weigh
  the degree of human authorship.

### 2.5 Campaign regulations and how they are actually enforced

The Academy publishes **Campaign Promotional Regulations** annually. The
substance:

- **No direct contact with voters outside official channels.** The membership
  list is not distributed; unsolicited emails, mailings, and calls to members
  are prohibited. Communications go out through the Academy's own systems.
- **Screenings and Q&As are permitted; hospitality is restricted.** Panels are
  allowed with limits on the number of participants; receptions, dinners, and
  meals for members during the voting windows are heavily curtailed. The
  distinction the rules draw is between *showing the film* and *entertaining
  the voter*.
- **No gifts.** No physical screeners, merchandise, or anything of value.
- **No negative campaigning.** Any communication that casts a competing film,
  performance, or filmmaker in a derogatory light is a violation — including
  when placed through a third party or a friendly journalist.
- **Talent social media is the grey zone.** A distributor can be sanctioned for
  what it does; it cannot control what an unaffiliated celebrity posts. Rounds
  of rule-tightening have followed campaigns that were effectively run through
  personal endorsements.

**Sanctions**, in escalating order: a private warning to the distributor;
forfeiture of the film's ceremony tickets; a ban on individuals attending;
**rescission of a nomination**; disqualification of the film; suspension or
expulsion from the Academy.

**Enforcement is complaint-driven.** The Academy does not police campaigns
proactively — rivals report each other, usually through consultants who know
exactly which line was crossed. Precedents that consultants cite to each other:
a producer barred from the ceremony for emailing voters to disparage a rival
film; an Original Song nomination rescinded after its composer, an Academy
committee member, emailed branch members soliciting votes; and a review of
campaign procedures triggered by an acting nomination driven almost entirely by
unregulated celebrity endorsements. The nomination in that last case stood —
which is the tell. **The rules bite hardest on process violations and most
weakly on the ones that are hardest to attribute.**

## 3. The season calendar

The season is a **14-month machine** with a fixed shape. Everyone in it is
working roughly a year ahead of the ceremony they are working toward.

| Period | Events | Who is doing what |
|---|---|---|
| **Jan–Mar** (prior year) | Sundance, Berlin, SXSW | Acquisitions executives buy next season's contenders; consultants take informal soundings on titles they might chase |
| **Apr–May** | Cannes | Auteur titles launch; a Palme or a competition premiere is a season-long asset. Distributors begin retaining consultants |
| **Jun–Jul** | Quiet | Strategy set. Campaign budgets approved; trade ad inventory reserved; the year's narrative drafted; talent availability blocked out in advance of their next shoot |
| **Aug–Sep** | **Venice**, **Telluride** (Labor Day weekend, line-up announced the morning it opens), **Toronto** (10 days from the Thursday after Labor Day) | The launch pad. Critical consensus forms here; TIFF's People's Choice vote closes the following weekend. Publicists manage first reviews; consultants read the room and re-rank their own slate |
| **Late Sep–Oct** | **New York Film Festival**, **BFI London**, **AFI Fest**, **Hamptons**, **Middleburg** | Platform releases begin. FYC screening series open in LA, NY and London. Guild screening committees start booking |
| **Nov** | **Governors Awards** (honorary Oscars) — the season's first large in-person mixer; wide expansions; Academy Screening Room opens | Talent works the room at the Governors dinner; consultants place first-person filmmaker essays in the trades; screener links go live |
| **Late Nov–Dec** | **National Board of Review**, **New York Film Critics Circle**, **LA Film Critics Association**, **National Society of Film Critics**; **Golden Globe** and **Critics Choice** nominations; December qualifying runs | Critics groups vote in a two-week cluster and set who is "in the conversation." Consultants triage: which of our five contenders is actually live |
| **Early–mid Jan** | **Golden Globes** ceremony; **Academy nomination voting** (a ~5-day window); guild nominations begin | Peak Q&A density. The Globes' televised speech lands *during or just before* nomination voting, which is most of its residual value |
| **Late Jan** | **Oscar nominations announced**; "phase two" begins | Entire campaigns are stood down or doubled overnight. Budgets are re-cut within 48 hours |
| **Feb** | **Nominees luncheon**; **PGA, DGA, SAG, WGA, ACE, ASC, ADG, CDG, BAFTA**; final voting window (~5 days, mid-to-late Feb) | The precursor ceremonies double as voter events. Consultants chase the ~30% of voters who decide in the last week |
| **Late Feb–Mar** | **The Academy Awards** | Ballots tabulated by an external accounting firm; results known to a handful of people |

Consequences that fall out of the calendar:

- **Prestige films release in Q4** so they are fresh in voters' minds, and
  platform-expand through the season on nomination momentum
  (`09-marketing-and-distribution.md` §7.2).
- **December is the crush.** Every contender is screening, every voter is
  invited to everything, and the marginal screening is worth much less than the
  same screening in October. Well-run campaigns front-load.
- **The nomination announcement is a hard fork.** A nominated film's campaign
  budget can triple; a snubbed film's goes to zero the same morning, and the
  planned expansion is cancelled with the bookings already made.
- **The final voting window is short** — five days or so. A late-breaking story,
  good or bad, in that window is worth more than two months of earlier work.

## 4. Precursors and their predictive value

A precursor predicts for one or more of three reasons: **voter overlap** with
the Academy, **ballot mechanism** matching the Academy's, or **timing** (an
award announced before Oscar voting closes does not merely predict — it
influences).

| Award | Voters | Why it predicts | Predictive of |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PGA** (Producers Guild) | ~8,000 producers; top award uses a **preferential ballot** | Mechanism match, not overlap — the Academy producers branch is only a few hundred people. PGA is the only major precursor that counts the way Best Picture does | Best Picture — historically the strongest single predictor, matching roughly two-thirds to three-quarters of the time |
| **DGA** | Feature-qualified members of a ~19,000-member guild | Deep overlap of taste and a long-standing convention that the DGA winner is the profession's choice | Best Director, very strongly — historically ~80%+ |
| **SAG** | ~2,500-member randomly-drawn nominating committee; **full membership** (six figures) votes the wins | Actors are the Academy's largest branch, so acting taste transfers. But SAG's electorate is vastly larger and more television-facing than the Academy's actors branch | Individual acting wins predict strongly (~70%+); **Ensemble** predicts Best Picture only moderately (~1 in 3), because it is an acting prize being read as a picture prize |
| **WGA** | Writers guild members | Overlap is good; the problem is eligibility | Screenplay — but the WGA **excludes films not written under a WGA or affiliated agreement**, which disqualifies many independents and most foreign-financed films. Its apparent "misses" are usually ineligibility, not disagreement |
| **BAFTA** | ~8,000 members, with substantial Academy cross-membership; longlist round plus juries in several categories | Overlap *and* timing — BAFTA's ceremony sits inside the Oscar final voting window | Increasingly aligned with the Oscars, with a durable pro-British tilt |
| **Golden Globes** | Restructured after 2023 into a body of roughly 300 international voters/journalists (previously a body of fewer than 100) | Almost no Academy overlap. Predicts weakly | Weakly predictive. Its value is a **televised speech in early January**, timed against nomination voting, and separate Drama / Musical-or-Comedy categories that double the number of "winners" a campaign can advertise |
| **Critics Choice** | A large body of critics and entertainment journalists | Broad nominee lists, televised | Modest prediction; useful as a January visibility event |
| **Critics' groups** (NBR, NYFCC, LAFCA, NSFC) | Critics | No Academy overlap at all | Predictive of *critical consensus*, not Academy behaviour. Their function is agenda-setting: they decide which films are discussed in December |
| **Craft guilds** (ACE Eddie, ASC, ADG, CDG, MPSE/CAS, VES) | The relevant craft's practitioners | Near-total overlap with the corresponding Academy branch at the **nomination** stage; none at the final stage, where the whole membership votes | Strong for their own nominations, weaker for the wins. The old heuristic that a film cannot win Best Picture without an editing nomination reflects that visible editing correlates with the kind of film that wins |
| **Festival prizes** (Cannes Palme d'Or, Venice Golden Lion, TIFF People's Choice) | Small juries; TIFF is **audience-voted** | Juries are president-driven and idiosyncratic — poor predictors. TIFF's People's Choice is a mass *approval* vote, structurally similar to a preferential ballot | TIFF People's Choice is a notable Best Picture bellwether; the Palme is a weak one; the Golden Lion has become a stronger one as the Academy internationalised |

**Reading the pattern:**

- A film that sweeps **PGA + DGA + SAG Ensemble** is close to a lock. It has
  won the preferential test, the directors, and the largest branch.
- **Splits are where seasons get interesting**, and the usual split is
  PGA/SAG to a consensus film and DGA to a formally ambitious one — which is
  precisely the §2.3 mechanism showing up two weeks early.
- A precursor's predictive power **decays as it becomes a target**. Once
  consultants campaign the guilds explicitly, guild results start measuring
  campaign intensity rather than independent taste.

## 5. Campaigning

An awards campaign is a distinct, budgeted marketing operation, run by
specialist consultants, largely separate from the film's release marketing and
frequently in tension with it.

### 5.1 Who actually runs it

| Role | Owns | Reports to |
|---|---|---|
| **Awards consultant / strategist** (outside firm, or a boutique of 2–15 people) | Strategy, category placement, the narrative, event calendar, guild relationships, competitive intelligence | The distributor's head of awards or head of marketing; often has a direct line to the filmmaker |
| **Head / SVP of Awards** (distributor, in-house) | Budget, slate triage across multiple contenders, sign-off on placement, the relationship with the Academy | President of Marketing (`09-…` §2) |
| **Awards publicist** | Trade and long-lead press, filmmaker profiles, first-person essays, roundtables, the interview calendar | The consultant and the distributor's publicity head |
| **Talent's personal publicist** | The individual client's exposure, protection, and message discipline — *not* the film's interests, when they diverge | The client |
| **Event producer** | Screenings, Q&As, receptions, the venue calendar, catering within the rules | The consultant |
| **Media buyer** | FYC trade pages, outdoor, digital, awards-site takeovers | The distributor's media department |
| **Guild liaisons** | Getting the film in front of PGA/DGA/SAG/WGA/BAFTA screening programmes | The consultant |
| **Researcher / competitive analyst** | Who else is in the category, what the other campaigns are doing, where the votes actually are | The consultant |

The consultant is the pivotal figure and is typically **retained per film or
per slate**, not employed. Top strategists carry two to six contenders a season
and are chosen for relationships as much as ideas — the ability to get a guild
to programme a screening, to get a trade to run a filmmaker profile in the
right week, and to get a star's publicist to say yes.

### 5.2 The two phases

**Phase one — get nominated** (roughly September to mid-January). The target is
the *branch*. A cinematographer campaign is aimed at ~200 people; a Best
Picture campaign at the whole membership but weighted to the branches that
nominate the things that correlate with Best Picture. Spend is dense, targeted,
and heavy on screenings.

**Phase two — win** (late January to the final ballot deadline). The target is
the whole membership, and the arithmetic changes: with plurality categories you
need the most votes; with Best Picture you need to be everyone's acceptable
option (§2.3). Phase two is shorter, louder, more expensive per day, and much
more about narrative than about exposure — by now everyone has seen the film.

The nomination morning is the hinge. Campaigns are re-budgeted within a day: a
Best Picture nomination unlocks the reserve; a snub in the top categories means
the remaining money goes to the one craft nomination that did land, or nowhere.

### 5.3 Building a campaign from the film's assets

A competent consultant starts with an **asset audit** — an unsentimental
inventory of what the film actually has, done before any money is committed.

1. **What is the film's single strongest category?** Every campaign has one
   real horse. Campaigns that claim eight categories with equal conviction
   convince voters of none.
2. **Who will campaign, and how well?** A charismatic, available lead is worth
   more than any other asset. A brilliant but hostile or unavailable one
   caps the campaign's ceiling and should change the budget.
3. **Is there a story about a person?** (§5.5.) Voters vote for people.
4. **What does the film look like in a clip?** Categories are decided partly on
   the two minutes shown at guild events and on the ceremony broadcast. A film
   with no extractable scene is at a structural disadvantage in acting
   categories.
5. **Is the craft visible?** Period design, transformation makeup, a long take,
   a distinctive score — visible work campaigns itself; invisible excellence
   needs a story explaining that it exists (§9.3).
6. **What is the release plan?** A campaign for a film voters cannot see in a
   cinema near them in December is fighting itself.
7. **What is the vulnerability?** Historical inaccuracy, an unhappy subject or
   estate, a cast member's past, a co-writer's credit dispute, an unresolved
   union issue. Whatever it is, a rival will find it. Good campaigns pre-brief
   their own weaknesses to the trades on their own timing.

From the audit comes a **one-page strategy**: primary category, secondary
categories, the narrative, the talent commitment required, the event count, and
the budget in phases with the nomination-morning contingency separated out.

### 5.4 The Q&A circuit — what it actually involves

The core mechanic of phase one, and far more grinding than it looks.

**A single FYC screening**, in outline:

| Step | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Booking** | A screening room (an Academy theatre, a studio lot theatre, a guild's own screening room, or a hired arthouse) in LA, NY, London, and increasingly Atlanta or Austin |
| **Invitation** | Through the Academy's or guild's official channels — direct email to members from a distributor is a rules violation (§2.5) |
| **The screening** | ~90–180 minutes, with a print/DCP check and a house manager |
| **The Q&A** | 20–40 minutes, a moderator (a critic, a fellow filmmaker, or a professional moderator paid $1–5K), and two to six participants from the film |
| **The reception** | Where permitted and within hospitality limits — the actual point, because it is where members meet the filmmaker |
| **Cost** | Roughly **$8K–35K** all-in per event: venue, projection, moderator, catering, security, talent travel and glam |

**Volume.** A serious lead-acting or Best Picture campaign runs **40–100+
appearances** across the season for its principals: FYC screenings, guild
screenings (each guild programmes its own series), festival panels, trade
roundtables, podcast and video interviews, and the precursor ceremonies
themselves. At peak — late November through mid-January — a contender may do
three to five events a week.

**What separates a good Q&A from a bad one:** the participant sounds like they
are answering the question for the first time; they credit collaborators by
name; they tell a story about *making the thing* rather than a story about
themselves; they are funny at least once; and they never appear to want the
prize. Voters are colleagues, and the room can tell the difference between a
filmmaker talking about work and a person campaigning.

### 5.5 How a narrative is actually constructed and pushed

Every campaign has a story, because voters do not vote for a performance
abstracted from a person. The classic frames:

- **The overdue veteran** — decades of great work, never won. The strongest
  frame in the business, because voting for it is a way of feeling good.
- **The transformation** — physical or vocal alteration, ideally documented.
- **The comeback** — a career interruption, followed by this.
- **The first-timer / the discovery** — youth, a debut, a non-professional.
- **The auteur's culmination** — the film a filmmaker has been building toward.
- **The film that speaks to the moment** — riskiest, ages fastest.
- **The craft feat** — "they shot it in nine days," "it's one take," "she built
  it practically." Craft narratives are how below-the-line campaigns work.

**How the narrative is pushed**, in order of value:

1. **First-person and profile journalism.** A well-placed profile in the trades
   or a major magazine puts the story in the voter's hands in the voter's own
   reading habits. Consultants pitch these in September for November
   publication.
2. **The Q&A anecdote.** The story is told, in the person's own words, in
   forty rooms. Repetition is the mechanism.
3. **Roundtables and video series.** The trades' actor/director roundtables are
   effectively campaign infrastructure, and placement in them is negotiated.
4. **Peer endorsement.** Other filmmakers saying it publicly. Far more
   persuasive to voters than a critic saying it.
5. **The precursor speech.** A good acceptance speech at a January or February
   ceremony, broadcast to the electorate, can move a category.
6. **The FYC creative.** Ads carry the narrative rather than argue it — a
   quote, an image, and the category.

A narrative fails when it is **contradicted by the person** (the "overdue
humble veteran" who is visibly campaigning hard), when it is **too naked**, or
when it is **hijacked** — a competing story about the same person that the
campaign cannot control.

### 5.6 Category placement

- The distributor decides which category a performance is submitted in. The
  Academy does not adjudicate lead-versus-supporting by screen time.
- Running a lead as a **supporting** performance to avoid a stronger field is
  standard, openly discussed, and sometimes penalised by voters who resent it.
- Voters retain a check: members may nominate a performance in whichever
  category they think it belongs, and where a performance draws support in
  both, the votes determine where it lands.
- Placement is also used **defensively within a film**: two leads of the same
  gender in the same category split their own votes, so one is often moved.
- Analogous choices exist below the line — which of two eligible songs to
  submit, whether to push Original or Adapted Screenplay when the source status
  is arguable, whether to campaign a film for Best Picture at all rather than
  concentrate on crafts.

### 5.7 Attacking a frontrunner

Negative campaigning is prohibited (§2.5) and happens continuously, because the
prohibition binds *distributors* and *communications*, not the ecosystem.

The recognisable forms:

| Form | How it works | Attributable? |
|---|---|---|
| **The accuracy story** | A journalist runs a piece on what the film got wrong about real events, sourced to the people depicted | Rarely |
| **The authorship story** | Questions about who really wrote it, edited it, or directed it — devastating in a specific category | Rarely |
| **The resurfaced past** | Old interviews, posts, or conduct allegations concerning a contender, surfacing in the voting window | Almost never |
| **The concern piece** | A think-piece framing the film as politically or ethically problematic | No — it is genuine commentary that a rival amplified |
| **Whisper at events** | Consultants and publicists talking to journalists and voters in person | Never |
| **The competing narrative** | Not an attack at all: pushing a frame in which the rival's virtue is a vice ("technically dazzling but cold") | Legal and the most effective |

Defences that work: pre-briefing the weakness early, having the filmmaker
address it directly and once, and getting the affected real-life subjects to
speak for the film. Defences that fail: silence into the final voting window,
and litigating the point publicly, which extends the story's life.

The honest summary: **the rules make attribution the crime**, so campaigns
optimise for unattributability, and the Academy's investigations mostly catch
the clumsy.

### 5.8 What separates a great campaign from a wasteful one

The skill axis for an awards strategist:

1. **Honest triage.** Knowing in October which of the slate's five hopefuls is
   actually live, and killing the other four before the money is spent. This is
   the single largest determinant of return and the hardest thing to do
   politically, because every film has a director who was promised a campaign.
2. **Picking one category and winning it.** Concentrated spend in a winnable
   race beats diffuse spend across eight.
3. **Sequencing.** Front-loading screenings into October and November, when a
   voter's attention is cheap, rather than fighting the December crush.
4. **Knowing where the votes are.** A Best Picture push weighted to the actors
   branch; a design campaign aimed at 300 people, not 10,000. Wasteful
   campaigns buy general-audience media that no voter reads.
5. **Talent management.** Getting the star to do 60 events and be good at the
   60th; protecting them from the one interview that will hurt; knowing when to
   stop.
6. **Narrative discipline.** One story, told consistently, that survives
   contact with a hostile journalist.
7. **Timing the peak.** Peaking in mid-December is a classic loss — the film is
   the frontrunner when nobody is voting and stale when they are. The peak
   should land on the two voting windows.
8. **Relationships that return calls.** With guild programmers, trade editors,
   festival directors, and rival consultants — the last matters because
   intelligence flows both ways.
9. **Knowing what a nomination is worth to *this* film** and refusing to spend
   past it (§1.6).

Wasteful campaigns share a signature: broad media buys, too many categories,
an eight-figure spend on a film with no realistic top-category path, events
scheduled for the filmmaker's ego rather than the voters' convenience, and a
narrative invented in a meeting rather than found in the film.

### 5.9 What it costs

Directional, with very wide spread. All figures are the *awards* line, over and
above the film's P&A (`09-…` §4).

| Line | Typical range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Consultant fees** | $20K–60K/month retainer, 4–8 months, plus success bonuses | A lead strategist plus a small team |
| **FYC trade advertising** | $30K–70K per full page; a serious campaign buys dozens across Variety, THR, Deadline, awards verticals | The most visible spend and among the least efficient |
| **Outdoor (LA)** | $20K–60K/month per major billboard position | Aimed at an electorate that drives the same three corridors |
| **Screenings and Q&As** | $8K–35K each; 30–80 events | The core of phase one |
| **Talent travel, security, glam** | $100K–1M+ per season for a fully campaigning lead | Some borne by the talent (§10.3) |
| **Digital / awards-site takeovers, podcasts, video** | $250K–2M | Growing share |
| **Screening-room platform, mastering, subtitling** | $50K–250K | Falling since physical screeners ended |
| **Events: premieres, festival presence, ceremony parties** | $250K–2M | Partly publicity, partly talent relations |

Totals in practice:

- **Craft-only or single-category indie campaign:** $250K–1M.
- **Serious specialty Best Picture campaign:** **$5M–20M.**
- **Deep-pocketed all-in campaign:** reported figures for the largest streamer
  pushes have run into the tens of millions — plausibly exceeding the film's
  negative cost. Treat published totals as estimates; nobody publishes this
  line honestly.

For context, a **30-second ceremony broadcast spot** costs seven figures, which
is why the ceremony itself is a distributor's advertising venue for *next*
year's slate as much as a celebration of this one's.

### 5.10 How campaigns fail

| Failure | What it looks like | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Campaigning a film that was never live** | Reviews are respectful, not passionate; no branch is enthusiastic | Millions spent for a single craft nomination or none |
| **Spreading across too many categories** | FYC ads listing nine races | No category has enough concentrated support to nominate |
| **Peaking too early** | Frontrunner from October; a "backlash" narrative by January | Voters look for a reason to move on, and find one |
| **Talent won't campaign** | The lead does festivals and nothing else | Measurably fewer nominations; consultants price this in from the start |
| **Talent campaigns badly** | Bored, self-regarding, or visibly hungry in the room | Actively negative; peers vote on how someone behaves |
| **An unmanaged vulnerability** | Accuracy or conduct story breaks in the final voting window | Category loss; sometimes the whole film |
| **Rules violation** | Direct voter contact, a derogatory communication, prohibited hospitality | Warning, forfeited tickets, or a rescinded nomination |
| **Category placement resented** | An obvious lead campaigned as supporting | Voters punish it in the very category the placement was meant to win |
| **Release plan fights the campaign** | Film unavailable in cinemas during voting, or dumped to streaming early | The film feels small; eligibility can even be jeopardised |
| **Consultant conflict on one slate** | Two contenders from the same distributor in the same category | The distributor's own films split the vote; someone gets quietly deprioritised |
| **No narrative** | A very good film about which there is nothing to say | Nominated and forgotten |

## 6. Critics and awards

Full treatment of critics, reviews, aggregate scores, and word of mouth is in
[`16-critics-reviews-and-word-of-mouth.md`](16-critics-reviews-and-word-of-mouth.md).
What matters *for awards* specifically:

- **Critical consensus is the entry ticket.** A film with poor reviews does not
  enter the awards conversation, almost regardless of campaign spend. Reviews
  function as the qualifying round.
- **But consensus doesn't win.** Once inside the conversation, outcomes are
  driven by branch voting, narrative, campaign exposure, and the preferential
  ballot (§2, §5) — not by who reviewed best.
- **Critics' groups vote in December**, ahead of the guilds, and shape which
  films are "in the conversation" without predicting the Academy well (§4).
- **Festival reception is the first and most durable verdict** for awards
  films, arriving months before release (`09-marketing-and-distribution.md`
  §10) and setting the frame the whole campaign then works within.
- **Metacritic tracks awards viability better than Rotten Tomatoes**, because
  the Academy responds to intensity of esteem rather than breadth of mild
  approval.
- **Audience instruments are near-irrelevant to awards.** CinemaScore predicts
  money, not Oscars, and the two diverge sharply — the genres that score best
  with audiences are the ones the Academy systematically overlooks (§8).
- **A hostile critical faction is more damaging than a lukewarm average**,
  because the final Best Picture round punishes films that a bloc actively
  dislikes (§2.3). A film at 78 on Metacritic with no detractors is a better
  Best Picture bet than one at 88 with a vocal opposition.

## 7. The wider awards landscape

Each body is worth something different. The useful question is never "is it
prestigious" but **"who votes, what does the trophy unlock, and where."**

| Body | Electorate | What it is actually worth |
|---|---|---|
| **BAFTA** (UK) | ~8,000 members, heavy Academy overlap; longlists and juries in several categories | Genuine Oscar signal *and* real commercial value in the UK and Europe; a BAFTA is the credential that matters most in British hiring |
| **César** (France), **Goya** (Spain), **David di Donatello** (Italy), **Lola** (Germany), **Ariel** (Mexico), **Japan Academy**, **Filmfare** (India) | National academies of the local industry | Domestic marketing and domestic hiring currency. Little Oscar signal except as a country's likely International Feature submission; strong effect on local subsidy and broadcaster support |
| **International Feature Film** (Oscar) | One submission per country from a national selection committee; shortlisted, then branch-nominated; final vote now open to the wide membership subject to viewing requirements | The single most politically fraught Oscar. A country's committee can eliminate its own best film for domestic reasons, and a film without a submitting country is simply ineligible in the category regardless of merit — it can still compete in the general categories |
| **Documentary Feature** | Shortlist of 15, then branch nomination | A nomination transforms a documentary's licensing value more than any other category transforms its film's, because the doc market is small and awarded titles are the ones that sell |
| **Animated Feature** | Nominated by a mixed group of animation-branch and volunteer members; final vote by the whole membership | Reliably won by whichever major studio release the general membership recognises; the category simultaneously honours and quarantines animation, which is why animated films almost never reach Best Picture |
| **Shorts** (Live Action, Animated, Documentary) | Branch/committee, shortlisted | A calling card. The commercial value is close to zero and the career value is real — a short nomination gets a first feature financed |
| **Independent Spirit Awards** | Members of the sponsoring organisation | Budget-capped (a cap in the low tens of millions, raised periodically), so it is a genuinely different race rather than a preview. Prestige within the independent sector and a real recruiting signal for financiers |
| **Gotham Awards** | Small jury-based | Very early (late November), agenda-setting rather than predictive |
| **Festival juries** — Cannes, Venice, Berlin, Sundance | Small juries (7–9), driven by the jury president's taste; Sundance has jury and audience awards | Confer **critical** prestige and **sales** value, not industry prestige. A Palme d'Or is worth more to a film's distribution deal and a director's next financing than to its Oscar odds |
| **TIFF People's Choice** | Festival audience ballot | The most Oscar-predictive festival prize precisely because it measures broad approval (§4) |
| **Razzies** | An open-membership joke body | Negative publicity; occasionally embraced by a good-humoured recipient, which neutralises it |
| **Academy Honorary / Governors Awards, Thalberg** | Board of Governors | Not competitive. Strategically important as the season's first mixer, and as a way for the Academy to resolve an "overdue" narrative outside the competition |
| **Sci-Tech Awards** | Scientific and Technical Awards Committee | Presented separately; the industry's actual R&D recognition, and meaningful in vendor credibility (`08-vfx-and-specialty.md`) |

## 8. Common misconceptions worth encoding correctly

1. **Awards are not a quality measure**; they are the output of a voting body
   with branch structure, campaign exposure, and narrative preferences.
2. **Best Picture's preferential ballot rewards consensus**, so the most
   admired film and the winning film routinely differ.
3. **Campaign spend matters**, but it buys *visibility*, not votes — an
   unwatched film cannot win, and a widely-watched mediocre film still doesn't.
4. **Release timing is close to a prerequisite.** A film released in March is
   fighting eight months of memory decay.
5. **Genre bias is real and durable**: drama and biography over-perform;
   comedy, horror, action, and animation under-perform outside their own
   categories.
6. **A nomination is worth more commercially than most wins**, because it
   arrives while the film is still in release.
7. **The nomination round and the final round pull in opposite directions.**
   The quota count at nomination stage *helps* polarising films; the runoff at
   the final stage *punishes* them. "How did that get nominated and then lose
   everything?" is a mechanical answer, not a mystery.
8. **"Snubbed" usually means "the branch disagreed with the critics."** Most
   categories are nominated by a few hundred specialists whose criteria are
   professional, not critical — editors nominate editing they can see was hard.
9. **The Academy is not a single electorate.** Nominations come from ~19
   different small electorates; the win comes from one large one. They behave
   nothing alike.
10. **Vote-splitting is mostly a myth in Best Picture** and mostly real
    everywhere else. The preferential count is specifically designed to prevent
    splitting; the plurality categories are not.
11. **Winning does not require winning anything else.** A Best Picture winner
    with no other statue is unusual but not remarkable; the categories are
    counted independently, by different rules, and there is no slate.
12. **Guild awards are not previews of the Oscars; they are separate elections
    with overlapping voters.** Where they predict, it is because of overlap,
    mechanism, or timing (§4) — never because anyone is "signalling."
13. **A campaign cannot make a film an awards film.** It can only make an
    awards film visible (§9).

## 9. What actually makes a film an awards film

Treated honestly: the Academy is an electorate of working professionals, mostly
over 45, voting on films they watched at home in December while doing other
things. What it rewards is legible.

### 9.1 Subject

Consistently over-performing subject matter: an individual's moral struggle;
the artist or performer's life; historical injustice confronted; war,
particularly with a moral rather than kinetic frame; institutional wrongdoing
exposed; illness, grief, and late life; and — reliably and self-servingly —
**the film industry itself**.

Consistently under-performing: genre premises resolved by plot, comedy that is
only funny, action whose meaning is the action, romance without a cost,
horror unless it is legible as being "about" something else, and children's
material outside its own category.

The blunt version practitioners use: **the film has to be about something a
voter can name in one sentence and feel serious about having voted for.**

### 9.2 Performance shape

What gets acting nominations, in rough order of reliability:

1. **A real person**, especially one the voter can compare against footage.
2. **Transformation** — physical alteration, weight, accent, age, illness.
   Visible effort reads as craft.
3. **A clear arc with a scene at the end of it** — the "clip," the two minutes
   that will be shown at every guild event and on the broadcast.
4. **Suffering with dignity.** Note honestly that the historical pull of
   disability, addiction, and mental illness portrayals has become
   contested — the Academy still responds to them and the industry has become
   much more careful about who is cast to play them.
5. **Screen time.** Supporting wins skew to the largest supporting roles.
6. **Being the emotional centre of a film voters liked**, which is why acting
   nominations cluster into Best Picture nominees.

What does not get nominated: restraint that reads as doing nothing, comedy
performances of enormous difficulty, ensemble work with no single showcase,
and performances buried under a visual-effects character (a bias that has only
partly eroded).

### 9.3 Craft legibility

The rule across every craft category: **the Academy rewards work you can see
doing work.**

- **Cinematography** rewards conspicuous formal choice — long takes, natural
  light as a stated feat, extreme scale, black and white.
- **Editing** rewards cutting you notice: montage, parallel action, pace. The
  hardest editorial work — rescuing a broken film, making a two-hander breathe
  — is invisible by definition and never wins.
- **Production design and costume** reward period and volume of build. A
  perfectly judged contemporary apartment loses to a mediocre castle.
- **Sound** rewards loud. **Score** rewards a theme you can hum, or an
  unmistakable sonic identity.
- **Visual effects** reward scale and creature work; the finest invisible
  effects work is, again, unrewarded because nobody knows it happened.
- **Makeup and hair** reward transformation and prosthetics over the
  extraordinary discipline of continuity across a long shoot.

Craft campaigns therefore consist substantially of **explaining that the work
exists**: making-of features, ASC/ADG/CDG panels, technical trade coverage, and
before/after material.

### 9.4 Shape, rating, and running time

- **Length reads as ambition.** Films under 100 minutes are structurally
  disadvantaged in Best Picture; over 150 minutes carries its own risk of the
  unwatched screener.
- **R-rated adult drama over-performs**; PG-13 is neutral; NC-17 is close to
  fatal because it constrains the qualifying release.
- **A director whose authorship is visible but not alienating.** Formally
  radical work gets nominated (§2.3(a)) and rarely wins.
- **Ensembles help**, because the actors branch nominates films it can imagine
  being in.

### 9.5 The honest summary

The Academy rewards **serious subjects, visible effort, and consensus safety**,
made by people its members know or would like to work with. That is not the
same as quality, and it is not corruption either — it is what any professional
electorate voting on its peers' work produces. Films that win are usually good.
The best film of a given year wins perhaps as often as chance would allow.

## 10. The campaign from the talent's side

The campaign is described above as something a distributor runs. From an
actor's or director's chair it is an unpaid four-to-six-month job with real
costs.

### 10.1 Who is around the client

| Role | Function during the season |
|---|---|
| **Personal publicist** | Gatekeeps every request; sets the message; kills the interview that would hurt. Fee typically **$5K–15K/month**, paid by the client, often rising during a campaign |
| **Agent** | Converts the campaign into offers — the point of the exercise — and manages the client's shooting schedule around the season |
| **Manager** | Long-game positioning; often the person who decides whether to campaign at all |
| **Distributor's awards consultant** | Wants maximum availability; is not the client's representative |
| **Stylist and glam team** | Hair, makeup, tailoring, and the fashion-house relationship for 20–40 red carpets |
| **Travel and security** | LA–NY–London shuttling through December and January |

### 10.2 The commitment

- **Four to six months**, September to March, overlapping the client's next
  shoot in most cases.
- **60–100+ appearances** for a live lead contender: FYC and guild Q&As,
  roundtables, podcasts, magazine shoots, festival panels, the precursor
  ceremonies, the nominees luncheon, and the ceremony itself.
- **Peak density** in December and January: three to five commitments a week,
  frequently in two cities.
- **The same twelve questions**, several hundred times. The professional skill
  is sounding like it is the first time on the fortieth occasion.

### 10.3 Who pays

- The **distributor** pays for the campaign itself: events, ads, travel to
  campaign obligations.
- The **talent** typically pays their own **publicist**, and — unless
  negotiated — a meaningful share of **glam and wardrobe**, which for a full
  season of carpets can run **$100K–500K+** for a lead contender. Fashion-house
  ambassadorships exist substantially to absorb this cost, which is why they
  are signed in October.
- Nobody is paid to campaign. Campaign obligations are sometimes written into
  the deal as a specified number of promotional days (`12` §6), but the awards
  circuit is usually goodwill plus self-interest.

### 10.4 What good and bad look like from the talent side

**Good:** turns up; is generous about the director, the crew, and rival
nominees; has one true story and tells it well; is funny; treats craft voters
as colleagues rather than an audience; and never, ever appears to want it.

**Bad:** visible hunger; complaining about the schedule; taking sole credit;
saying something in November that has to be explained in February; going dark
after nominations; or — the most expensive — refusing to campaign at all.
Refusal is a defensible artistic position and it measurably costs nominations;
consultants downgrade a film's prospects the moment they learn the lead won't
tour.

### 10.5 Below the line

The craft version is smaller, cheaper, and more collegiate: guild screening
series, craft-society panels, technical trade interviews, and society awards
dinners. A department head might do **10–25 events** rather than 80. It is also
proportionally more valuable, because the electorate is a few hundred peers who
will hire them (§11.4).

## 11. The career economics of awards

What a nomination or a win is actually worth, by seat. The consistent pattern:
**nominations change access; wins change titles; neither reliably changes
money as much as people assume.**

| Seat | What a nomination changes | What a win changes | Durability |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lead actor** | Offer flow improves immediately; the scripts arrive earlier in the chain | Quote steps up a bracket; the ability to be the reason a film is financed | 18–36 months of elevated flow; the title is permanent |
| **Supporting actor** | Moves from "and" credit to genuine co-lead consideration | Smaller quote effect than the lead equivalent; large effect on the *quality* of offers | 12–24 months |
| **Director** | Final cut, casting approval, and greenlight leverage on an unusual project | The above, plus the ability to attract cast who would otherwise pass | 1–2 projects |
| **Writer** | Meetings, open assignments, and a step up in fee bracket | A durable title that survives dry spells; rewrite rates rise | Long — writing credits age well |
| **Producer** | Financing access; sellers take their calls | The ability to raise a fund or a slate | Long |
| **Craft head** (DP, editor, designer, composer, costume, makeup, sound, VFX supervisor) | Access to a higher tier of project and to directors who hire by name | Day rate rises modestly (**10–30%**); the real gain is choice of project and larger departments/budgets | Long — craft reputations are sticky |
| **Casting director** | Recognition in a field that only recently gained a competitive category | Tier access | Long |
| **Distributor / label** | Recruiting credibility with filmmakers | Library value; internal budget protection | Multi-year |

Qualifications an honest model needs:

- **The average effect is real; the individual effect is unreliable.** Many
  winners see no measurable change in the following two years, particularly
  supporting winners whose narrative was about the film rather than about them.
- **The so-called curse is a selection effect plus a pricing effect.** A
  winner's quote rises, which prices them out of exactly the mid-budget
  material that got them the award; meanwhile the roles that win awards are
  rare and are not written more often just because someone won one.
- **Nominations compound; wins plateau.** Repeated nominations build a
  reputation as a fixture, which is worth more over a career than a single
  statue.
- **Below the line, the award is a hiring signal in a market with no other
  public quality metric**, which is why its effect on craft careers is
  proportionally larger than its effect on stars' fees
  (`12-talent-labor-and-careers.md` §9).
- **For an agency, the client's nomination is a repricing event** and the
  reason agencies fund parts of campaigns informally — car services, glam,
  events — in ways the Academy's regulations do not reach.
