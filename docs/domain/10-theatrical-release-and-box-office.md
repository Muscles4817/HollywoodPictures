# 10 — Theatrical Release & Box Office

*How a film performs, week by week, and why.* Box office is the industry's
public scoreboard and the mechanism by which word of mouth becomes money.

> **Domain reference.** Real industry, not this game. See `README.md`.

---

## 1. The vocabulary

### 1.1 The reported numbers

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Gross** | Total ticket sales at the box office (what the public sees reported) |
| **Rentals / film rental** | The distributor's share of the gross, after the exhibitor's cut |
| **Admissions** | Tickets sold. The honest unit; most of the world reports it, the US reports gross |
| **Domestic** | US + Canada, reported together |
| **International / overseas / foreign** | Everything else |
| **Worldwide (WW)** | Domestic + international |
| **Opening weekend (OW)** | Friday–Sunday of the first weekend (US), plus previews |
| **Previews** | Thursday-evening (and increasingly earlier) shows, now folded into opening weekend |
| **Cume** | Cumulative gross to date |
| **Per-screen average (PSA)** | Gross ÷ locations; the key metric for platform releases |
| **Locations / theatres** | Venues, not screens — a multiplex playing a film on four screens is one location |
| **Screens / showtimes** | The actual capacity given to a film. Locations understate a saturation booking (§15) |
| **Drop / hold** | Week-over-week percentage change |
| **Legs / multiple** | Total domestic gross ÷ opening weekend gross |
| **Internal multiplier** | Three-day weekend ÷ Friday. An early read on word of mouth before week two |
| **Front-loaded** | A film that takes most of its gross early |
| **Break-even** | The gross at which the film covers negative cost + P&A after the exhibitor's share |
| **Index** | A territory's or demographic's share of a film relative to its norm — "Mexico indexed 180 on animation" |

### 1.2 The terms of trade

The unglamorous half of the vocabulary, and the half outsiders never see.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Film rental terms** | The split of gross between distributor and exhibitor, negotiated per title |
| **Sliding scale** | Terms that decline weekly — e.g. 70% to the distributor in week one, 60/50/40/30 thereafter |
| **Aggregate deal** | A single blended percentage across the whole run, now the common form for majors |
| **90/10 over the nut** | Legacy structure: the exhibitor keeps a weekly operating allowance (the **house nut**), the distributor takes 90% of everything above it, subject to a weekly floor |
| **House nut / house allowance** | The cinema's agreed weekly overhead for that auditorium |
| **Settlement** | Post-run renegotiation of terms on a film that under- or over-performed. Historically routine, now less so |
| **Holdover / move-over** | Keeping a film a further week; moving it to a smaller auditorium |
| **Clearance / zone** | Exclusivity for one cinema within a geographic zone. Largely defunct in the US since the Paramount consent decrees were terminated (2020, with a two-year sunset) |
| **Four-wall** | The distributor rents the auditorium outright and keeps 100% of the gross |
| **Break** | The pattern of a release — how many locations, in which markets, on which date |
| **Day-and-date** | Two territories, or theatrical and home, opening simultaneously |
| **PLF** | Premium large format — IMAX, Dolby Cinema, Cinemark XD, ScreenX, 4DX. Higher ticket, limited supply |

## 2. The shape of a run

### 2.1 The arc

A conventional wide release:

```
Week 1  ████████████████████████  100%   (opening weekend)
Week 2  ██████████████            ~55%   (a 45% drop is "normal")
Week 3  ████████                  ~32%
Week 4  █████                     ~20%
Week 5  ███                       ~13%
…then a long tail, typically out of most cinemas by week 8–12
```

Two things about this curve are worth stating explicitly, because everything
downstream follows from them:

1. **It is a decay curve, not a build.** Barring platform releases and rare
   sleepers, a film's biggest week is its first. The commercial question is
   never "when will it peak" but "how slowly does it fall".
2. **The curve is partly self-inflicted.** Screens are reallocated on the
   evidence of the previous weekend, so a soft week removes the capacity that
   would have produced the next one (§7, §13).

### 2.2 The week is Friday-to-Thursday

The industry week runs **Friday through Thursday**, not Monday through Sunday.
New titles open Friday in the US, UK, Ireland, and Australia; Wednesday in
France and Korea; Thursday in Germany. Every booking, every reported "week",
and every hold decision is anchored to that Friday changeover. When someone
says a film is "in its third week", they mean its third Friday.

Weekends carry roughly **60–70%** of a wide release's week in normal
(non-holiday) play, rising toward 75% for family titles and falling toward 55%
for adult dramas, whose audience is older, less schedule-constrained, and
willing to go on a Tuesday.

### 2.3 Previews and the Thursday number

Previews began as midnight shows for fan titles and have crept steadily
earlier: 3pm, 4pm, and 5pm Thursday starts are now standard for event films,
which makes "Thursday previews" a full evening of trading rather than a
midnight novelty. They are folded into the reported Friday and therefore into
opening weekend.

| Film type | Previews as % of opening weekend | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Fan-driven event / franchise | **8–13%** | A pre-sold audience buying on-sale day, competing to see it first |
| Horror | 4–7% | Urgent audience, but younger and less ticketed in advance |
| Broad commercial | 3–5% | — |
| Family / animation | **1–3%** | Children are not out on a Thursday night |
| Adult drama | 1–3% | Audience decides late and does not queue |

A big preview number is read two ways within an hour of landing: as a
**level** (how big is this) and as a **ratio** (how front-loaded is this). A
$40M Thursday against a $120M weekend is a normal event film; a $40M Thursday
against a $95M weekend means the pre-sold audience *is* the audience, and week
two will be brutal.

**Advance ticket sales** are the earliest hard signal of all, and they belong
to the exhibitor, not the studio — the circuits see their own on-sale data in
real time and share it selectively. On-sale for a tentpole opens 4–8 weeks
out, sometimes with a trailer-drop stunt attached.

### 2.4 The days of the week

| Day | Typical share of a 3-day weekend | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Friday** (incl. previews) | 36–42% | Usually the biggest day. Fan and horror titles skew harder |
| **Saturday** | 33–38% | The biggest day for family and for any film with good word of mouth |
| **Sunday** | 22–28% | Softens on holiday weekends where Monday is the fourth day |

The **internal multiplier** — three-day gross ÷ Friday — is the first
reception signal available, roughly 36 hours before CinemaScore has propagated
anywhere:

| Internal multiplier | Reads as |
|---|---|
| **< 2.4** | Front-loaded or actively bad word of mouth; Saturday fell below Friday |
| **2.5–2.8** | Normal wide release |
| **2.9–3.2** | Good word of mouth; Saturday well up on Friday |
| **> 3.3** | Family, holiday, or a genuine sleeper building in real time |

A **Saturday below Friday** on a non-holiday weekend is the single ugliest
intra-weekend signal in the business. It means the people who saw it on Friday
told the people who might have come on Saturday not to bother.

Weekdays have their own texture: Monday is typically **30–40% of Sunday**;
Tuesday usually *beats* Monday in the US because of chain discount-Tuesday
programmes; Wednesday and Thursday sag; and the whole weekday shape inverts
during school holidays, when matinees carry the day.

### 2.5 What actually happens, week by week

| Week | What the audience is doing | Distributor | Exhibitor |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pre-week** | Deciding, if it decides at all | Final media flight; talent tour; advance-sales monitoring; screen count locked | On-sale open; showtime grid built; PLF allocation set |
| **Week 1** | Pre-sold and most-interested segment arrives Thu–Fri; first non-pre-sold audience Sat–Sun | Overnight actuals; Sunday estimate; CinemaScore and PostTrak in hand by Friday midnight; spend re-weighted Saturday morning | Watches occupancy hour by hour; adds shows to a sell-out, cuts them from an empty room, from Saturday morning |
| **Week 2** | The general audience, deciding on what it has heard | The drop determines everything. Holdover negotiations; second-wave creative if the film is playing | Decides who is held, who is moved to a smaller house, whose showtimes are halved |
| **Week 3** | Word of mouth only; marketing largely spent | Expansion for platform titles; a "cross $100M" push for a wide hit; nothing for a miss | New titles arrive; screens are reassigned. First big location losses for underperformers |
| **Week 4–5** | Latecomers, repeat viewers, families on a second visit | Awards positioning begins for Q4 titles; window date being decided against performance | Move-overs to the small auditoriums; matinee-only schedules |
| **Week 6–8** | Residual | PVOD/digital date announced (17–45 days out from open, see `09` §7.4) | Most locations gone; discount and second-run houses pick it up |
| **Week 9+** | Nothing, unless it is a phenomenon or an awards title | Re-release, expansion, or "sing-along"/extended-cut versions on the rare title that sustains | A handful of holds, plus holiday re-bookings |

### 2.6 Holiday weeks break the model

Roughly six weeks a year do not obey any of the above, and comparing them to
normal weeks produces nonsense.

- **Thanksgiving** is reported as a **five-day** (Wed–Sun) as well as a
  three-day. Wednesday plays like a Saturday; Friday is the biggest single day
  of the year for family titles.
- **Christmas week** inverts everything. From roughly 26 December to 1 January
  every weekday plays at or above weekend level, and week-over-week *increases*
  of 10–40% are routine. A film that opens on 20 December can post a positive
  second weekend and still be in trouble; the honest comparison is
  Christmas-to-Christmas across years, not week-to-week.
- **Independence Day, Memorial Day, Labor Day, Presidents' Day** produce
  four-day weekends where Sunday holds instead of falling and Monday plays
  like a Sunday.
- **Easter and school half-terms** float, which is why year-on-year March/April
  comparisons are unreliable and why family titles chase the date rather than
  the month.

### 2.7 Other run shapes

Not every release decays from a peak.

| Pattern | Shape | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| **Platform** | 4–10 locations, enormous PSA, expanding weekly to 100 → 600 → 1,500+ | Success is *rising cume*, not a hold. The metric is PSA at each expansion step, and the danger is expanding faster than the reviews and awards heat can support |
| **Sleeper** | Modest open, drops under 20%, occasionally *up* in week two or three | Almost always word of mouth plus screens being added rather than removed. The rarest and most profitable shape |
| **Event exclusive** | PLF/IMAX-only for a week, then wide | Concentrates a huge PSA and a premium ticket into a scarcity window |
| **Front-loaded fan title** | Vast opening, −65% or worse, gone by week five | Everything was harvested on day one. Perfectly viable if the opening is big enough |
| **Awards re-expansion** | Long tail, then a bump on nomination morning | The one case where late marketing money still moves the number |

## 3. Second-weekend drop as a quality signal

The single most diagnostic number in the business.

| Second-weekend drop | Reads as |
|---|---|
| **< 35%** | Excellent word of mouth; strong legs; family or crowd-pleaser |
| **35–50%** | Normal for a wide commercial release |
| **50–60%** | Front-loaded; typical of horror and fan-driven event films |
| **60–70%** | Poor reception, or extreme front-loading |
| **> 70%** | Collapse — bad word of mouth, or the entire audience arrived on day one |

### 3.1 Why the number carries so much information

The second weekend is the first weekend whose audience did not decide in
advance. Week one is bought: it is the people the campaign converted, plus
whatever fandom already existed. Week two is the general public choosing
between this film and everything else, on the evidence of what people who went
last week said about it.

So the drop measures a **ratio of two different populations** — the pre-sold
against the persuadable — and it is high precisely when the first was large
relative to the second. That is why the number is diagnostic without being
purely a quality signal: a −62% drop can mean "bad film" or "the entire
fanbase showed up at once and there was never a second audience", and telling
those apart requires the CinemaScore and the PostTrak recommend score
(`16-critics-reviews-and-word-of-mouth.md` §7).

### 3.2 The four things that add up to a drop

A second-weekend number is the product of four separate effects, and
practitioners try to separate them before drawing conclusions:

1. **Demand exhaustion.** The pre-sold audience is finite and consumed itself
   in week one. Purely arithmetic; nothing to do with quality.
2. **Word of mouth.** The recommend rate determines whether the persuadable
   audience shows up. This is the part that is about the film.
3. **Capacity loss.** Showtimes are cut on Saturday of week one and locations
   on the following Friday. A film can lose 25–35% of its showtimes going into
   week two even while holding all its locations, which mechanically produces
   a chunk of the drop (§13, §15).
4. **Competition.** A new title aimed at the same quadrant takes both the
   audience and the screens. Two comparable openings a week apart is a dating
   failure with a predictable cost.

### 3.3 Genre baselines and the audience behaviour behind them

Front-loading is not a property of a genre; it is a property of how that
genre's audience makes its decision.

| Genre | Typical 2nd-weekend drop | The behaviour that produces it |
|---|---|---|
| **Family / animation** | **25–40%** | Parents plan around weekends and holidays, cannot always go on the first one, and take a second set of children later. Repeat viewing is normal, not exceptional |
| **Broad comedy** | 40–50% | Social, recommendation-driven, but with no urgency to be first |
| **Adult drama** | 30–45% | Older audience, decides late, avoids opening-weekend crowds, is reachable by reviews for weeks |
| **Franchise / superhero** | 55–68% | A fandom that treats opening weekend as an event and considers spoiler exposure a cost of waiting. The whole core arrives in 72 hours |
| **Horror** | **55–70%** | A young audience that goes on the first weekend for the communal scare, plus a genre where the twist propagates instantly and negative word of mouth is common by design |
| **Sequel to a disliked film** | 60–75% | Brand awareness opens it; the previous instalment's reputation prevents any second wave |

Horror is the instructive case: it is systematically front-loaded *and*
systematically profitable, because the openings are large relative to negative
costs of $5M–$20M. Front-loading is a shape, not a verdict.

### 3.4 Reading drops honestly

- **Compare like weekends.** A second weekend against a four-day holiday first
  weekend is not a drop, it is an artefact. Practitioners compare three-day to
  three-day, and Friday to Friday.
- **The Friday-to-Friday drop is the cleaner number** than weekend-to-weekend,
  because it strips out holiday and internal-multiplier noise. A film whose
  Friday falls 65% but whose weekend falls only 50% is *improving*.
- **Weekday holds matter as much as the weekend.** A film that drops 55% on
  the weekend but holds its Monday–Thursday better than its comps has an older
  audience arriving slowly, and its multiple will beat its drop.
- **Third weekend confirms or refutes the second.** A −50% followed by −30% is
  a film that found its audience; −50% followed by −55% is a film in decline
  that had a soft week-two comparison.

## 4. The multiple (legs)

Total domestic gross ÷ opening weekend.

| Multiple | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| **1.8–2.2×** | Very front-loaded — horror, fan-service sequels, bad word of mouth |
| **2.5–3.0×** | Typical modern wide release |
| **3.0–4.0×** | Good word of mouth |
| **4.0×+** | Genuine phenomenon or a holiday-boosted family title |

### 4.1 What the multiple is made of

The multiple is not a mystical property; it is the sum of every subsequent
week divided by the first. Three inputs move it, and they are separable:

1. **How much audience was left after week one.** A film that opened to its
   entire addressable audience has nothing left to convert. A film that opened
   to a fraction of it has room.
2. **Whether the remaining audience is persuaded to come.** This is word of
   mouth, and it is set by the gap between what the campaign promised and what
   the film delivered (`09-marketing-and-distribution.md` §12.3).
3. **Whether the capacity survives long enough for them to.** Legs require
   screens. A hold-worthy film that loses its locations to a crowded slate
   cannot express its legs; this is a real and common way for a good film to
   post a mediocre multiple.

Add a fourth for Q4: **calendar**. A film sitting in the Christmas corridor
inherits two weeks in which the whole market plays at 150–250% of normal, and
that alone can add 0.5–1.0× to the multiple with no change to the film.

### 4.2 Worked example — three films, one opening weekend

Three wide releases each open to **$40M**. Everything after that is the film.

| | **A — mis-sold thriller** | **B — solid crowd-pleaser** | **C — family sleeper, December** |
|---|---|---|---|
| CinemaScore | C+ | A− | A |
| Wknd 1 | $40.0M | $40.0M | $40.0M |
| Wknd 2 | $13.6M (−66%) | $24.0M (−40%) | $30.0M (−25%) |
| Wknd 3 | $5.4M (−60%) | $15.6M (−35%) | $34.5M (+15%, Christmas week) |
| Wknd 4 | $2.2M | $10.1M | $22.0M |
| Wknd 5+ | $2.5M | $12.0M | $28.0M |
| Weekday gross across the run | ~$14M | ~$46M | ~$95M |
| **Domestic total** | **~$78M** | **~$148M** | **~$250M** |
| **Multiple** | **1.95×** | **3.7×** | **6.2×** |

The same opening — the same marketing spend, roughly the same screen count —
produces a three-fold difference in gross. This is the single most important
structural fact about theatrical: **marketing sets the opening; the film sets
the multiple** (`09` §12.3).

Note also what the weekday line does. Film C's weekdays are worth more than
Film A's entire run, because holiday weekdays and a family audience are the
same thing viewed from two directions.

### 4.3 What produces a genuinely good multiple

In descending order of reliability:

1. **A film that over-delivers on a modest promise.** Satisfaction is measured
   against expectation, not against quality in the abstract.
2. **A reason to recommend that makes the recommender look good** — a twist, a
   performance, an experience that has to be seen in a cinema.
3. **An audience that could not all come at once** — families, older adults,
   anyone whose attendance is schedule-constrained rather than urgent.
4. **Repeat viewing** (§16). A film with 15% repeat attendance has a built-in
   second audience that costs nothing to acquire.
5. **A clear run** — no comparable competitor for three weekends.
6. **A holiday corridor.**
7. **Exhibitor conviction.** A circuit that believes in a hold will keep
   showtimes on a film for an extra fortnight, and legs are impossible without
   that.

### 4.4 The historical trend

The trend is toward lower multiples: wider releases, bigger opening campaigns,
saturation bookings, and shorter windows have concentrated grosses into
opening weekend. A 1970s or 1980s hit could open on a few hundred screens,
expand for weeks, and run for months at a multiple of **5–8×**; some ran a
year. That essentially no longer happens outside family and holiday titles.

The causes are structural, not cultural:

- **Saturation booking.** Opening everywhere at once means there is no
  geographic expansion left to do.
- **National advertising.** The campaign is spent by opening weekend, so there
  is no sustained spend to drive weeks three through eight.
- **Short windows.** An audience that knows the film is home in five weeks has
  a cheap alternative to going now (`09` §7.4).
- **Information speed.** The verdict on a film is universally available by
  Friday midnight. Word of mouth that used to take three weeks to propagate
  now takes three hours, which compresses both good and bad outcomes.

## 5. Seasonality

The domestic calendar has a shape that all dating decisions respect. It is
driven by four underlying forces, and every seasonal effect below is one of
them wearing a costume:

- **School calendars** — who is free on a Tuesday afternoon, and when.
- **Competitive density** — how many films are chasing the same screens.
- **Audience availability and habit** — holidays, weather, cultural routine.
- **Format and screen supply** — PLF and IMAX are contested at the peaks.

| Period | Character |
|---|---|
| **Jan–Feb** | Dump months. Low grosses, awards holdovers expanding, horror and low-expectation titles |
| **Mar–Apr** | Rising; a real corridor for mid-size hits; Easter is strong for family |
| **May–early Aug** | **Summer.** The tentpole corridor. Memorial Day, Independence Day, and every weekend contested |
| **Late Aug–Sep** | Post-summer trough; genre and dumped titles |
| **Oct** | Horror season (Halloween), plus the start of awards platform releases |
| **Nov** | Thanksgiving — a huge family corridor; tentpoles and prestige expansions |
| **Dec** | **Christmas/New Year.** The highest-grossing weeks of the year; long runs, family and event films, awards qualifying |

### 5.1 Period by period, and what actually drives it

**January–February.** Post-holiday spending fatigue, bad weather in the north
(which helps attendance in some markets and destroys it in others through
closures), and no school holiday until Presidents' Day. The market is *soft*,
which means it is also *cheap*: competition is thin, screens are available,
and a horror title or a broad comedy can open above its weight because it is
the only new thing. Awards contenders in wide expansion do a large share of
the business. **What a film gains here**: uncontested screens, low P&A
benchmarks, and a chance at the "surprise hit" narrative. **What it loses**:
the market is 30–40% smaller than summer, so the ceiling is lower, and being
dated here is read by the trades as a lack of confidence — a self-fulfilling
signal that suppresses tracking.

**March–April.** The first real corridor. Spring breaks are staggered across
states through March, so there is a rolling family audience for a month rather
than a single week. Easter moves between late March and late April and drags
the family window with it. Competitive density is moderate: this is where
mid-size films that would be crushed in summer can post a 3× multiple.

**May–early August.** Schools break; the market runs at 150–200% of a January
baseline; and every weekend has a tentpole on it. Two mechanisms matter more
than the raw size: **weekday play** (children are free Monday to Friday, so
weekday grosses run at 45–55% of the week rather than 30–35%), and **screen
scarcity** (a film that opens between two tentpoles gets fewer screens, fewer
PLF sessions, and loses them faster). Summer's danger is that the audience is
large but the share available to any one film is small. Late July and early
August are visibly softer than late May and June as the corridor exhausts
itself.

**Late August–September.** Schools return; the audience contracts sharply; and
the slate is thin because everyone avoided it. The trough is real but not
uniform — September has repeatedly produced enormous horror openings, precisely
because the young audience is back in a social routine and there is nothing
else to see.

**October.** Halloween is the only genre-specific seasonal effect in the
calendar and it is powerful: horror indexes 130–180% of its normal level
through the month. The wrinkle is that Halloween itself is worthless when it
falls midweek — the audience goes to parties, not cinemas — so the money is in
the two weekends *before* it. October is also when awards platform releases
start (`13-awards-and-critical-reception.md`), which competes for arthouse
screens rather than multiplex ones.

**November.** Thanksgiving is the second-biggest corridor of the year: a
five-day weekend, families assembled and looking for something to do together,
and no school for a week. Family and four-quadrant titles index highest.
Prestige films expand into it. The risk is density — three or four wide
releases stacked on the same Wednesday is normal.

**December.** The highest-grossing weeks of the year, and structurally
different from everything else: from Christmas Day to the first weekend of
January the entire market plays at holiday level every day, which multiplies
the value of *legs* rather than of opening. A film that opens in mid-December
with good word of mouth can gross more in its second and third weeks than its
first. Awards-qualifying runs happen here. Screen supply is the tightest of
the year and holdovers are ruthless.

### 5.2 What a film gains or loses by moving

Dates are claimed 12–24 months out and moved constantly. The trade-offs are
well understood:

| Move | Gains | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Into summer or Christmas** | A market 1.5–2.5× larger; PLF availability if booked early; prestige of the slot | Direct competition for the same quadrant; screens shared; P&A must rise to be heard above the noise |
| **Out of a tentpole's weekend** | Screens, showtimes, and an audience that is not being shouted at | Reads publicly as retreat; tracking softens on the news; media buys already placed may be wasted |
| **From spring to autumn (or vice versa)** | Re-positioning for awards or for a genre season | Campaign assets and press already spent; talent availability for the tour has to be re-secured; the delay costs interest on the negative |
| **Later, for post/VFX reasons** | A finished film | Every marketing dollar spent to date decays; the trade narrative becomes "troubled"; the new date is whatever is left |
| **Earlier, to grab an empty corridor** | Uncontested weekend | Compressed campaign; the last three weeks of saturation cannot be shortened without losing the opening (`09` §3) |

**The unglamorous mechanic**: date moves are negotiated socially as well as
commercially. Studios telegraph intentions at CinemaCon and through the
trades, and moving off a rival's date is an acknowledged act of deference that
gets reciprocated later. A studio that repeatedly parks on other people's
dates finds its own dates parked on.

### 5.3 Non-US calendars

Non-US calendars diverge and do not simply shadow the domestic one:

- **China** — Chinese New Year (the single biggest week in the world by
  admissions), the summer "domestic protection" period when imports are
  effectively unbookable, National Day in early October.
- **Japan** — Golden Week (late April to early May), Obon (August), and the
  year-end/New Year corridor. Dating is often months behind domestic.
- **Korea** — Chuseok and the summer peak; extremely fast, front-loaded runs.
- **India** — Diwali, Eid, Pongal and Republic Day, all of which are claimed by
  local star vehicles that a Hollywood title should avoid.
- **Europe** — school holidays vary by *region* within countries (Germany and
  France stagger them deliberately), and Italy and Spain historically go very
  quiet in high summer.
- **Southern hemisphere** — Australia's long summer holiday runs mid-December
  to late January, which makes the Christmas corridor even more valuable there
  than in the US.

## 6. What determines opening weekend

In rough order of predictive power:

1. **Awareness and interest** (tracking), which is bought with P&A.
2. **Pre-sold IP** — a known franchise, sequel, or brand.
3. **Screen count and format footprint** (IMAX/PLF allocation).
4. **Genre** — horror and event action over-index on opening; drama does not.
5. **Star power** — real but weaker than it was; a star reliably raises the
   floor of interest more than the ceiling of gross.
6. **Date and competition**.
7. **Reviews** — matter enormously for adult and prestige films, and barely at
   all for pre-sold franchise openings (they show up in week two instead).

Ranked lists are how the business talks about this, but they hide the
mechanisms. Each factor acts through a specific chain, and the chains behave
differently under pressure.

### 6.1 The mechanisms, one at a time

**Awareness → interest → first choice.** Tracking measures these as three
separate quantities for a reason: they are three separate failures. *Total
awareness* is bought almost mechanically with reach — enough television and
digital in the last three weeks will make almost anyone aware of almost
anything. *Definite interest* is a property of the material, not the spend: it
is whether the premise, once understood, is something a person wants. *First
choice* is interest surviving contact with everything else opening. A film can
be at 90% awareness and 8% first choice, and it will open badly no matter how
much more is spent, because the money is buying a quantity that is already
maximised. The practitioner's diagnosis — "aware but not interested" — names
exactly this.

**Pre-sold IP → decision made in advance.** A franchise does not merely raise
interest; it changes *when* the decision is made. A pre-sold audience decides
months out, buys in advance, and treats the opening weekend as a fixed
appointment. This is why franchise films have huge preview numbers, huge
Fridays, and terrible second weekends: the mechanism that produces the opening
is the same one that exhausts the audience.

**Screens and formats → capacity, not demand.** Screen count does not create
an audience; it removes a constraint on one, and it is a *forecast made
visible* (`09` §7.3). A booking of 4,000 locations with 8–12 showtimes each in
the biggest auditoriums is what allows a $100M weekend to physically occur; a
booking of 2,600 caps the same film well below that regardless of interest.
PLF and IMAX matter disproportionately because they combine a ticket premium
of $4–8 with the highest occupancy rates in the building — an event title can
take 15–25% of its opening weekend from under 1,000 PLF screens.

**Genre → urgency and social form.** Genre acts by setting how quickly the
audience feels it must go. Horror is the extreme case: it is a communal,
opening-weekend, go-with-friends experience with a young audience, minimal
review sensitivity, and no reason to wait. Adult drama is the opposite — the
audience is older, decides on reviews and conversation, and does not consider
the first weekend meaningful. This is why an $8M horror film can open to $30M
and a $60M drama can open to $9M and both be working as designed.

**Stars → floor, not ceiling.** A star's mechanism is *risk reduction for the
undecided*: a recognisable lead gives an unconvinced viewer a reason to
believe the film will be competently entertaining. That reliably lifts the
floor of interest, which is why stars still command their quotes
(`12-talent-labor-and-careers.md`). It does not create urgency, which is why
star power no longer produces enormous openings by itself, and why the
strongest star effects now appear internationally (§9) where a familiar face
substitutes for cultural context.

**Date and competition → share of a fixed market.** Competition acts through
two channels that are worth separating: **attention** (two campaigns for the
same quadrant in the same fortnight cannibalise interest) and **capacity** (the
exhibitor cannot give two saturation bookings the same screens). The second is
often the bigger effect and is invisible in tracking.

**Reviews → permission, conditionally.** For adult and specialty films reviews
*are* the awareness mechanism and act directly on the opening. For pre-sold
titles they act on the second weekend instead, by shaping what the opening
audience expects to find and therefore what it says afterwards
(`16` §6).

### 6.2 What money can and cannot buy

| Quantity | Bought with P&A? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Total awareness | **Yes, reliably** | The most purchasable thing in the business |
| Unaided awareness | Partly | Requires distinctive creative, not just reach |
| Definite interest | **No** | A property of the premise and the assets, not the spend |
| First choice | Partly | Can be won by out-shouting a rival on the same weekend |
| Screen count | Indirectly | Won by convincing exhibitors, and by the tracking the spend produced |
| Legs | **No** (`09` §12.3) | — |

### 6.3 How an opening projection is actually built

The number the studio takes into the weekend is not a single model output. It
is a triangulation, done by the research and distribution departments jointly
in the final ten days:

1. **Comps** — three to six prior titles matched on genre, quadrant, season,
   screen count, and rating, adjusted for ticket-price inflation.
2. **Tracking** — the awareness/interest/first-choice grid by quadrant,
   converted to an opening range through historical conversion rates.
3. **Advance sales** — the most reliable input, available from the circuits in
   the final week, and the one that overrides the model when it disagrees.
4. **Screen and showtime count** — the physical ceiling (§15).
5. **Judgement** — the distribution chief's read, which in practice is the
   final arbiter and is where the value of experience actually lives.

The public number is then set *below* the internal one, habitually, so the
film can beat it (§10.3).

## 7. What determines the rest of the run

1. **Word of mouth** — CinemaScore and PostTrak recommend scores.
2. **Whether the campaign matched the film.**
3. **Competition arriving** in subsequent weekends, especially for the same
   quadrant.
4. **Holdover screens** — a film that holds gets kept; a film that drops loses
   its locations, which mechanically accelerates the decline.
5. **Repeat viewing** — rare, and concentrated in event and family titles.
6. **Awards momentum**, for platform releases expanding through the season.

### 7.1 The mechanisms

**Word of mouth is multiplicative and lagged.** It does not add an audience;
it multiplies the audience marketing already delivered, and it compounds week
over week (`16` §8). Its sign is set by the gap between expectation and
delivery, which is why a modest film that promises modestly can out-leg an
ambitious one that promised more than it had. The measurable proxy is
**definite recommend** — the percentage of the exit-polled audience who will
actively tell someone to go — because that is the quantity that propagates.
CinemaScore is the more famous number and the coarser one; recommend is what
the distribution department actually models against.

**Campaign–film match acts by changing who is in the room.** A mis-sold film
does not merely disappoint; it disappoints the *wrong audience*, assembled at
some expense, whose disappointment is loud and specific. This is why a
mis-sell is worse than a small campaign: it converts marketing spend into
negative word of mouth at scale.

**Competition acts on capacity first, attention second.** The film that opens
against you takes your best auditoriums and your evening showtimes, and it
does so on a schedule the exhibitor decides on Sunday night. The gross you
lose is real even if nobody who wanted to see your film changed their mind.

**Holdover screens are the reflexive term.** A film that holds is kept, at
full showtimes, in the big houses. A film that drops is halved, moved to a
120-seat auditorium at 11am and 10pm, and gone the following Friday. Because
next week's gross is a function of this week's capacity, decline compounds
faster than demand does. **Screen loss is both an effect and a cause of
decline** — an exhibitor reassigning screens turns a soft week into a
collapse.

**Repeat viewing is a second audience at zero acquisition cost** and is
covered separately in §16, because at the top of the market it is the
difference between a hit and a phenomenon.

**Awards momentum acts only on titles whose audience reads about awards.** A
nomination morning can lift a specialty title 40–200% week over week and
justify a re-expansion to 1,000+ locations; it does nothing measurable for a
wide commercial film.

### 7.2 The run's decision points

| Moment | Decision | Made by |
|---|---|---|
| Friday 9pm, week 1 | Add or cut Saturday showtimes | Exhibitor, per location |
| Saturday 8am, week 1 | Sustain, cut, or redirect the remaining media spend | Studio marketing + distribution |
| Sunday night, week 1 | Next Friday's showtime grid | Exhibitor film buyers |
| Monday, week 2 | Holdover negotiations; expansion or contraction | Distribution sales team |
| End of week 2 | Whether this is a title the studio talks about publicly | Studio leadership |
| Week 3–4 | PVOD/window date; whether to spend on an awards or "phenomenon" push | Distribution + home entertainment |
| Week 5+ | Re-release, extended cut, sing-along, or quiet exit | Distribution |

## 8. Break-even

The rule of thumb everyone quotes: **a film must gross roughly 2–2.5× its
negative cost worldwide to break even theatrically**, because:

- the exhibitor keeps roughly **45–50%** domestic and **55–60%** international;
- P&A must also be recovered out of the distributor's share;
- and studio overhead and interest are charged on top
  (`11-money-accounting-and-participations.md`).

### 8.1 The split, more precisely

"Half" is a useful average and a poor description. What a distributor actually
retains varies by market, by title, and by week:

| Market | Distributor's share of gross | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **US/Canada** | 50–55% blended | Sliding scale or aggregate; a tentpole can command 60%+ in week one, dropping to 30–35% by week five |
| **UK, Australia, Western Europe** | 40–47% | Lower than the US almost everywhere |
| **Japan** | ~50% | Often through a local distribution partner taking a further fee |
| **China (revenue-share import)** | **~25%** | The lowest share of any major market, before remittance |
| **Four-wall** | 100% | The distributor pays the rent instead |

The declining scale is why **legs are worth less per dollar than an opening**
to the distributor: the same $10M grossed in week six returns perhaps $3.5M in
rentals against $6M in week one. It is also why exhibitors like long runs and
distributors like big openings — a structural conflict of interest that shapes
every booking conversation (§13).

### 8.2 Worked — studio tentpole

A $100M negative with $100M global P&A:

```
Worldwide gross          $400M
Exhibitor's share       −$200M   (≈50% blended)
Distributor's rentals    $200M
P&A                     −$100M
Negative cost           −$100M
Theatrical result           $0   ← "break-even" at 4× negative cost, all-in
```

### 8.3 Worked — mid-budget wide release

A $35M negative, $40M domestic P&A, $20M international P&A, modest overseas
appeal:

```
Domestic gross           $70M     (opens $20M, 3.5× multiple)
International gross      $45M
Worldwide gross         $115M

Domestic rentals         $37M     (53%)
International rentals    $19M     (42%)
Total rentals            $56M
P&A                     −$60M
Negative cost           −$35M
Theatrical result       −$39M     ← recovered, if at all, from home
                                     entertainment and licensing
```

This is the shape of most "respectable" mid-budget results, and it is why
mid-budget theatrical largely migrated to streaming: at $115M worldwide the
film is a theatrical loss and a lifetime break-even, which is a hard case to
make at greenlight.

### 8.4 Worked — low-budget horror

A $9M negative, $22M domestic P&A, front-loaded run:

```
Domestic gross           $58M     (opens $26M, 2.2× multiple)
International gross      $37M
Worldwide gross          $95M

Domestic rentals         $31M
International rentals    $15M
Total rentals            $46M
P&A                     −$30M     (incl. $8M international)
Negative cost            −$9M
Theatrical result        +$7M     ← profitable before ancillary, which is
                                     where the real money is
```

The genre's economics are entirely about the ratio: a 2.2× multiple is
irrelevant when the opening alone is three times the negative cost.

### 8.5 Worked — platform / specialty release

A $12M negative, opening in 5 locations and expanding over eight weeks, with
an awards campaign:

```
Locations:  5 → 40 → 250 → 900 → 1,400, then contracting
PSA week 1  $45,000  (the number the whole strategy exists to produce)

Domestic gross           $28M
International gross      $16M
Worldwide gross          $44M

Total rentals            $19M
P&A                      −$9M     (incl. ~$3M awards campaign, see `13`)
Negative cost           −$12M
Theatrical result        −$2M     ← close to flat, with awards value,
                                     library value, and prestige as the return
```

The platform strategy is a mechanism for *manufacturing evidence*: a huge
per-screen average is a news story, and the news story is the marketing the
film cannot afford to buy. Its failure mode is expanding too fast — going to
900 locations on a Friday when the reviews and awards heat only support 250
turns a strong PSA into a weak one overnight and ends the run.

### 8.6 Worked — true independent

A $4M negative, no studio, distribution acquired at a festival for a modest
minimum guarantee, or self-distributed:

```
Acquisition / MG          $2M to the producers (which is their recoupment)
Distributor P&A           $6M
Domestic gross           $11M
Domestic rentals         $5.5M
Distributor position     −$2.5M before international and ancillary
Producer position         Repaid from the MG; sees an overage only after the
                          distributor recoups MG + P&A + a distribution fee
                          (`11-money-accounting-and-participations.md`)
```

The lesson independents learn the hard way is that **a theatrical release is a
cost centre they are paying for out of their own revenue share**. It is often
undertaken anyway, because a theatrical run raises the value of every
downstream window and is a contractual requirement for many awards and
festival paths.

### 8.7 What the rule of thumb hides

The 2–2.5× rule is *theatrical-only against negative cost*, ignores marketing,
and ignores the fact that **most films recoup after theatrical**, via home
entertainment, licensing, and library value. It also hides:

- **Participations.** First-dollar and gross-after-breakeven deals are paid
  out of rentals before the studio sees anything (`11` §2).
- **Interest and overhead.** Studio overhead charges of 10–15% of the negative
  and interest on the production loan are real line items in the P&L.
- **Tax incentives**, which can reduce the effective negative by 15–40%
  (`03-financing-and-dealmaking.md`).
- **Timing.** Rentals arrive 30–90 days after the run; P&A is spent before it.
  A film can be profitable and still create a cash-flow hole for two quarters.

A theatrically unprofitable film can be a lifetime-profitable asset — and
studios increasingly greenlight on lifetime models, not opening weekends.

## 9. International specifics

- **Split**: roughly **30–40% domestic / 60–70% international** for a broad
  studio title, but genre-dependent. Action, animation, spectacle, and
  established franchises travel; comedy, dialogue-driven drama, and
  culturally-specific material do not.
- **Release dating abroad** is staggered by territory, sometimes weeks or
  months after domestic, though day-and-date is now standard for tentpoles to
  fight piracy.
- **Localisation** — dubbing (standard in much of Europe, Latin America, and
  Asia) or subtitling, plus censorship compliance and locally-cut trailers.
- **Currency and remittance** — grosses are converted, and some territories
  restrict repatriation.

### 9.1 The major territories

Shares below are directional percentages of a broad studio title's
international gross; every one of them swings hard by genre.

| Territory | Share | What plays, and why |
|---|---|---|
| **China** | 0–25% | Quota-limited imports (a few dozen revenue-share slots a year), ~25% distributor share, content approval by the regulator, and dates often confirmed only 2–4 weeks out. Spectacle, IMAX-scale action, and known franchises; almost nothing dialogue-led. Local titles now dominate the calendar, and the Hollywood share has fallen materially since 2019. A Chinese release can add hundreds of millions or be denied entirely, which makes it unbankable at greenlight |
| **UK & Ireland** | 8–12% | English-language, near day-and-date with the US, no dubbing. Over-indexes on British talent, prestige drama, and comedy the US thinks is niche. Half-terms and Boxing Day drive family. Reviews carry more weight than in the US |
| **Japan** | 5–12% | Slow, deliberate dating — often 1–4 months behind domestic — with a local distribution partner. Animation and family are enormous; local live-action and anime dominate the annual top ten. Dubbed and subtitled versions run in parallel, with the subtitled version skewing urban and older. Long runs are still possible here in a way they are not in the US |
| **Korea** | 4–8% | Fast, front-loaded, extremely high multiplex penetration; Wednesday openings; a sophisticated audience with a strong local industry. Real-time national admissions reporting makes it the most transparent market in the world |
| **France** | 5–8% | Wednesday openings; subtitled (**VO**) prints in Paris and major cities, dubbed (**VF**) in the regions — the same film needs both. A protected local industry and a strong local comedy sector that Hollywood cannot displace. Strict media-chronology window rules |
| **Germany** | 5–8% | Thursday openings; a **dubbing market** with a mature dubbing industry and audience expectation of it. Family, comedy, and broad action; regionally staggered school holidays spread the family window |
| **Mexico** | 4–7% | Very high admissions on low ticket prices; young audience; dubbed prints dominate outside the biggest cities. Over-indexes hugely on animation and horror |
| **Brazil** | 3–6% | Similar profile to Mexico; dubbed-dominant; sensitive to currency and to macroeconomic conditions |
| **Australia & NZ** | 3–5% | English-language, near day-and-date, southern-hemisphere holiday calendar (December–January is the long school break) |
| **Italy / Spain** | 3–6% each | Dubbing markets; historically very soft in high summer; strong local comedy sectors |
| **India** | 1–4% | Vast admissions on very low ticket prices, so gross understates reach. Hollywood dubs into Hindi, Tamil, and Telugu, and takes a modest share of a market dominated by local star vehicles dated to festivals |
| **Middle East** | 1–3% | Growing since the Saudi market reopened in 2018; censorship-sensitive, and cuts are sometimes required or the title is refused |
| **Russia/CIS** | 0% currently | A top-ten market until the majors withdrew in 2022 |

### 9.2 How dating differs abroad

- **Day-and-date for tentpoles**, driven by piracy and by the impossibility of
  containing spoilers for a week, let alone a month. Some territories open
  *ahead* of the US to exploit a local holiday or to bank a strong first
  weekend narrative.
- **Deliberate lag for mid-size and specialty titles**, to piggyback on
  domestic reviews, awards nominations, or an unexpected hit's momentum. A
  sleeper can be re-planned across 40 territories in a fortnight.
- **Local calendar first, domestic calendar second.** A film that opens in
  July domestically may be held to a Golden Week, Chuseok, or Christmas slot
  abroad if that is worth more than the piracy risk.
- **Regulatory dating.** China's slots, India's festival congestion, and
  censorship review timelines are constraints, not preferences.

### 9.3 Dubbing versus subtitling

The line is largely historical and is stable within each market:

- **Dubbing markets** — Germany, France (outside the major cities), Italy,
  Spain, Latin America, Japan (family), Turkey, and much of Eastern Europe.
  Dubbing costs roughly $50,000–$200,000 per major-language version for a
  studio title and takes 3–6 weeks, which puts a hard floor under how fast a
  territory can be dated after picture lock (`07-postproduction.md`).
- **Subtitling markets** — the Nordics, the Netherlands, Portugal, and most
  urban Asian markets outside Japan's family sector.
- **Both, in parallel** — Japan, France, and Korea commonly run VO and dubbed
  versions simultaneously, which doubles the print/DCP logistics and splits
  the showtime grid.

Dubbing is not merely a cost: it is an audience-widening decision. A dubbed
family title reaches children who cannot read subtitles, which is why
animation's international index is so much higher than live-action drama's.

### 9.4 How a territory's taste is actually assessed

Not by intuition. The international distribution group maintains, for every
territory:

1. **A comp library and an index.** Each territory has a normal share of a
   film's international gross, and each genre has a multiplier on that share.
   "This territory indexes 150 on animation and 60 on adult drama" is the
   working language.
2. **Local marketing and distribution leadership** — a managing director per
   major territory who owns the local campaign, local dating, and the
   relationship with local exhibitors, and whose read overrides head office on
   local questions.
3. **Local research** — tracking exists in the six to ten biggest territories
   and nowhere else; smaller markets are estimated from comps.
4. **Exhibitor feedback**, which is blunt and early: what circuits will commit
   is the cleanest read on local expectation.
5. **Talent recognition data** — a star's local familiarity is measured
   separately per territory and can differ wildly from their domestic
   standing.

### 9.5 International failure modes

| Failure | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Dated too late; piracy fills the gap | Opening down 20–50% in the affected territories |
| Dubbing not commissioned in time | Territory slips a quarter or opens subtitled and under-performs |
| Censorship cut refused or imposed badly | Title banned, or released in a version the local press attacks |
| Comedy sold as comedy abroad | Humour does not translate; word of mouth collapses in week two |
| Local competition misjudged | Opening against a local festival tentpole in India or Korea can halve the result |
| Over-reliance on a China release | The film is greenlit on a gross that may never be approved |
| Remittance restrictions | Money earned and not repatriable, or repatriable at a punitive rate |

## 10. Reporting and estimates

### 10.1 How the numbers physically move

The chain is shorter and more human than outsiders assume:

```
Cinema POS system
   ↓  (automated overnight feed, plus phoned/emailed reports from small circuits)
Circuit head office
   ↓
Comscore  (the industry's box-office data provider; absorbed Rentrak's
            theatrical business, and now collects from the great majority of
            US/Canada screens)
   ↓
Studio distribution departments  ← also receive their own numbers direct
   ↓
Studio-issued estimates → the trades (Variety, THR, Deadline)
   ↓
Public sites (Box Office Mojo, The Numbers) and the wider press
```

Two features of this chain matter. First, **the studios are both a customer
and a source**: they publish the weekend estimates that the trades report, and
those estimates are their own. Second, **coverage is not complete** — a small
percentage of screens report late or not at all, which is one reason Monday
actuals differ from Sunday estimates even without any spin.

### 10.2 The weekend timetable

| When | What lands | Who acts on it |
|---|---|---|
| **Thu ~10pm–1am ET** | Preview grosses | Distribution, marketing, the trades by breakfast |
| **Fri ~5–7am ET** | Thursday actuals; full-day Friday forecast | Distribution chief, studio leadership |
| **Fri all day** | Hourly occupancy and same-day sales from the circuits | Exhibitor programmers adjust Saturday showtimes |
| **Fri ~9pm–midnight** | CinemaScore and PostTrak from opening-night exits | Everyone. This is the reception verdict |
| **Sat ~5–7am ET** | **Friday actuals** | The weekend projection is rebuilt; marketing spend is re-weighted or cut |
| **Sat morning call** | Distribution, marketing, and research agree the shape of the weekend | Studio |
| **Sun ~8–9am ET** | Saturday actuals; the **Sunday estimate** is agreed | Distribution chief signs it off |
| **Sun ~late morning ET** | Estimates released to the trades; the weekend narrative is set | The press |
| **Mon early afternoon** | **Monday actuals** correct the estimate | Everyone quietly |
| **Mon–Tue** | Booking calls for the following Friday | Distribution sales and exhibitor buyers |

International numbers arrive on a different and messier schedule, territory by
territory, and the "international weekend" reported on Sunday is a partial
figure with real gaps.

### 10.3 The politics of estimates

Sunday estimates are a **studio press release**, not a measurement, and every
professional reads them as such.

- **Optimism when a headline is at stake.** A studio that can claim "#1 at the
  box office" or "the biggest opening of the year" will find the number that
  gets there. Monday's actuals correct it and nobody reports the correction.
- **Pessimism before the fact.** Studios habitually project *below* their
  internal number in the run-up, so the opening "beats expectations". Tracking
  leaks to the trades serve the same purpose.
- **The most-scrutinised gap is second place.** Sunday estimates that put two
  films within a few hundred thousand dollars routinely flip on Monday, and
  the flip is a genuine reputational event for whoever claimed the crown.
- **Rounding conventions carry meaning.** "$100.1M" exists because $100M is a
  threshold worth crossing; so do "over $1 billion worldwide" announcements
  timed to a specific Sunday.
- **Adjusted-for-inflation and "biggest ever" claims** are almost always
  constructed against a favourable comparison set — biggest opening for a
  September release, biggest for an R-rated film, biggest for the studio.
  Nobody involved thinks this is dishonest; it is the genre.

### 10.4 What the reported number is not

- Grosses are reported **gross of the exhibitor's share** — the headline number
  is never what the studio receives, which is a persistent source of public
  misunderstanding about profitability.
- They are **not admissions.** Domestic gross rises with ticket prices, which
  makes "biggest ever" lists a poor guide to how many people actually went.
  Most of the rest of the world reports admissions and is more honest for it.
- They **exclude nothing for PLF premiums**, which means a film with a heavy
  IMAX footprint looks larger relative to its attendance than one without.
- They **omit four-walled and non-reporting venues**, and the international
  figures aggregate dozens of currencies at whatever rate the studio used that
  morning.

## 11. Failure modes and their signatures

| Signature | Diagnosis |
|---|---|
| Big opening, >65% drop | Bad film, well sold. The campaign worked; the movie didn't |
| Weak opening, small drops, high multiple | Good film, badly sold — or a niche that found itself |
| Weak opening, big drop | Nobody wanted it and nobody who saw it liked it |
| Strong domestic, weak international | Culturally specific; comedy, or dialogue-led |
| Weak domestic, strong international | Spectacle-led, star-driven abroad, or franchise inertia |
| Great reviews, no gross | No awareness; insufficient P&A, or wrong date |
| Great tracking, weak opening | Tracking measured awareness, not desire — the hook didn't convert |

### 11.1 More signatures, and what a distributor does about each

| Signature | Diagnosis | The distributor's actual response |
|---|---|---|
| **Big opening, >65% drop, C CinemaScore** | Mis-sold, or the film betrays its own promise | Cut all remaining spend by Saturday morning of week one; accelerate the PVOD date to the 17-day minimum; take the money and stop paying to advertise a film people dislike |
| **Big opening, >65% drop, A− CinemaScore** | Pure front-loading, not a quality problem | Hold the line: keep showtimes, argue the case to exhibitors with the exit polls, and expect the multiple to land around 2.2× anyway |
| **Weak opening, tiny drops** | Good film, badly sold, or a niche that found itself | Re-cut the campaign around word of mouth ("the film everyone is talking about"), buy back showtimes, and lobby circuits hard for a hold. The rare case where post-opening spend is justified |
| **Saturday below Friday, non-holiday** | Instant negative word of mouth | Assume a 60%+ second weekend; brief leadership before Sunday's estimate goes out |
| **Great previews, soft Friday** | The pre-sold core was the whole audience | Revise the weekend projection down on Friday lunchtime; manage the trade narrative before the estimate |
| **Strong Thursday, strong Friday, weak Sunday** | An urgent young audience with nothing behind it | Book for a fast decay; expect week two at −60% |
| **Good hold, collapsing showtimes** | Exhibitor gave the screens to a new release | Sales team escalates to the circuit's film buyer with occupancy data — the one argument that works (§13) |
| **Great reviews, no gross** | No awareness; wrong date; insufficient P&A | Re-platform: pull back to strong-performing locations, chase awards attention, and treat theatrical as a marketing spend for the home window |
| **Strong PSA in platform, dies on expansion** | Expanded faster than the interest supported | Halt the expansion, retreat to the top 150 locations, hold for nominations |
| **Big domestic, no international** | Culturally specific material, dialogue-led comedy, or a US-only star | Re-plan territory by territory; some markets go direct to home entertainment |
| **Strong international, weak domestic** | Spectacle-led, franchise inertia, or a star with more equity abroad | Shift the sequel's development toward what travels; the domestic result stops being the greenlight criterion |
| **Everything soft, no obvious cause** | The date was wrong, or the film is simply invisible | The hardest case: usually a positioning failure that was knowable at greenlight (`02-development.md` §8) |

### 11.2 The distributor's triage rule

By Saturday morning of the opening weekend, every wide release is sorted into
one of three boxes, and almost nothing moves between them afterwards:

1. **Working** — sustain spend, fight for holdovers, extend the window, start
   thinking about a sequel.
2. **Front-loaded but fine** — harvest efficiently, book for decay, do not
   spend money chasing legs that the exit polls say do not exist.
3. **Failed** — stop spending immediately, accelerate the window, protect the
   downstream value, and say as little as possible.

The discipline that separates a good distribution department from a bad one is
largely the speed and honesty of this triage. Money spent on Monday of week
two advertising a film with a C CinemaScore is the most reliably wasted money
in the industry.

## 12. Who actually runs a release

Distribution is a **studio department**, separate from marketing and entirely
separate from production. Nobody who made the film works on the release, and
the filmmakers have no contractual say in screen counts or holdovers.

### 12.1 The distributor's side

| Role | Owns | Reports to |
|---|---|---|
| **President / Head of Distribution** | The release strategy: dates, patterns, screen counts, terms, and the go/no-go on every in-run decision. Sits on the greenlight committee because "when can this open, and how wide?" is a greenlight question | Studio chairman |
| **EVP/SVP Sales (Domestic)** | The circuit relationships. Negotiates terms and commitments with the major chains, which is where the actual money is decided | Head of Distribution |
| **Division / regional sales managers** | Groups of circuits and independent exhibitors by region. The legacy branch-office structure, now consolidated to a handful of people covering the country | EVP Sales |
| **Bookers** | The mechanical allocation: which title, which location, how many screens, which weeks. High-volume, detail-critical work | Sales managers |
| **Distribution operations / print traffic** | DCP delivery, keys (KDMs), version control across formats and languages, the physical fact of the film being playable on 4,000 screens on Thursday afternoon | Head of Distribution |
| **Box-office analysis / research liaison** | Comps, projections, the weekend reporting, the Sunday estimate | Head of Distribution |
| **Head of International Distribution** | Everything above, per territory, through local managing directors | Studio chairman |
| **Local territory MD** | Local date, local pattern, local exhibitor deals, local marketing sign-off | International head |

### 12.2 The exhibitor's side

| Role | Owns |
|---|---|
| **Chief Content / Programming Officer** (circuit) | The circuit's whole booking strategy and the relationships with all studios |
| **Film buyer** | Which films play which of the circuit's locations, on what terms, for how many weeks. The counterpart to the studio's sales team |
| **Programmer / scheduler** | The showtime grid — which auditorium, how many shows, what times. Rebuilt weekly, adjusted daily |
| **Regional operations / district manager** | Staffing, concessions, and the physical operation that turns showtimes into admissions |
| **Theatre manager** | The building. Occupancy on the night, and the first person to know a film is dying |

### 12.3 The skill axis — what a good distribution chief does

1. **Dates a film honestly against what it is.** The most consequential
   decision in the department, made 12–24 months out, and the one where
   optimism is most expensive. Recognising that a film is a $60M title, not a
   $200M title, and dating it accordingly, is worth more than any in-run
   tactic.
2. **Reads the exhibitor's real appetite** rather than the flattering version.
   What a circuit will commit tells you what the market thinks; a chief who
   argues with that number instead of believing it books a release the film
   cannot fill.
3. **Triages fast and without ego** (§11.2). Killing spend on Saturday morning
   of a bad opening saves millions; killing it on Wednesday saves nothing.
4. **Wins screens without burning relationships.** Terms are negotiated across
   an entire slate over years. A chief who extracts punitive terms on a
   tentpole pays for it on the next three mid-budget titles.
5. **Knows what a number means, quickly.** Distinguishing front-loading from
   rejection on Friday night — from the internal multiplier, previews ratio,
   and exit polls — determines whether the next week's decisions are right.
6. **Protects the downstream.** Window timing, re-release potential, and
   awards positioning are all live decisions during the run, and a chief who
   only optimises the theatrical number destroys value elsewhere.
7. **Manages the narrative.** The trades' reading of a weekend affects
   tracking on the studio's next three films, exhibitor confidence, and talent
   relationships. Setting expectations low and framing results honestly enough
   to stay credible is a real, learnable skill.

### 12.4 How it fails

| Failure | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Over-wide booking on a soft title | PSA collapses, exhibitors lose money, and the circuit remembers at the next negotiation |
| Under-wide booking on a hit | Sell-outs the film never converts; a permanently lost opening weekend |
| Date held out of pride against a bigger film | 20–40% of the opening surrendered for nothing |
| Spend sustained on a failed opening | Millions burned advertising a film the audience has already rejected |
| Window accelerated on a film that was holding | Theatrical legs cut off in exchange for a smaller home number |
| Terms squeezed too hard | Fewer screens on the next title, worse showtimes, no goodwill on a hold |
| Late deliverables to operations | Missing formats, missing language versions, screens that cannot play the film on opening day |

## 13. How an exhibitor decides what to book and hold

Screen availability is not a given the distributor plans around; it is the
output of a separate business making its own decisions. Understanding those
incentives explains most of what happens to a film after Friday.

### 13.1 The exhibitor's economics

- **Concessions are the profit.** Ticket revenue is shared with the
  distributor at punishing rates; popcorn and soft drinks are not, and run at
  **80–85% gross margin**. Concessions are typically **30–40% of a circuit's
  revenue** and a much larger share of its *profit*.
- Therefore the exhibitor's real objective is **admissions**, not gross. A
  cheap ticket that brings a family of four through the door is worth more
  than a premium ticket sold to one person.
- **The building is a fixed cost.** Rent, staff, and utilities are paid
  whether a 200-seat auditorium is full or holds nine people. The marginal
  cost of an extra showtime is close to zero, which is why grids are dense and
  why an underperforming film is given a bad time slot rather than removed
  outright.
- **Subscription programmes** (unlimited monthly passes) change the calculus
  again: they pre-commit the customer to the *building*, so the circuit's
  interest shifts toward having something for everyone rather than maximising
  any single title.

### 13.2 What the film buyer is actually looking at

| Input | What it tells them |
|---|---|
| **Comps** | What films of this type did in *these specific locations*, which the studio's national comps do not capture |
| **Advance sales on their own system** | The best data anyone has, and they own it |
| **Tracking** | Bought from the same research firms the studios use |
| **The studio's marketing commitment** | A stated national spend is a promise the buyer holds them to |
| **The rest of the weekend** | Booking is a portfolio decision — the buyer needs the whole grid to work, not one title |
| **Local demographics** | A location's catchment decides whether it wants the horror title, the family title, or the subtitled prestige title |
| **Auditorium inventory** | How many big houses and PLF screens exist, and who else wants them |
| **Terms asked** | A high rental ask on an unproven title is a reason to book fewer screens, not more |
| **The commitment demanded** | Studios ask for guaranteed weeks; a two- or three-week commitment on a title the buyer doubts is a genuine cost |

### 13.3 The weekly rhythm on the exhibitor's side

| When | What happens |
|---|---|
| **4–8 weeks out** | Terms and screen commitments negotiated for a wide release; PLF blocks allocated, often months ahead for event titles |
| **2 weeks out** | Locations and screen counts largely settled; on-sale opens |
| **Wednesday/Thursday** | Next week's grid finalised and published |
| **Friday, hourly** | Occupancy watched live; a sell-out gets shows added for Saturday, an empty room loses them |
| **Saturday morning** | The weekend's shape is clear; adjustments made for Sunday |
| **Sunday evening** | The following Friday's grid built: who holds, who moves to a smaller house, who goes |
| **Monday** | Studio sales calls; holdover arguments and settlements |

### 13.4 What actually earns a hold

1. **Occupancy per showtime, not gross.** A film doing $6,000 in a location
   across six shows in a 300-seat house is worse than one doing $5,000 across
   two shows in a 120-seat house. Buyers think in load factor.
2. **The trajectory within the weekend.** A film up on Saturday and holding
   Sunday reads as a film with a next week.
3. **Weekday performance.** Weekday strength signals an older or repeat
   audience and is the classic argument for extending a run.
4. **Nothing better for the slot.** Holds are decided against the alternative,
   never in the abstract — a strong incoming slate kills holds that a weak one
   would have granted.
5. **The relationship.** A distributor with a good slate and a record of
   honest expectations gets the benefit of the doubt for a week; one that
   over-promises does not.

### 13.5 The structural conflict

The two sides want different shapes.

| | Distributor wants | Exhibitor wants |
|---|---|---|
| **Run shape** | A huge opening (when their share is highest) | A long run (when their share is highest) |
| **Window** | Short, to feed the home business | Long, to protect the reason to attend |
| **Screens** | Saturation on their title | A balanced grid across many titles |
| **Terms** | The highest possible rental | The lowest, plus a house allowance |
| **Metric** | Gross | Admissions and concession spend per head |

Most of the friction visible in a release — sudden showtime cuts, refused
holds, aggressive expansion of a rival — is this conflict resolving itself
weekly. Since the US consent decrees ended, block booking and circuit-wide
dealing are no longer prohibited in the way they were, and terms are set by
negotiating leverage rather than by regulation.

## 14. The distributor's operational week during a release

What the department actually does, hour by hour, on the weekend that decides
the film. This is the same information as §10.2 seen from the inside, with the
decisions attached.

| When | Who | What they do |
|---|---|---|
| **Mon–Wed before** | Sales, bookers | Final screen count confirmed; showtime counts collected by circuit; PLF sessions verified |
| **Wed** | Operations | Every DCP and KDM confirmed playable; foreign-language and format versions checked. A film that cannot play on Thursday afternoon is an unrecoverable failure |
| **Thu am** | Research, distribution | Final projection agreed internally; the public number set lower |
| **Thu 3–7pm** | Everyone | Previews start; hourly sales monitored |
| **Thu ~11pm** | Analysis team | Preview gross compiled and circulated; the previews-to-projection ratio is the first real evidence |
| **Fri 5am** | Distribution chief | Thursday actuals; weekend projection revised; leadership briefed before the trades write |
| **Fri daytime** | Marketing | Media weight for the weekend adjusted — a good Thursday buys more Saturday spots, a bad one stops the buy |
| **Fri evening** | Research | CinemaScore and PostTrak fielded at exit; results by late evening |
| **Fri ~11pm** | Chief + marketing head | The verdict conversation. Triage decision (§11.2) is effectively made here |
| **Sat 5am** | Analysis | Friday actuals; internal multiplier estimated; second-weekend forecast built |
| **Sat 9am call** | Distribution, marketing, research, sometimes the chairman | Sustain / harvest / stop. Remaining spend reallocated the same morning |
| **Sun 8am** | Chief | Saturday actuals; the Sunday estimate agreed, with an eye on what rivals will claim |
| **Sun late am** | Publicity | Estimates to the trades; the weekend's narrative is set for the week |
| **Mon pm** | Analysis, sales | Actuals; holdover calls with circuit buyers; next Friday's locations agreed |
| **Tue–Wed** | Chief, home entertainment | Window date confirmed or accelerated; international dating adjusted on the domestic result |

The compressed truth: **the film's commercial fate is understood within about
30 hours of the first preview, and almost every decision that follows is
either harvesting or damage control.**

## 15. Capacity, showtimes, and the arithmetic ceiling

Gross is not demand. It is:

```
seats  ×  showtimes  ×  occupancy  ×  average ticket price
```

Every one of those terms is a real constraint, and practitioners think about
the ceiling before they think about the demand.

- **Locations vs screens vs showtimes.** A 4,000-location release is not 4,000
  screens. A saturation booking of an event title runs on **8,000–12,000
  screens** and 40,000+ showtimes a weekend, because a single multiplex will
  put it in four or five auditoriums. Reported "theatre counts" therefore
  understate a big booking badly, and comparing two films' theatre counts
  without their showtime counts is meaningless.
- **The US/Canada market has roughly 40,000 screens** across around 5,500–6,000
  locations, with the three largest circuits controlling close to half. That
  number is the physical ceiling on everything.
- **Occupancy is the honest efficiency metric.** Across a full year, average
  occupancy in the US runs in the low teens as a percentage of seats. On the
  opening weekend of an event film, prime-time shows run **70–95%**, which is
  where showtimes get added and where the ceiling actually bites: a sold-out
  Saturday evening is revenue that cannot be recovered.
- **Ticket price** in the US averages around **$11**, with a **$4–8 premium**
  for PLF and IMAX and matinee/discount pricing pulling the other way. Because
  the reported number is gross and not admissions, price inflation flatters
  every year-on-year and all-time comparison.
- **PLF supply is the contested resource.** Domestic IMAX commercial screens
  number in the few hundreds; all PLF brands together are a low four-figure
  count. For an event film these screens can deliver **15–25% of the opening
  weekend** on a few percent of the screen count, which is why IMAX
  allocations are negotiated a year ahead and why two event films are almost
  never allowed to sit on the same date.

**Why this matters for interpretation**: a film that "should" have opened to
$150M and opened to $120M may have been capacity-constrained rather than
under-desired, and the tell is occupancy — a weekend at 90% prime-time
occupancy left money on the table, while one at 55% did not.

## 16. Repeat viewing and event-ness

Ordinary hits are seen once. The very biggest grosses in history are not
explained by more people going; they are explained by the same people going
again. This is a distinct mechanism from word of mouth and it behaves
differently.

### 16.1 What it is worth

Repeat attendance is measured directly in exit polling ("is this your first
time seeing this film?").

| Film type | Typical repeat share of admissions |
|---|---|
| Ordinary wide release | 2–5% |
| Well-liked franchise title | 8–15% |
| Family/animation in a holiday corridor | 10–20% |
| Genuine cultural phenomenon | **20–35%** |

At 25% repeat viewing, a film's gross is a third higher than its unique
audience implies, and — crucially — the incremental audience costs **nothing
to acquire**. This is the single biggest lever separating a $600M domestic
gross from a $300M one.

### 16.2 What makes a film repeatable

1. **A spectacle that rewards a better screen.** The most common driver: the
   second viewing is in IMAX, and it costs more.
2. **Density.** A film with more in it than one viewing can hold — jokes,
   detail, foreshadowing, background — gives people a reason to return.
3. **Social obligation.** Taking someone else who has not seen it. Family
   titles run on this almost entirely, and it is why they hold so well.
4. **Fandom identity.** For some audiences the count of viewings is itself the
   point, and communal screenings, costumes, and quote-along behaviour
   formalise it.
5. **Emotional re-experience.** Films people cry at get re-attended in a way
   films people admire do not.
6. **Music.** Musicals, concert films, and sing-along re-releases have the
   highest repeat rates in the business.

### 16.3 Event-ness — the other half

Event-ness is the property of a film being something that has to be attended
*now*, in a cinema, with other people. It acts on the opening; repeat viewing
acts on the tail; the very biggest films have both.

Markers of event-ness:

- **Spoiler risk.** If the culture will spoil it within a week, waiting has a
  cost, and the audience compresses into the first weekend.
- **A shared occasion.** The film is a place people go together — a holiday
  outing, an opening-night crowd, a fan gathering.
- **Format as a reason.** "See it in IMAX" is an argument for the cinema over
  the sofa and is the exhibitor's single strongest marketing message.
- **Cultural conversation.** Being in the conversation is a reason to attend
  independent of whether the film is good.
- **Scarcity.** A limited premium-format run, a one-week exclusive, or a
  concert film with announced end dates all manufacture urgency.

### 16.4 The tension

Event-ness and legs pull against each other. Maximum event-ness compresses the
gross into the opening; maximum repeatability spreads it. The films that post
the largest totals manage both — an urgent, unmissable opening, followed by an
audience that comes back. Films that engineer event-ness without giving anyone
a reason to return get the opening and the −65% drop, which is a perfectly
good business as long as the budget was set accordingly.

## 17. Common misconceptions

1. **"Opening weekend is the whole story."** It is roughly a third of the
   domestic gross and a much smaller fraction of lifetime value. It is
   reported obsessively because it is the only number available on a Sunday.
2. **"The studio gets the box office."** It gets roughly half of it domestic,
   less abroad, before P&A, participations, overhead, and interest (§8).
3. **"A film that made more than it cost was profitable."** Negative cost is
   less than half the money at risk once P&A is counted.
4. **"Big drops mean a bad film."** Drops measure how pre-sold the audience
   was at least as much as how much it liked the film (§3.1).
5. **"Marketing can save a film in week two."** It cannot. It can only stop
   wasting money (`09` §12.3).
6. **"Screen count is a reward for a good film."** It is a forecast made
   before anyone has seen it, by people with their own economics (§13).
7. **"All-time gross lists show the most popular films."** They show ticket
   price inflation. Admissions-adjusted lists look completely different.
8. **"The Sunday number is the number."** It is an estimate written by an
   interested party (§10.3).
9. **"International is a bonus."** For most studio films it is the majority of
   the gross and increasingly the reason the film exists in the shape it does.
10. **"A theatrical loss is a failure."** Theatrical is one window. Plenty of
    theatrically unprofitable films are lifetime-profitable assets, and
    greenlights are increasingly made on that basis (§8.7).
