# 17 — Star Power and Its Decline

*What a movie star actually adds at the box office, how that changed from the
contract era to now, and what the evidence says about the "death of the movie
star".* Extends `12` §3 (what makes a star bankable) and `10` §6 (what
determines opening weekend). Researched November 2026.

> **Domain reference.** Real industry, not this game. See `README.md`.

**How this was sourced.** Unlike most of the library, this document was written
from live research. Two limits apply. First, the research environment could
fetch Wikipedia pages directly but reached trade press (Variety, THR, Deadline,
The Numbers, Box Office Mojo) and academic papers only through search-engine
summaries of those pages. Every trade figure below was cross-checked against at
least two reports where possible, but none was read in the original article.
Second, the academic findings come from abstracts, journal metadata and press
accounts of the papers, not from the full texts. Where a claim depends on a
single source, an estimate, or the author's own arithmetic, it is marked.

Each claim is tagged by evidence strength:

- **[Documented]**: a recorded number or a peer-reviewed finding.
- **[Consensus]**: what trade reporting and executives broadly agree on, without
  rigorous measurement behind it.
- **[Contested]**: a claim with credible evidence on both sides, or one that rests
  mainly on opinion.
- **[Folklore]**: repeated widely, with little or no evidence.

## 0. Summary

1. **The decline is real, but it is not a collapse of all star value.** Original,
   star-led live-action films have lost most of their share of the top of the
   chart. One 31-year study puts original films at 36% of the North American top 50
   in 1995 and 10% in 2025. Upfront pay for the top tier has stayed at roughly
   $20–30M in nominal terms since 1996, which is about a halving in real terms.
   **[Documented, with the share figure from a single source]**
2. **Academic research has always found star effects smaller than the industry
   assumed.** The effects are real for opening revenue and for winning screens, but
   much of the apparent effect disappears once you account for stars being cast in
   films that were already more promising. The research finds no reliable effect on
   profit. **[Documented]**
3. **The best-supported model is that a star multiplies a film's other qualities
   rather than adding a fixed number of tickets.** A 2017 meta-analysis of 61
   studies finds a multiplier effect. An older line of research finds that stars
   cushion films with bad reviews, and the two findings pull in different
   directions. **[Documented, with the interpretation contested]**
4. **Star draw is concentrated in a small, ageing top tier.** In a 2023 industry
   survey asking which actors would draw people to a theater, most of the top 100
   were over 40. Several younger actors who are widely called stars have recently
   led films that failed (2024–2026). **[Documented survey; the interpretation is
   Consensus]**
5. **Recent hits involving stars combine a star with a "must-see" premise and a
   premium big-screen format.** Examples are *Top Gun: Maverick*, *F1* and
   *Project Hail Mary*. Mid-budget star-led films without an event hook fail
   often, including when reviews are good. **[Consensus, supported by a pattern of
   cases]**
6. **The claim that star value persists abroad is weaker than it used to be.**
   Hollywood's share of the box office outside the US has fallen sharply, most of
   all in Japan, China and Korea. Streamers paid stars heavily from about 2018 to
   2023 and have since become more selective. Third-party firms now publish
   estimates of a star's streaming value. **[Documented for market share;
   Contested for star value specifically]**

## 1. The history, era by era

### 1.1 The studio contract era (1920s–1950)

Stars were salaried employees on seven-year contracts. The studio chose their
roles, built their image, and kept the upside. Two legal events ended this:

| Year | Event | Effect on star value |
|---|---|---|
| Dec 1944 | *De Havilland v. Warner Bros.* (California Court of Appeal). Suspension time could no longer be added on to extend a seven-year contract. | Capped how long a studio could hold an actor. Usually read as the start of studio control over talent weakening. **[Documented]** |
| May 1948 | *United States v. Paramount Pictures* (US Supreme Court). Studios had to sell their theatre chains and end block booking. | Without guaranteed screens, studios stopped needing large standing rosters of contract players. Talent became freelance. **[Documented]** |
| 1950 | James Stewart's deal for *Winchester '73* (Universal), negotiated by Lew Wasserman. Stewart took 50% of profits instead of his $200,000 fee, which eventually paid about $600,000. He also got approval over the director and co-stars. | Usually cited as the first confirmed profit-participation deal for an actor in the sound era. This is the origin of "points." **[Documented, per Wikipedia]** |

The structural consequence was that **a star's value stopped being captured by
the studio and started being captured by the star**, through the agent. The
whole later debate about whether stars are "worth it" rests on this. Once a
star's fee rises to match the value they add, a studio gains nothing from
hiring them. Ravid's 1999 finding (§4) is exactly this.

### 1.2 The star era (roughly 1980–2008)

This period runs from the rise of agency packaging to the peak of the "$20
million club." Its features:

- **Upfront fees climbed to a ceiling of $20M, and then to $25M.** Jim Carrey's
  $20M for *The Cable Guy* was announced in June 1995. That was close to double
  what Mel Gibson and Harrison Ford were then being paid. Arnold Schwarzenegger
  reportedly got $20M for *Eraser* (1996). Julia Roberts was the first woman to
  reach $20M, for *Erin Brockovich* (2000), and later got a reported $25M for
  *Mona Lisa Smile* (2003). Sources disagree on whether Carrey's first $20M was
  for *Batman Forever* or *The Cable Guy*. **[Documented, with "firsts" disputed]**
- **First-dollar gross** was the top tier's real prize: a percentage of gross
  receipts from the first dollar, before the studio recovered its costs. It is
  described in `11-money-accounting-and-participations.md`.
- **Original star vehicles regularly opened big and grossed $100M or more.**
  Concrete markers, with domestic openings:

| Film | Year | Star | Opening | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *Liar Liar* | 1997 | Jim Carrey | $31.4M | $181M domestic, $303M worldwide, $45M budget. An original comedy. **[Documented, Wikipedia]** |
| *Signs* | 2002 | Mel Gibson | $60.1M | Original screenplay. **[Single secondary source]** |
| *Bruce Almighty* | 2003 | Jim Carrey | $68.0M | Original screenplay. **[Single secondary source]** |
| *I Am Legend* | 2007 | Will Smith | $77.2M | A December record at the time. Based on a novel, but sold on Smith. $585M worldwide. **[Documented, Wikipedia]** |

- **Will Smith's run is the period's emblem.** He had eight consecutive films
  over $100M domestic, from *Men in Black II* (2002) to *Hancock* (2008), most of
  them outside existing franchises. Tom Cruise and Tom Hanks had comparable runs
  for a time. If voice roles such as *Shark Tale* and *Toy Story 2* are left out,
  the runs are shorter. **[Documented, with counting caveats]**
- **The year-end top ten still held many films that were not franchises.** In
  1995, six of the global top ten were not sequels or existing film franchises:
  *Toy Story*, *Apollo 13*, *Pocahontas*, *Seven*, *Waterworld* and *Jumanji*. Three
  of them were sold mainly on their leads (Hanks, Pitt and Freeman, Costner).
  **[Author's tally from Wikipedia "1995 in film"; what counts as "IP" is a
  judgement call]**

### 1.3 The turn (roughly 2006–2012)

The usual turning-point markers. Each is documented as an event, but contested
as an explanation.

- **August 2006: Paramount does not renew Tom Cruise's production deal.** Sumner
  Redstone cited Cruise's conduct. Cruise's side said they had walked away. Trade
  analysts at the time read it as studios trying to unwind gross-participation
  deals as DVD revenue flattened. **[Event Documented; the cause Contested]**
- **2008–2010: the DVD market contracts and the recession hits.** In April 2009
  the *Wall Street Journal* reported that studios were cutting first-dollar gross
  deals. Paramount dropped them on two projects, though Denzel Washington kept his
  for *The Book of Eli*. Deals moved toward back-end pay that starts only after
  the studio reaches "cash break-even." Jim Carrey took no upfront fee for *Yes
  Man* in exchange for a larger back end. Cruise later forwent a preferential
  gross share on *Mission: Impossible – Ghost Protocol*. **[Consensus, from
  multiple trade reports. There was a pullback with exceptions, not an
  industry-wide end.]**
- **Franchise consolidation.** Disney bought Marvel (2009) and Lucasfilm (2012).
  By 2015 nine of the global top ten were franchise or existing IP. The exception
  was *The Martian*, a novel adaptation led by Matt Damon. **[Author's tally from
  Wikipedia "2015 in film"]**

### 1.4 The franchise / IP era (roughly 2012–2019)

- A series of trade pieces asked whether the movie star was dead. One example is
  THR's "If Jennifer Lawrence Can't Open a Movie, Who Can?" (2018), written after
  *Red Sparrow* opened to $16.9M against a reported $69M budget, with $46.9M
  domestic and about $146M worldwide. **[Figures Documented; the framing is
  opinion]**
- Will Smith's *Gemini Man* (2019) opened to $20.6M on a $138M budget. Deadline
  estimated a net loss of about $111M. **[Documented, Wikipedia citing Deadline]**
- In 2019 every film in the domestic top ten was a sequel, remake or adaptation of
  existing IP. **[Commonly reported; not re-tallied here]**

### 1.5 The post-2020 market (2020–2026)

The market itself shrank. North American admissions are estimated at about
770–780 million in 2025. That is down about 5% on 2024, and roughly half the
early-2000s level of about 1.4–1.5 billion a year. Revenue stayed near $8.9B
because ticket prices rose: $11.31 average in 2024 per The Numbers, and about
$13.46 for adults in 2026 per EntTelligence, which uses a different measure.
**[Documented within roughly ±5%; sources differ on the exact admissions
figure]**

So did the share taken by original films. The "Hollywood Originality Report"
(Bamfuzzle, 2025/26) analysed the North American top 50 for 1995–2025:

| Measure | 1995 | 2020–24 average | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Original screenplays as share of top-50 *titles* | 36% | — | 10% |
| Originals' share of top-50 *revenue* | 34.2% | 9.7% (lowest five-year average in the series) | 10.9% |

**[Single source, a new and non-trade study whose method was not verified here.
It also measures only the top 50, so it cannot show whether fewer originals are
being made.]**

The global top ten for 2025, from Wikipedia: *Ne Zha 2*, *Zootopia 2*, *Avatar: Fire
and Ash*, *Lilo & Stitch*, *A Minecraft Movie*, *Jurassic World Rebirth*, *Demon
Slayer: Infinity Castle*, *How to Train Your Dragon*, *F1* and *Superman*.
***F1* is the only original live-action film on the list, and the only one sold
mainly on a star.** **[Author's tally]**

### 1.6 Has opening power actually fallen? A ticket-count comparison

Comparing nominal grosses across thirty years overstates recent openings. The
author's approximate conversion to tickets, using the average ticket price of each
year:

| Opening | Nominal | Avg ticket | ≈ Tickets |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Liar Liar* (1997) | $31.4M | ~$4.59 | ~6.8M |
| *I Am Legend* (2007) | $77.2M | ~$6.9 (not verified) | ~11M |
| *Top Gun: Maverick* (2022), 3-day | $126.7M | ~$10.5 (premium-heavy) | ~12M |
| *Project Hail Mary* (2026) | $80.6M | ~$13+ (56% of gross from premium-format screens) | ~6M |

**[Author's arithmetic. Average-price figures are approximate, and premium-format
pricing means the 2020s rows are overstated as tickets per dollar if anything.]**

The point: a 2026 opening described as "the second-biggest for a non-franchise
film in a decade" sold roughly as many tickets as a successful 1997 star comedy.
**The ceiling for star-associated openings has not vanished**, as *Maverick*
shows. **What has vanished is the middle**: the steady supply of $25–60M openings
for original star vehicles in comedy, thriller and drama.

### 1.7 Salary as a market signal

| Period | Top-tier upfront fee | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1995–96 | $20M (Carrey, Schwarzenegger) | Plus back end, e.g. Carrey's reported 15% of profits on *The Cable Guy* |
| 2000–03 | $20–25M (Roberts) | |
| 2009–12 | Pressure on upfront fees and first-dollar gross | See §1.3 |
| 2018–23 | $20–30M+ upfront at streamers (e.g. *Red Notice*: Johnson, Reynolds and Gadot; sources disagree, $20–30M for Johnson) | Streamers buy out the back end, so they pay more upfront (§7) |
| 2022 | Cruise reportedly $13M upfront on *Top Gun: Maverick*, about $100M in total with the back end | Variety 2022, via secondary reports |
| 2025 | Pitt reportedly $30M upfront on *F1*, his biggest single-film fee, plus a gross participation | Secondary reports. Forbes estimates of total 2025 earnings: Sandler $48M, Cruise $46M, Pitt $41M |

**Reading.** The top-tier upfront fee in nominal dollars is roughly where it was
in 1996. US prices roughly doubled between 1996 and 2025 (the author's CPI
estimate), so **real upfront pay for the top tier has fallen by about half.**
Total earnings at the very top have been kept up by back ends on hits and by
streaming buyouts. **[Fee figures are press-reported estimates. The inflation
reading is the author's.]**

## 2. Why: the proposed causes and how strong the evidence is

| Cause | Mechanism claimed | Evidence strength |
|---|---|---|
| **Franchises make the character or brand the star** | The audience's reason to go is fixed months in advance by the IP (`10` §6.1), so the actor is replaceable | **Well documented as a fact about the market**: the fall in originals' share of the chart (§1.5), and the top ten tallies. **Contested as the cause** of falling star draw: it could equally be that studios stopped making star vehicles, so stars had nothing to open. Ben Fritz's *The Big Picture* (2018), drawing partly on the 2014 Sony hack material, documents the studio decision-making side. |
| **Studios changed how they greenlight** | Conglomerate parents, with DVD gone, prefer predictable IP returns. Mid-budget adult films are no longer made at the majors | **Well documented for the behaviour**: the collapse of mid-budget slates at the majors, Fritz (2018), and AFM reporting of "no middle ground" in pre-sales (2024–25). This supply-side cause sits behind most of the others. |
| **Streaming absorbed the mid-budget star film and the stars** | The genres that stars opened (adult thriller, rom-com, star comedy) moved to Netflix, Amazon and Apple, where they cannot produce a box-office record | **Plausible, partly documented.** Netflix and others made large star-led films from about 2018 to 2023 (*Red Notice*, *Extraction*, Sandler's Netflix deal). Apple released star films in theaters and then retreated (§7.2). No causal estimate exists. |
| **The audience aged and shrank** | Frequent moviegoers who choose films by star skew older. Younger audiences pick by IP or by social buzz | **Supported by survey.** In NRG's April 2023 survey (via Puck), most of the top 100 draws were over 40, the top 20 averaged about 57.5 years old, and the top ten were all men. NRG noted the sample skewed older. **Contested** in what it means. |
| **Fame fragments across social media and TV** | Attention spreads across thousands of creators and series. No actor reaches the shared awareness that 1990s stars had | **Plausible, weakly evidenced.** The library already notes (`12` §3.4) that follower counts don't convert. Searching found no rigorous study linking an actor's follower count to opening revenue, only anecdote (e.g. Bollywood commentary on Salman Khan's 2025 *Sikandar*). |
| **International markets went local** | Overseas audiences now favour local films and local stars, so a Hollywood face buys less abroad | **Well documented for market share** (§6.1). **Not directly measured** for stars as such. |
| **"There are simply no new stars"** | The pipeline that made stars (mid-budget star vehicles) is gone, so none get made | **Contested and partly circular.** It is the same as the greenlight cause seen from the actor's side. Powell, Chalamet, Zendaya and Sweeney are cited as counter-examples, with mixed records (§3). |
| **Stars were never as powerful as believed** | Bankability was always partly an illusion caused by selection (stars are cast in better projects) | **Strongly supported by academic work** (§4). This reframes the debate. Part of the "decline" may be the industry's beliefs catching up with what was always true. |

**The honest summary.** The causes on the supply side (greenlighting, franchises,
streaming) are the best documented. The causes on the audience side (fame
fragmenting, the audience ageing) are plausible and partly surveyed, but not
measured causally. The academic re-reading, that star power was always
overestimated, is the best evidenced claim and the least discussed in the trades.

### 3.3 What the stars who still open films have in common

This is the author's synthesis of the cases above. It is **[Consensus/Contested]**:
the pattern is consistent, but it is not a measured result.

1. **The film is an event in its own right.** It has a spectacle premise and is
   built for premium large formats (*Maverick*, *F1*, *Project Hail Mary*,
   *Oppenheimer*, *Sinners*). In every big success the star is attached to
   something already marketable as "must see on the big screen." Mid-budget star
   vehicles without that hook (*Fall Guy*, *Running Man*, *Crime 101*, *Smashing
   Machine*) fail even with good reviews.
2. **The star's persona matches the genre.** Cruise in aviation action and Pitt
   in a sports drama fit their personas. Johnson in a sombre biopic did not.
   This matches the genre-binding already described in `12` §3.3.
3. **A director or brand is doing part of the work.** Nolan, Gerwig, Coogler,
   Kosinski, and the Barbie and F1 brands. Very few recent successes can be put
   down to the star alone.
4. **The stars are mostly older, established names.** Cruise (60 at *Maverick*),
   Pitt (61 at *F1*) and Gosling (45 at *PHM*). Younger names have hits that
   depend on word of mouth (*Anyone But You*) or on IP (*Twisters*, *Dune*,
   *Wicked*). When they lead original theatrical films alone, they fail more
   often.
5. **The quality signals are strong.** The successes had A-range CinemaScores,
   high review scores and long runs. That fits the multiplier model in §4.

## 4. The threshold and the conditionality: what the research says

### 4.1 The academic literature, in order

| Study | Data | Main finding about stars | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| **De Vany & Walls (1999)**, *Journal of Cultural Economics* 23(4) | ~2,000 films, 1984–1996 | Revenues follow a heavy-tailed (Pareto) distribution with effectively infinite variance, so average-based forecasts are close to meaningless. Only **19 stars** (including a couple of directors) had a non-trivial correlation with films grossing over $50M domestic. After controlling for budget, screens, rating and sequel status, few individual stars had any significant effect, and some (Ryder, Stone, Kilmer) were linked to *lower* revenue. Stars help a film *open* but don't sustain it: "the audience makes a hit." | Peer-reviewed. Influential. The specific star coefficients are known here only from press summaries. |
| **Ravid (1999)**, *Journal of Business* 72(4) | Films from the early 1990s | Simple comparisons show star films earning more. **In regressions the star effect disappears into budget**: any big spend raises revenue. Neither stars nor big budgets predict *return on investment*. Consistent with **rent capture**: stars take their value in salary. | Peer-reviewed |
| **Basuroy, Chatterjee & Ravid (2003)**, *Journal of Marketing* 67(4) | Films with weekly reviews | Star power and big budgets **do little for films with mostly positive reviews**, but are **positively associated with box office for films with mostly negative reviews**. Bad reviews hurt more than good reviews help, and only in week one. | Peer-reviewed. **This is the evidence base for "stars as insurance" and "floor, not ceiling."** |
| **Elberse (2007)**, "The Power of Stars," *Journal of Marketing* 71(4) | 1,200+ casting announcements, 600+ stars, ~500 films. Event study on the Hollywood Stock Exchange (HSX), whose prices correlated 0.94 with eventual box office | Casting announcements do move expected revenue. A star is worth **about $3M** in expected theatrical revenue on average. Cruise leaving *Cold Mountain* knocked about $21.5M off its HSX value. An extension using real studio share prices found **no evidence that stars raise profits**. Elberse: the industry's belief in stars "stems from a focus on revenues rather than profits." | Peer-reviewed. HSX is a proxy for expectations, not outcomes. |
| **Karniouchina (2011)**, *International Journal of Research in Marketing* 28 | Online buzz measures | **Star buzz** lifts opening-week revenue and anticipation. **Movie buzz** helps throughout the run. Early buzz can backfire in later weeks if the film disappoints, but in simulations star buzz stays **net positive even for poorly received films**. | Peer-reviewed |
| **Nelson & Glotfelty (2012)**, *Journal of Cultural Economics* 36(2) | Box office in nine countries. A continuous star measure from IMDb page traffic | Replacing an average lead with a top star adds about **$16.6M** in revenue. Replacing three average stars with three top stars adds about **$64.4M**. | Peer-reviewed. The figures come from an abstract summary. Does not correct for selection (see Hofmann). |
| **Liu, Mazumdar & Li (2015)**, *Management Science* 61(7) | A switching model that corrects for non-random matching of stars to films | A star's presence matters **more than the film's own characteristics for winning theaters** (opening screen count). But **the revenue gap between star and non-star films is explained entirely by differences in the films**, not by the star. | Peer-reviewed. **The strongest "selection" result.** |
| **Hofmann, Clement, Völckner & Hennig-Thurau (2017)**, "Empirical Generalizations on the Impact of Stars…," *IJRM* 34(2) | **Meta-analysis**: 61 studies, 172 effects | Ignoring selection **strongly overstates** star effects. Stars show a **multiplier effect** on the other things that drive box office: **1.127** for commercially successful stars, **1.083** for artistically recognised ones. | Peer-reviewed meta-analysis. **The best single summary of the field.** |
| **Carrillat, Legoux & Hadida (2018)**, *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* | Meta-analysis: 150 studies, 634 effect sizes | The effect of a star's **popularity fades quickly after opening**. The effect of **artistically recognised** stars holds steady through the run. Popularity's box-office impact is **weaker than assumed**, which "calls into question the bankability of popular stars." | Peer-reviewed meta-analysis |

### 4.2 Threshold: is star draw continuous, or limited to a top tier?

- **The evidence supports a steep threshold.** De Vany & Walls found only 19 names
  out of the whole 1984–96 talent pool with a meaningful link to hits. Nelson &
  Glotfelty's "top star versus average lead" contrast implies a large gap at the
  top, and they measure star power continuously. Industry practice treats
  bankability as a short list: the "A-list" and the estimates grid (`12` §3.1).
  **[Documented in the 1990s data; Consensus now]**
- **The research does not settle whether the effect below the top is small but
  positive or effectively zero.** Most studies code "star" as a yes/no based on a
  list such as the Quigley top ten or the Hollywood Reporter Star Power ranking,
  which builds the threshold into the data. **[Unknown]**
- **The tier is narrow and old.** In NRG's 2023 survey, Zendaya at #17 was the
  youngest in the top 25. Among Gen Z respondents alone, Tom Holland and Zendaya
  ranked 10th and 14th. **[Documented survey; single source]**

### 4.3 Conditionality: does a star add turnout only when the film already looks good?

Two bodies of evidence pull in different directions. Both are real.

| Model | Evidence | What it implies |
|---|---|---|
| **Star as insurance (protects the floor)** | Basuroy et al. (2003): stars help films with bad reviews and do little for films with good ones. Karniouchina (2011): star buzz stays net positive even for poorly received films. De Vany & Walls: stars help a film open before word of mouth spreads, then fade. Trade practice: a star as "a reason the film gets bought at all" (`12` §3.3 item 9). | Stars add most where the film is otherwise weak, by buying a first weekend that the film's word of mouth would not. |
| **Star as multiplier (raises the ceiling)** | Hofmann et al. (2017): a multiplier of about 1.08–1.13 on the other characteristics that drive box office. Liu et al. (2015): the revenue gap comes from the films, not the stars. Carrillat et al. (2018): popularity fades after opening. Recent cases (§3): the hits combine a star with a strong premise. The flops have stars and lack the premise. | Stars add most, in absolute dollars, to films that already look good. A multiplier applied to a weak base produces little. |

**Reconciling them.** The two are compatible if you separate *when* and *how
much*. In percentage terms, a star's effect is concentrated in the **opening**
(all studies agree). A star can lift a weak film's first weekend somewhat, which
is the floor or insurance effect, measured against the critics. But in
*absolute* revenue the gain scales with the film's own appeal, which is the
multiplier. Nothing a star adds survives poor word of mouth. **[Author's
reconciliation; Contested]**

**What the field cannot yet say.** Whether a star can lift a film whose *trailer
and premise* test poorly. As distinct from bad reviews, this is the question that
matters for marketing. No public study isolates premise appeal from star
presence. The trade evidence is suggestive but anecdotal: *The Smashing Machine*
tracked at $8–17M and opened at $5.8M; *The Fall Guy* tracked at $30–40M and
opened at $28.5M. Both suggest the star did not rescue a premise that audiences
were lukewarm about. **[Unknown in research; Consensus in trade practice]**

### 4.4 How tracking treats stars in practice

- Tracking measures **awareness**, **definite interest** and **first choice**
  for the film, not for the star (`10` §6.1). The star shows up indirectly: in
  early awareness (casting news), and in how well that awareness turns into
  interest among the star's core demographic.
- **PostTrak** exit polls ask what brought people in. One example: at *The Fall
  Guy*, 50% of the audience said Gosling was the main reason and 35% said
  Blunt. Studios use these splits after the fact to settle the attribution
  argument described in `12` §3.5. **[Documented]**
- **NRG-style "which actors would make you go" surveys** measure stated
  preference. That is a different and weaker quantity than revealed draw.
- Elberse's HSX method is an academic version of what studios do internally:
  how much did expectations move when the name attached?

## 5. Market-wide or per-star?

The question: has star draw weakened as a whole, or have fewer people reached
the top tier?

| Hypothesis | Evidence for | Evidence against |
|---|---|---|
| **The whole market values stars less** | Real upfront fees for the top tier have roughly halved since 1996 (§1.7). Originals' share of the top 50 fell from 36% to 10% (§1.5). Studios cut first-dollar gross from 2009. Academic meta-analyses suggest stars were always overvalued, and pay has adjusted. | The top tier's *total* earnings remain very high: Cruise's ~$100M on *Maverick*, Pitt's $30M plus gross on *F1*. Streamers paid at or above theatrical rates in 2018–23. |
| **Fewer people reach the top tier (a narrower top)** | NRG 2023: the draws are old and few; Gen Z picks are IP-linked actors. Recent young "new stars" have both hits and flops (§3.2). The pipeline of mid-budget star vehicles that once built stars is gone (§2). | Some new names do open IP films, and *Anyone But You* shows a newer pairing can drive a long run. |
| **The market shrank under everything** | Admissions fell from about 1.4–1.5B to about 0.78B (§1.5). Every category opens smaller in tickets. | IP tentpoles still post very large nominal openings (a reported ~$360M for *Spider-Man: Brand New Day*, July 2026; **not verified here**), so the shrinkage is not uniform. |

**What the evidence supports: all three, compounding.** The data best fits this
reading: **the market as a whole stopped *paying* for stars as a separate input**
(pay, deal terms, greenlights). That **removed the films that created new
stars**, so **the top tier narrowed and aged**. And **the whole theatrical market
shrank**, so even top-tier openings sell fewer tickets than in 2000–2010. The
evidence does *not* show that the few stars still on top have lost their per-film
draw. Cruise's and Pitt's career-best openings came in 2022 and 2025. **[Author's
synthesis; Contested]**

Telling "less value per star" apart from "fewer stars" properly would need
star-level panel data across eras with the selection correction of Liu et al.
2015. That analysis does not appear to have been published.

## 6. International

### 6.1 What is documented

- **Hollywood's share abroad has fallen sharply:**
  - **Global:** Bloomberg (2025) reports US films' share of the global box office
    fell from **92% to 66%** over about twenty years. The FOCUS report (June 2026)
    puts North American films at **52% of global admissions in 2025**, down from
    63% in 2019. Asian films rose from 27% to 36%.
  - **Japan:** US films took about **20%** in 2025, down from a pre-pandemic
    average of about 39%. Local films took 76%.
  - **China:** per Maoyan, US films took 36% (2018), then 30% (2019), then **14%
    (2024)**.
  - **EU:** European films held 31.4% in 2025, against 26.1% in 2019.

  **[Documented, via secondary reports of Bloomberg, FOCUS and Maoyan]**
- **The international share varies enormously between star films:**

  | Film | International share |
  |---|---|
  | *F1* (sport with global fandom) | ~70% |
  | *Top Gun: Maverick* | ~52% |
  | *Project Hail Mary* | ~50% |
  | *Sinners* (Southern-gothic horror rooted in African American history) | ~25% |

  **[Documented]**
- **Cruise in Japan is a real per-star effect.** *Maverick* became his
  biggest film there (about $101M) after a run of more than a year. **[Documented]**

### 6.2 What this means for the claim that "star value persists abroad"

Before this research the library said (`12` §3.2) that foreign value "persists
years after domestic value has gone," and (`10` §6.1) that "the strongest star
effects now appear internationally." Both have since been qualified (§9).

- **The pre-sales version** is still standard trade practice at the AFM and
  Cannes markets. In 2024 sales agents reported interest in "genre films with
  strong storylines and star power," for example the Gerard Butler-led *Fallen*
  franchise. **[Consensus]**
- But 2024–25 market reporting also says buyers are **more risk-averse**, want a
  **clearly defined audience** and "distinctive, filmmaker-driven" projects, that
  **pure territory pre-sales are now rare**, and that there is **"no middle
  ground"** in the pre-sales market. Without a predictable Pay-1 TV value,
  distributors struggle to justify minimum guarantees. **[Consensus, 2024–25 trade
  reporting]**
- **There is no recent systematic evidence** that stars' value holds up *better*
  abroad than at home. The fall in Hollywood's foreign share suggests the
  opposite for the market as a whole, at least in East Asia. Whether particular
  stars (Cruise in Japan, Johnson's earlier strength in China) are exceptions or
  the rule is **[Unknown]**.
- **Genres that travel** are action, spectacle and sci-fi, with sport where the
  sport is global. **Comedy travels poorly** (`12` §3.2). Culturally specific
  drama and horror can be domestic-heavy: *Sinners* took about 75% of its gross
  at home. **[Consensus, consistent with the cases]**

## 7. Streaming

### 7.1 How streamers paid for stars

- **The upfront buyout model.** Streamers cannot offer back ends tied to box
  office, so they pay large fixed fees up front and buy out the back end.
  *Red Notice* (Netflix, 2021) is the standard example. Reports put Dwayne
  Johnson at $20–30M or more, with Reynolds and Gadot at about $20M each. Sources
  disagree. **[Documented, with conflicting figures]**
- **Netflix reviewed its large talent payments** in 2024 (Puck, May 2024). Large
  upfront deals still happen: one 2026 report puts above-the-line costs at about
  $80M on Netflix's $200M Brad Pitt-led *The Adventures of Cliff Booth*.
  **[Single-source reports]**
- **Netflix's own most-watched lists are star-heavy.** *Extraction* (Hemsworth)
  and *Bird Box* (Bullock) led its 2021 data release. Sandler's *The Ridiculous 6*
  was its most-watched film in 2016. But Netflix also called its own data "a big
  black box, mostly." **[Documented, but the platform chose what to disclose]**

### 7.2 Apple: the one public test of star films released in theaters by a streamer

Apple released a run of expensive star-led films in theaters in 2023–24:
*Killers of the Flower Moon*, *Napoleon*, *Argylle* and *Fly Me to the Moon*.
After disappointing results it **changed course in 2024**. *Wolfs* (Clooney and
Pitt) dropped its wide theatrical release and went to streaming, where it became
Apple TV+'s most-watched film. *F1* (2025), which had a premium-format event
premise, was Apple's first clear theatrical hit. **[Documented]** The pattern
matches §3.3: the stars alone did not carry the theatrical releases. The event
film with stars attached did.

### 7.3 Measuring star value on streaming

- **Third-party estimates now exist.** Parrot Analytics sells a "Talent Demand"
  measure. In November 2025 it estimated that Mark Wahlberg's films had generated
  about **$680M** in streaming value since 2020, counting acquisition, retention
  and advertising. It noted he led **7 of the top 170** most-watched Netflix
  films in that period. **[Modelled vendor estimate; partly promotional]**
- **Stated preference differs by platform.** Greenlight Analytics' survey data
  had Wahlberg at **54% "will see" on streaming versus 41% in theaters**. Its
  analyst described him as now an "added value element" on a theatrical film
  rather than "the primary incentive." **[Single source]**
- **Contested.** Industry podcasts and commentary (e.g. *The Town*, February 2026)
  argue that frequent releases on platforms everyone already has **dilute the
  scarcity that made someone a star in the first place.** **[Opinion]**

**Reading.** A star appears to be worth more relative to the alternative on
streaming, where the decision is low-cost and made at home and a familiar face
reduces risk, than in theaters, where the decision is costly and made on
urgency. This fits the "risk reduction for the undecided" mechanism already in
`10` §6.1. It is **[Plausible but not independently measured]**, because the
platforms do not publish viewing data at the level needed to test it.

## 8. What is genuinely unknown

1. **The counterfactual.** As `12` §3.5 notes, no one observes the same film
   without its star. The academic corrections (Liu et al. 2015, Hofmann et al.
   2017) use statistical matching, not experiments. Every estimate of star value
   is model-dependent.
2. **Whether star value fell per star or the tier just shrank.** No published
   panel study across eras separates the two (§5).
3. **Whether a star can rescue a weak *premise*,** as distinct from weak
   *reviews*. Studios hold the tracking data to answer this and do not publish it.
4. **Streaming star value.** The platforms hold the data and disclose it
   selectively. Vendor estimates (Parrot) are models.
5. **Recent academic estimates.** The peer-reviewed literature found here ends
   with data from the 2010s. **No published study found estimates star effects
   on post-2020 theatrical data.**
6. **Whether the 2022–26 hits signal recovery or are exceptions.** *Maverick*,
   *F1* and *Project Hail Mary* have one or two of the oldest top-tier stars, a
   premium-format premise and a strong director or producer each. It is unclear
   whether a new generation can do the same.
7. **International star value at the level of individual stars,** for any period
   after the 2010s.

## 9. Where this extends or qualifies the rest of the library

The passages below were written before this research. Each has been corrected
in place to point here; this section records what changed and why.

- **`10` §6, "Stars → floor, not ceiling."** Supported by Basuroy et al. (2003),
  De Vany & Walls (1999) and Karniouchina (2011). **Qualified** by the meta-
  analysis (Hofmann et al. 2017), whose multiplier finding means that in absolute
  dollars a star adds most to films that are already strong. "Floor" is right for
  *relative protection of a weak film's opening*. In absolute terms the larger
  effect is the multiplier on films that are already strong. Also, Liu et al.
  (2015) find a star's most robust effect is on **screen allocation**, a channel
  `10` §6 lists separately as "screens and formats."
- **`10` §6.1, "the strongest star effects now appear internationally."** Not
  supported by recent data at market level (§6). It is better stated as a claim
  about **pre-sales and financing for genre films**, where it remains trade
  consensus, than about box office, where Hollywood's foreign share has fallen.
- **`12` §3.5, "stars still matter enormously for mid-budget films."** True for
  **financing and streaming**. It is the opposite of what recent theatrical
  results show. Mid-budget star vehicles are where most of the 2024–26 star-led
  flops sit (§3.2).
- **`12` §3.2, "foreign value persists years after domestic value has gone."**
  Softened the same way: still true of pre-sales estimates for genre packages,
  not shown for box office since the late 2010s (§6.2).
- **`12` §3.4, social media following.** Consistent. Note that there is no
  rigorous study either way, only anecdote and industry-commissioned surveys.
