# Design review: why big studios cannot lose, and what the real market does instead

Status: **proposal, for the owner's decision.** Nothing here is built.

Follows `docs/DESIGN_REVIEW_player_economy.md` §13 (the investigation). That
section found the symptoms; this one works out, with the owner, what the real
industry says the game should do instead. Every industry claim cites the
standing reference in `docs/domain/` as `NN §x`. The reference's own caveat
applies: its figures are directional.

---

## 1. The problem, in one paragraph

A careful player studio cannot lose. In ten-year harness runs a Mid-Major grows
£80M to £330-960M and a Major £200M to £500-960M, every time. The player's
films are about 20 quality points better than rivals' at every budget (67-75
against about 50), audiences score them 91-96 against about 65, and good films
then pay through every channel at once: the after-market earns 0.85 per pound
of cinema rentals against the market's 0.38. A Careless studio (median quality
36) goes broke; a careful one (65-75) cannot fail. The economy responds to
quality steeply, a careful player can make 70-quality films every time, and
nothing else in the market stands in the way.

The owner's reading, which the reference supports: **(a) making a good film is
too easy, and (b) a good film making money is too reliable.** Two more things
make it worse: **(c) rivals do not compete like real studios,** and **(d)
studio Brand does the wrong job.**

## 2. What the real market does

### 2.1 What makes money

- **The opening is bought, not earned.** In order: awareness (P&A), pre-sold
  IP, screens and premium formats, genre (horror and event films over-index on
  the opening), star power ("real but weaker than it was"), date and
  competition, and reviews last - "enormously" for adult and prestige films,
  "barely at all" for pre-sold franchise openings (10 §6).
- **Money buys awareness, not interest.** "Definite interest" is "a property
  of the premise", and P&A does not buy legs (10 §6.2, 09 §12.3).
- **Word of mouth multiplies the audience marketing delivered; it does not add
  one** (10 §7.1). It comes from the gap between promise and delivery, and
  "word of mouth is just quality" is listed as a misconception (16 §10). The
  best multiple is "a film that over-delivers on a modest promise" (10 §4.3).
- **Quality is neither necessary nor sufficient.** Critic-proof hits are a real
  category - horror, broad comedy, faith-based, franchises (16 §9). "High
  critics / low audience" is the critical darling that fails; the middle is
  "the dead zone", the worst outcome per pound (16 §9.1). Reviews "do not save a
  film nobody has heard of" (16 §6.4).
- **Review sensitivity depends on the film:** decisive for a platform release
  ("a factor of several on total gross"), 10-25% of a wide adult drama's
  opening, a few percent of a tentpole's opening (but 10-20 points on its
  second-weekend drop), nothing for a horror opening. "Review sensitivity is
  inversely proportional to marketing spend" (16 §6, §6.2, §6.3).
- **Stars are a modest multiplier,** about 1.08-1.13x on the film's other draws,
  with no measurable profit effect once you correct for stars being cast in
  better prospects; draw sits with a small, older top tier (17 §4.1-§4.3,
  12 §3.5). "IP outperforms stars" (12 §3.5).
- **Concept is the campaign.** "A hook that survives compression"; a premise
  that cannot be stated cannot be marketed (09 §12.1, §13.3; 02 §12.10).
- **Cheap, hooky films are the best return on capital.** In the reference's
  worked slate a horror film returned about 1.3x on $45M deployed and a
  franchise sequel about 0.7x on $360M (11 §5.4). Over-spending on a small film
  is the more common error (09 §1.3).

### 2.2 How skewed returns are

- About half of wide releases are profitable on theatrical alone (11 §9).
- In the worked twelve-film slate, "two films produce more contribution than
  the other ten combined; two films destroy nearly as much. Median performance
  is close to zero," and the slate clears about 1.5% on $2.2B. "You cannot
  select the winners in advance": the two biggest losers "were the most
  confidently modelled films on the slate" (11 §5.4).
- Revenues are Pareto-distributed with effectively infinite variance (De Vany &
  Walls, 17 §4.1). "Selection skill in greenlighting is real but weak"; good
  studios are distinguished by failing cheaply, exploiting hits and laying off
  risk (11 §11.3).
- Studios survive on a mixed slate - 2-4 tentpoles, 3-6 mid-budget, 4-8 low
  budget, 1-3 awards plays - judged over 3-5 years, with the library as "the
  only reliably profitable part of a studio" (01 §2.2.7; 11 §5.4, §12).

### 2.3 After the cinema run

- Lifetime post-theatrical is about 40-60% of worldwide rentals (11 §3.1), and
  "every downstream price is indexed to the theatrical result" - pay-1 licences
  run at about 10-20% of domestic box office, floored and capped (11 §3.1).
- Genre changes the mix (prestige drama 35-45% from licensing; animation 20-35%
  from consumer products, 11 §3.4).
- Critical standing matters for the **long tail**: "library valuation follows
  durable reputation, not opening weekend" (16 §11.4).

### 2.4 Why good films are hard

- Studios take 8-20 releases a year from 5,000-20,000 submissions and expect to
  write off 60-90% of development; "script never got there" is the commonest
  death (02 §1.2, §9.2).
- Compromise accumulates: across writers' drafts until "the material lost its
  point" (02 §15); in casting, where "the best person for the job is usually
  working" and casting is "sequencing, not selection" (12 §5.6); in the cut,
  where more than three people with approval "converge toward the average of
  their notes" and test screenings push toward "scores rise, film gets duller"
  (07 §9.6, §13.2, §13.3).
- Post can fix pace and clarity, not "a premise nobody wants, a performance that
  isn't there… chemistry that never existed" (07 §12).
- Even the best miss (*Mickey 17*, 17 §3.2; the second-film problem, 12 §12.4),
  though the reference has no base rate.

### 2.5 Marketing

- A wide release under about $25-35M "simply is not visible"; P&A runs from
  $2-15M domestic for a platform release to $30-60M for a studio mid-budget film
  and $100-200M+ global for a tentpole (09 §1, §1.3; 11 §6.4).
- Ratios to budget are highest on cheap films (horror: $15M negative, $30M P&A,
  11 §5.4); global tentpole marketing often approximates the negative (09 §1).
- **Why it pays on average:** it buys the opening and the screens (exhibitor
  commitments follow tracking, 10 §6.2), and it raises every downstream licence
  price, which is indexed to theatrical (11 §3.1) - theatrical is "an expensive
  advertisement for every window that follows" (09 §11.4).
- **Its limits:** diminishing returns once awareness saturates (10 §6.1); a late
  heavy-up on a dead film is "pure loss" (09 §1.6); mis-selling is worse than a
  small campaign (09 §12.3); a campaign that oversells raises the bar word of
  mouth must clear (16 §8.3).
- **Craft beats spend:** at the same P&A, openings "differ by a factor of two"
  (09 §13).

### 2.6 Studio brand

- The reference never says a studio's name raises audience demand or marketing
  efficiency. Audience-facing draw sits with IP, franchise labels and director
  names (17 §3.3; 09 §4.2). *(General knowledge, not in the reference: a few
  labels do draw - Pixar and Disney Animation, Marvel Studios, A24, Blumhouse;
  corporate names like Universal or Sony do not.)*
- What standing buys is **trade leverage:** booking negotiated across the year
  and judged on "how the studio's last three films performed for them"
  (09 §7.6); the benefit of the doubt on holds (10 §13.4); better rental terms,
  55-60% for tentpoles against 50-55% (01 §8.4); winning date standoffs
  (09 §7.1, §7.5); talent through first-look and overall deals (12 §5.5);
  output deals and promotional partners (11 §3.2, 09 §1.5); an awards record
  that recruits filmmakers (13 §1.4). None of it buys legs.

### 2.7 How studios compete

- Scripts in auctions with 8-20 buyers, preempts and best-and-final rounds;
  "overpaying for heat" makes "the price the project's problem" (02 §2.1, 01 §2.2).
- Talent through holds and availability (12 §5.6).
- **Dates are the scarce resource** - 30-40 viable weekends a year, staked
  24-36 months out; studios play chicken, smaller titles move, and
  counter-programming is "the most reliable dating strategy for a non-tentpole"
  (01 §2.2.7, §10; 09 §7.1, §7.5).
- **Distinct identities:** the franchise major, the specialty awards label
  (A24, Neon, Focus, Searchlight), the library-and-franchise studio
  (Lionsgate), the low-budget horror shop (Blumhouse, about $5-15M a film)
  (01 §2; 03 §11; 13 §1.4).
- **They adapt:** a failed comparable reprices a whole category overnight; a
  strategic shift abandons a class of film; a new head of production clears
  the slate within 6-12 months (02 §9.2, 01 §2.2.6).

## 3. What the game does today, against that

| | Real market | The game today |
|---|---|---|
| Script quality before buying | A judgement; readers disagree; the page is not the film | Exact stat bars for dialogue, characters, structure, originality (`components/common/ScriptDetails.tsx`) |
| Getting the best people | "Usually working"; casting is sequencing | Usually free; the player picks the best they can read |
| Compromise | Notes, rewrites, availability, cut by committee | Little; a careful player's films land at quality 53-79 (10th-90th percentile) |
| The opening | Awareness, IP, screens, genre, stars, date | Broadly right: marketing reach, cast fame, hook, accessibility, genre audience (`engine/audienceSimulationInputs.ts`) - but marketing reach is multiplied by Brand |
| Word of mouth | Delivery against the promise; reviews weighted by film type | Reception score, 70% audience and 30% critic, and audience score follows quality one-for-one above a reference of 51 (`engine/scoring.ts:computeAudienceScore`) |
| Quality's effect on money | Neither necessary nor sufficient | Steep and reliable: Careless 36 goes broke, Careful 65-75 cannot lose |
| After-market | Indexed to box office; reputation adds library value | Concave in gross, then multiplied by audience score, critic score, awards and studio prestige - the player's licensing multiplier 2.5, the market's 0.97 (`engine/ancillary.ts`) |
| Studio brand | Trade leverage: holds, terms, dates, talent, deals | Marketing efficiency, 0.3 at Brand 0 to 1.0 at Brand 100, plus release strength and buzz (`engine/audienceSimulationInputs.ts:computeMarketingEfficiency`) |
| Rival strategy | Distinct archetypes that adapt | Scripts scored by craft, originality and price with weights set only by size (Indie, Mid-Size, Major) and a genre lean, then a random pick among the close ones; spend rolled at random within a range; people hired on track record near a price (`engine/rivalStudios.ts`, `engine/rivalCasting.ts`). Every rival of a size behaves alike and none learns |
| Returns | Skewed; median near zero; winners unpredictable | A careful player's films return a median of +30% to +90% all-in, 20-34% losing |

What the game gets right, and should keep: openings are built from the right
ingredients; marketing has a floor and a release-type cost; rivals' small
films spend about twice their negative on marketing, which matches the
reference (11 §5.4 - the earlier investigation called that overspending; it is
normal); critic-proof genres already exist in the reception model.

## 4. Proposal

Four pieces. Each changes what the box-office gates measure, so this is a
recalibration project, not a tweak, and the gates
(`docs/DESIGN_box_office_calibration_targets.md`) are re-run after each.

### 4.1 What makes money

1. **Legs come from delivery against the promise, not from quality.** The
   campaign sets an expectation - from marketing intensity, the hook, the
   stars, IP - and word of mouth follows how far the film beats or misses it.
   A modest film that over-delivers gets the best multiple; a heavily sold film
   that is merely good gets a short run. This replaces reception-as-quality as
   the main driver of legs.
2. **Reviews weighted by film type,** inversely to marketing spend: decisive for
   a platform release, meaningful for wide adult films, small for tentpole
   openings (but felt in the second weekend), none for horror openings.
3. **Audience score loosens from quality.** Audiences reward fulfilment of the
   genre and the promise (the existing fulfilment term) as much as craft; a
   critic-proof film can score well with audiences while critics pan it.
4. **After-market indexed to box office,** with genre setting the mix and a
   single, bounded library premium for durable reputation (critical standing,
   awards) instead of four multiplied quality readings.
5. **Returns made genuinely skewed:** keep the existing variance gate, and add a
   player-side target - a careful studio's median film near break-even all-in,
   with a few hits carrying the slate (§2.2).

### 4.2 Brand becomes trade leverage

Brand stops multiplying marketing reach. Instead a studio's standing - built
from its recent record with exhibitors, not from audiences - buys:

- **screens and holds:** more screens at opening for the same tracking, and
  the benefit of the doubt for a week when a film softens;
- **rental terms:** a better share for a studio with a strong record;
- **dates:** winning a standoff, or being able to claim a date early;
- **talent:** a better hearing from people deciding between offers (stage 8's
  decision already has a studio term to plug into);
- optionally, **an audience-facing label** for the few studios that earn one
  (a family-animation or horror label known for a kind of film), tied to the
  studio's genre identity rather than to Brand.

### 4.3 Rivals as profiles that adapt

Each rival gets a profile: weights on concept and hook, pre-sold IP, stars,
budget discipline, genre, prestige and awards, marketing appetite, risk
appetite, and a release habit (wide, platform, festival). Archetypes from the
reference seed them - franchise major, specialty awards label, low-budget
horror shop, library-and-franchise studio, star-vehicle studio - with variation
within each.

They then **adapt**, not necessarily wisely: a hit in a genre raises its weight
(chasing the last success), a failed comparable reprices a genre, a run of
losses brings a regime change that redraws the profile. They **compete for the
same inputs** - bidding by how much their own profile values a script, booking
the people their profile wants, staking dates and moving off them when a bigger
film arrives - so the player meets competition for the best scripts, people
and weekends.

The harness bot uses the same profile machinery, so player strategies and rival
strategies are the same kind of thing.

### 4.4 Good films become hard to make

- **Scripts are read, not measured:** coverage gives a read with its own
  uncertainty, readers can disagree, and how the page plays is only known when
  it is shot (the same evidence model as talent, stage 4).
- **Compromise:** notes from the studio and financiers, a director's demands
  (which already exist), rewrites that can lose the point, and the best people
  being booked - availability as a real constraint, which stage 8 started.
- **Execution:** a wider spread on the shoot and in the cut, with a
  cut-by-committee pull toward the middle.

## 5. Order, and how we will know

| Step | Why this order | Judged by |
|---|---|---|
| 1. What makes money (4.1) and Brand as trade leverage (4.2), together | They decide what winning means and how the market responds; both move the same gates | Box-office gates hold or improve; big studios can lose (some ten-year runs shrink); a careful studio's median film near break-even all-in, with a skewed tail |
| 2. Rival profiles (4.3) | Needs the money model settled, so profiles have the right things to value | Rival quality and strategy vary by studio; rivals bid against the player for top scripts and people; the rival field stops polarising |
| 3. Hard to make good films (4.4) | Affects the player most; easiest to judge once the market is right | A careful player's film quality spreads wider, with real misses; Careless still fails |

Acceptance, overall: no strategy at any tier is safe for ten years; Careful
does better than Careless on average but loses some careers; Indie stays hard;
Boutique does not run away; the rival field keeps a spread of healthy and
struggling studios.

## 6. Open questions for the owner

1. **Audience-facing labels.** Should a studio be able to earn a label that
   audiences follow (Pixar, A24, Blumhouse-style), tied to a kind of film, or
   should audiences ignore studio names entirely?
2. **How lopsided.** The reference's worked slate clears about 1.5% on a year's
   spend. Should a well-run studio in the game do roughly that - thin, volatile
   margins, carried by a few hits and the library - or somewhat better, for
   playability?
3. **Script reads.** Replacing exact script stats with uncertain coverage
   changes the development screens. Is that the direction you want, or keep the
   stats and make the page-to-screen step uncertain instead?
4. **Scope of step 1.** It touches the audience simulation, reception and the
   after-market at once. Acceptable as one step, or split legs first and
   after-market second?
